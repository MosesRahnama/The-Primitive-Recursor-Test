Based on an analysis of the given first-order term rewriting system (TRS):

$$\begin{aligned}
F(x, y, Z) &\rightarrow x \\
F(x, y, S(n)) &\rightarrow G(y, F(x, y, n))
\end{aligned}$$

Here is the evaluation of each proposed proof method.

---

### **(A) Lexicographic Path Order (LPO) with precedence $F > G > S > Z$**

1. **Does it succeed?** **Yes.** 
   * **Rule 1:** $F(x, y, Z) >_{lpo} x$ holds because $x$ is a strict subterm of the left-hand side (LHS).
   * **Rule 2:** We compare $s = F(x, y, S(n))$ and $t = G(y, F(x, y, n))$. Since $F > G$, we must show $s >_{lpo} y$ (which holds by subterm) and $s >_{lpo} F(x, y, n)$. Comparing the latter two under LPO (with a standard left-to-right status for $F$): the first two arguments match ($x=x, y=y$), and the third argument strictly decreases ($S(n) >_{lpo} n$ by subterm). Thus, $s >_{lpo} t$.
2. **Does it stay within the benchmark boundary?** **Yes.** LPO is a purely syntactic ordering on the term algebra of the TRS and does not import any external mathematical theories or algebraic assumptions.

---

### **(B) Nonlinear polynomial interpretation over $\mathbb{N}$**

1. **Does it succeed?** **Yes.** 
   * The interpretation is well-defined and strictly monotonic in all arguments for the carrier set $\mathbb{N}$ (since $x, y, n \ge 0$, and the coefficients ensure strict growth).
   * **Rule 1:** $[F(x, y, Z)] = x + y + 1 > x = [x]$, which holds since $y + 1 > 0$.
   * **Rule 2:** $[F(x, y, S(n))] = x + (y+1)(n+2) = x + (y+1)(n+1) + y + 1$. 
     $[G(y, F(x, y, n))] = y + x + (y+1)(n+1)$.
     Comparing the two yields $y+1 > y$, which simplifies to $1 > 0$ (strictly holds).
2. **Does it stay within the benchmark boundary?** **No.** Semantic interpretations over $\mathbb{N}$ require importing the structural axioms of arithmetic (addition, multiplication, and ordering over natural numbers) to justify the comparisons, which goes beyond the rules of the rewrite system itself.

---

### **(C) Multiset Path Order (MPO) with precedence $F > G > S > Z$**

1. **Does it succeed?** **Yes.**
   * **Rule 1:** $F(x, y, Z) >_{mpo} x$ holds by the subterm property.
   * **Rule 2:** To show $F(x, y, S(n)) >_{mpo} G(y, F(x, y, n))$, since $F > G$, we need $F(x, y, S(n)) >_{mpo} y$ (true by subterm) and $F(x, y, S(n)) >_{mpo} F(x, y, n)$. Comparing the arguments of the two $F$ terms as multisets: $\{x, y, S(n)\} >_{mpo}^{mul} \{x, y, n\}$. Removing the common elements $\{x, y\}$ leaves $\{S(n)\} >_{mpo}^{mul} \{n\}$, which holds because $S(n) >_{mpo} n$.
2. **Does it stay within the benchmark boundary?** **Yes.** Like LPO, MPO is a purely syntactic, self-contained method operating on the free term algebra of the system.

---

### **(D) Dependency pairs with subterm criterion, projecting to the third argument of $F$**

1. **Does it succeed?** **Yes.**
   * $F$ is the only defined symbol in the system. The only dependency pair is:
     $$F^\sharp(x, y, S(n)) \rightarrow F^\sharp(x, y, n)$$
   * Using the subterm criterion with the projection $\pi(F^\sharp) = 3$:
     $$\pi(F^\sharp(x, y, S(n))) = S(n) \quad \rhd \quad n = \pi(F^\sharp(x, y, n))$$
   * Since $S(n) \rhd n$ is a strict subterm relation, the dependency pair is strictly decreasing, proving global termination.
2. **Does it stay within the benchmark boundary?** **Yes.** The dependency pair framework combined with the subterm criterion is a purely syntactic method based entirely on the structural subterm relation of the TRS without any external assumptions.

---

### **(E) Exponential interpretation over $\mathbb{N}$**

1. **Does it succeed?** **Yes.**
   * The interpretation is strictly monotonic because the base $x+y+2 \ge 2$.
   * **Rule 1:** $x + y + 2 > x$, which holds.
   * **Rule 2:** $(x+y+2)^{n+2} > y + (x+y+2)^{n+1}$. Letting $A = x+y+2 \ge 2$, this becomes $A \cdot A^{n+1} > y + A^{n+1}$. Since $A \ge y + 2$, we have $A \cdot A^{n+1} \ge (y+2)A^{n+1} = y A^{n+1} + 2 A^{n+1} > y + A^{n+1}$, which strictly holds for all $x,y,n \in \mathbb{N}$.
2. **Does it stay within the benchmark boundary?** **No.** Like the polynomial interpretation, this relies on importing the theory of natural numbers and exponentiation.
