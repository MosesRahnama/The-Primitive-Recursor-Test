**Yes** — strong normalization can be established from the two rules alone.

**Reasoning.**  
The only redexes are the \(F\)-headed terms. The two left-hand sides, \(F(x,y,Z)\) and \(F(x,y,S(n))\), do not unify, so there are no critical overlaps. Define a numeric weight on any term by summing, over every \(F\)-redex it contains, the number of \(S\)-symbols in that redex’s third argument.

- **Rule 1** (\(F(x,y,Z) \to x\)) removes an \(F\)-redex whose third argument contains \(0\) occurrences of \(S\), strictly decreasing the total weight.
- **Rule 2** (\(F(x,y,S(n)) \to G(F(x,y,n))\)) replaces one \(F\)-redex whose third argument contains \(k+1\) occurrences of \(S\) by a single new \(F\)-redex (inside the inert context \(G\)) whose third argument contains only \(k\) occurrences of \(S\). No other \(F\)-redexes are created or altered. Hence the weight again drops by at least one.

Because \(G\) has no rewrite rules, it cannot generate new redexes or hide the decrease. The weight is a natural number and is strictly lowered by every rewrite step, so no infinite reduction sequence exists. This argument uses only the structural information present in the given rules.
