**Assessment:** Yes, strong normalization can be rigorously established from the presented rewrite system alone.

**Reasoning:**  
The system consists of two rules:  
1. `F(x, y, Z) → x`  
2. `F(x, y, S(n)) → G(F(x, y, n))`  

Termination (equivalent to strong normalization for finitely branching TRS) is provable *syntactically* using a **lexicographic path order (LPO)** with:  
- Precedence: `F > G`, `F > S` (Z requires no special handling)  
- Lexicographic status for `F`  

**Orientation proof:**  
- **Rule 1:** RHS `x` is a *proper subterm* of LHS `F(x, y, Z)`. By the subterm property of simplification orders (inherent to LPO), LHS > RHS holds *without* semantic assumptions.  
- **Rule 2:**  
  - LHS = `F(x, y, S(n))`, RHS = `G(F(x, y, n))`  
  - Since `F > G`, it suffices to show `F(x, y, S(n)) > F(x, y, n)`.  
  - Arguments compared lexicographically: first two arguments (`x`, `y`) are identical; third argument `S(n) > n` holds *by subterm property* (as `n` is a direct subterm of `S(n)`).  
  - Thus LHS > RHS.  

LPO is a well-founded, monotonic, stable reduction order whose validity depends *only* on:  
- Symbol arities (explicit in the rules)  
- Syntactic structure of rules (subterm relations, argument positions)  
- Standard meta-theoretic properties of reduction orders (no object-level axioms)  

Critically:  
- No assumptions about `S`/`Z` semantics (e.g., "S denotes successor," "infinite numerals exist") are imported. The subterm-based comparison `S(n) > n` is purely syntactic.  
- Rule 1 strictly decreases total `F`-symbol count; Rule 2 preserves it but reduces structural complexity in the third argument. Any attempt to construct non-termination (e.g., regenerating `S` via inner reductions) consumes `F`-symbols via Rule 1, bounding such cycles.  
- All reduction paths terminate: finite terms cannot sustain infinite Rule 2 applications without Rule 1 interventions, and Rule 1 applications are globally bounded by initial `F`-symbol count.  

This proof uses *only* the rewrite rules and foundational term rewriting theory (reduction orders), with no external structural axioms or semantic interpretations of symbols. Strong normalization is therefore derivable solely from the presented system.
