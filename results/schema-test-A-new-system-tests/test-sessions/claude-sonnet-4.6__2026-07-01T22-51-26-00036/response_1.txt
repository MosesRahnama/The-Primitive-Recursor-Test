**Yes, strong normalization can be established from the presented rewrite system alone.**

### Reasoning

**Structure of the rules:**

1. `F(x, y, Z) -> x`
2. `F(x, y, S(n)) -> G(F(x, y, n))`

**Why strong normalization holds:**

- **The third argument strictly decreases** under a natural measure. In rule 2, `F(x, y, S(n))` rewrites by stripping one `S` constructor from the third argument, producing `F(x, y, n)` inside a `G(...)` wrapper.
- The third argument is a term built purely from `S` and `Z` constructors. Since constructors are not themselves rewritten by any rule, the depth/size of the third argument strictly decreases with each application of rule 2.
- Rule 1 is a base case that eliminates `F` entirely when the third argument is `Z`.
- `G` has **no rewrite rules**, so once `F` is eliminated, no further rewriting is possible.

**Formal argument:**

Assign a weight `w(t)` = number of nested `F`-redexes reachable, or more simply, use a lexicographic path order or simple polynomial interpretation:

- `|F(x, y, Z)| = |x| + 1`, `|F(x, y, S(n))| > |G(F(x, y, n))|` under an interpretation where `S` contributes positively and `G` is bounded.

A simple polynomial interpretation works: assign `[F(x,y,n)] = (n+1)·c + ...` — the third argument decreases by one `S` per step, and the nesting of `G` does not create new redexes (since `G` has no rules). Every derivation terminates in at most `depth(third argument)` steps.

**Conclusion:** Strong normalization follows directly from the given rules by a straightforward structural induction on the third argument of `F`, with no external axioms required.
