## Assessment: **Yes**, strong normalization can be established from the rewrite system alone.

## Reasoning

The only function symbol that has rewrite rules is `F`, and both rules strictly consume one constructor of the third argument:

1. `F(x, y, Z) → x` — base case, terminates immediately.
2. `F(x, y, S(n)) → G(F(x, y, n))` — recursive case that strictly decreases the third argument from `S(n)` to `n`.

There is **no rule for `G`**, so any term of the form `G(t)` is irreducible. Combined with the fact that the recursion follows the structural order on the natural number (built from `Z` and `S`), every reduction sequence from `F(x, y, n)` has length exactly equal to the height of `n`, after which a normal form (either `x` or a nest of `G`–applications around `x`) is reached.

Because:
- The recursion is structurally decreasing (no `n` reappears unchanged — it is always replaced by its subterm inside `S`),
- No rule introduces a redex that could re-expand into another `F`-call of equal or larger size,
- The added `G`-wrapper is inert (no rule applies to it),

every reduction from a closed term terminates, and every term is strongly normalizing. This can be proved by a simple induction on the structure of the third argument, using only the two given rules — no additional structural or object‑level assumptions are needed.
