## Assessment

**Yes, strong normalization can be established from the presented rewrite system alone.**

### Reasoning

**Structure of the system:**

The two rules are:
1. `F(x, y, Z) → x`
2. `F(x, y, S(n)) → G(F(x, y, n))`

**Why it strongly normalizes:**

1. **The third argument strictly decreases.** In rule 2, `F(x, y, S(n))` rewrites by peeling off one `S` constructor from the third argument, producing `F(x, y, n)`. The third argument goes from `S(n)` to `n` — a strict structural decrease.

2. **`G` has no rewrite rules.** `G` is inert; it introduces no new redexes. It simply wraps the result.

3. **`x` and `y` are passed through unchanged** (they appear identically on both sides or are simply returned).

4. **A simple termination measure works:** Use the **size (depth) of the third argument** as a lexicographic or polynomial measure. Every application of rule 2 reduces the third argument by one `S`, and rule 1 eliminates the `F` entirely. Since the third argument is a finite term built from `S` and `Z`, this measure is a natural number that strictly decreases at each step.

5. **No rule for `G` means no reduction can occur under a `G`** beyond whatever is produced by the `F` call — so there is no possibility of divergence introduced by `G`.

**Conclusion:** The system is **primitive recursive in structure** (essentially computing a fixed number of `G`-wrappings determined by the numeral in the third argument), and strong normalization follows directly from the strict structural descent on the third argument — no external axioms or assumptions are needed.
