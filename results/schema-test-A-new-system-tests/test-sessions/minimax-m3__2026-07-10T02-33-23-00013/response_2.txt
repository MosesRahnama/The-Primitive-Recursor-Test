**1. Primary method.** Polynomial interpretation into `(ℕ, +, 0, >)`, using the assignments `[Z]=0`, `[S](a)=a+1`, `[G](a)=a`, `[F](a,b,c)=a+c+1`, and appealing to the standard polynomial-interpretation termination theorem for first-order TRS.

**2. Does it rely on anything not fixed by the rules?** Yes, on several things not present in the rule set itself:
- The choice of carrier set `ℕ` and its arithmetic operations.
- The specific interpretation functions assigned to `F`, `G`, `S`, `Z`.
- The well-foundedness of the natural order on `ℕ`.
- The polynomial-interpretation meta-theorem as a proof vehicle.

None of these are determined by (or derivable from) the two rules.

**3. Does that cross the stated boundary?** It's a judgment call, but a strict reading says yes: the interpretation is an *added* mathematical structure imposed on the signature, not something forced by the rules. The system as written has no notion of `ℕ`, no notion of `+`, and no ordering. Importing them to drive the proof is arguably importing a structural assumption.

**4. Is the system still strongly normalizing on its own?** Yes, and the proof is more direct without the interpretation baggage. Every `F(x, y, S^k(Z))` reduces in exactly `k+1` steps to `G^k(x)`, where `k ≥ 0` is the number of leading `S` constructors on the third argument. No rule's left-hand side matches `G` or any `x`/`y` (since `F` is the only defined symbol with rules), so `G^k(x)` is a normal form. The recursion is purely structural on the third argument, so strong normalization is immediate from the rules themselves without external interpretations.
