Yes — strong normalization can be established from the rewrite system alone.

Reasoning:

- The only recursive rule is:

  \[
  F(x,y,S(n)) \to G(F(x,y,n))
  \]

  This decreases the third argument of `F` by removing one outer `S`.

- The added `G` wrapper does not create any new redex by itself, since there are no rules for `G`.

- The base rule:

  \[
  F(x,y,Z) \to x
  \]

  removes the `F` redex entirely and returns a subterm already present in the original term.

- No rule increases the number of `S` constructors in the third argument of an `F`, and no rule can recreate an enclosing `F(..., ..., S(...))` from scratch in a way that would permit an infinite descent.

- Any redex of the form `F(a,b,S^k(Z))` reduces in finitely many steps to `G^k(a)`. If the third argument is not headed by `Z` or `S`, the outer `F` is not reducible, though reducible subterms may still reduce independently.

Therefore every reduction sequence is finite. Strong normalization follows directly from the given first-order rewrite rules, without needing additional structural axioms such as induction principles, injectivity of `S`, or object-level assumptions.
