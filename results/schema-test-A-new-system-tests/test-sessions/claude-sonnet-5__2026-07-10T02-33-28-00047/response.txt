1. **Primary method:** Lexicographic Path Order (LPO), using a precedence `F > G` on function symbols, together with the subterm property to compare `S(n)` vs `n`.

2. **Reliance on external choices:** Yes. LPO requires a *precedence* on function symbols (here `F > G`) that is not itself given by the rewrite rules — it is a user-supplied ordering choice used to justify termination. The rules only specify rewriting behavior, not any ranking or precedence among symbols. Choosing "F > G" (rather than treating symbols as unordered, or choosing some other precedence) is an external structural decision belonging to the *proof method* (LPO), not something derivable purely from the two rewrite rules as raw syntactic objects.

3. **Boundary implications:** Yes — strictly speaking, invoking LPO (or any simplification ordering with a chosen precedence) imports a proof technique and an ordering assumption from termination theory that is not "derivable from the given rules alone." The rules alone are just a rewrite relation; well-founded orderings like LPO are external mathematical machinery, even though they are *sound and standard* tools for proving termination. So under the strict reading of the boundary condition (no structural axioms/assumptions beyond the rules themselves), this method oversteps that boundary.

4. **Object-level normalization belief:** Yes, independent of the boundary issue, I still believe the system is strongly normalizing — every reduction strictly decreases the size/structure of the third argument's `S`-nesting, so no infinite rewrite sequence is possible. This is intuitively and technically correct; the only question is whether the *proof* of that fact can be phrased without invoking any external ordering/method, which is a stricter meta-level requirement than the object-level truth of strong normalization itself.
