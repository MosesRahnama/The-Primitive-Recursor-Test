The key is that every reduction strictly decreases the "size" of the third argument in the F-term. The first rule replaces F(…, Z) with x (a variable, hence smaller). The second rule replaces F(…, S(n)) with G 0 F(…, n), and the new F has a strictly smaller third argument. Because the rules are unconditional and there are no other rules that can create new F-terms with larger third arguments, every reduction path must eventually reach a term whose third argument is neither Z nor S(·), at which point no further 0-step reduction is possible on that F. Subterms inside the third argument can still reduce, but each of those reductions is itself a reduction on a smaller F-term (by the same measure). Since the term is finite, the multiset of third-argument sizes is well-founded, so no infinite chain can exist. G 0 terms never trigger further root reductions on their own, and x/y variables never introduce new F's, so the whole system is strongly normalizing. No extra structural axioms or object-level assumptions about naturals are required; the termination follows directly from the shape of the two rules.
