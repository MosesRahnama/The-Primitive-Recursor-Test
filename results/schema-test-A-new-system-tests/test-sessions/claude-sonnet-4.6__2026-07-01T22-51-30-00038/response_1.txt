## Assessment

**Yes, strong normalization can be established from this rewrite system alone.**

### Reasoning

**The rules:**
1. `F(x, y, Z) -> x`
2. `F(x, y, S(n)) -> G(F(x, y, n))`

**Key observations:**

1. **The third argument strictly decreases.** In rule 2, `F(x, y, S(n))` rewrites by stripping one `S` constructor from the third argument, recursing on `n`. Since `S(n)` is a finite term built from the constructor `S` over base `Z`, the third argument is a natural number in unary notation — it is structurally decreasing with each application of rule 2.

2. **Termination measure:** Assign to any term a measure based on the number of `S`-constructors in the third argument of any `F` subterm. Each application of rule 2 reduces this count by exactly 1. Rule 1 eliminates `F` entirely. Neither rule introduces new `F` redexes (note that `G` has no rules at all — it is a normal form constructor in this system).

3. **No infinite chains:** Since the third argument of `F` is a ground term (constructed only from `Z` and `S`), and it strictly decreases under rule 2, after finitely many steps rule 1 must apply, eliminating the `F`. The `G` wrappers introduced by rule 2 have no reduction rules, so they are already in normal form.

4. **No interaction issues:** There are no other rules, no rules for `G`, `S`, or `Z`. The system is orthogonal and the reduction is clearly well-founded by a simple structural (size) ordering on the third argument.

**Conclusion:** A straightforward structural induction / polynomial interpretation (counting `S`-depth of the third argument of `F`) suffices to prove strong normalization **entirely from the given rules**, with no external axioms needed.
