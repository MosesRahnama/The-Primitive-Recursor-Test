### A. LPO

With \(F > G > S > Z\):

- \(F(x,y,Z) >_{\mathrm{LPO}} x\) by the subterm property.
- For the second rule,
  \[
  F(x,y,S(n)) >_{\mathrm{LPO}} F(x,y,n)
  \]
  because \(S(n)>_{\mathrm{LPO}}n\), and the left side also dominates \(y\). Since \(F>G\), this gives
  \[
  F(x,y,S(n)) >_{\mathrm{LPO}} G(y,F(x,y,n)).
  \]

Thus both rules are oriented strictly. This is a purely syntactic termination proof from the given TRS.

### B. Polynomial interpretation

At the roots, the inequalities do hold:

\[
[F(x,y,Z)]=x+1>x,
\]

and

\[
[F(x,y,S(n))]=x+n+2>x+n+1
 =[G(y,F(x,y,n))].
\]

However, the interpretation is not strictly monotone in every argument: \(F\) ignores its second argument, and \(G\) ignores its first. Consequently, a rewrite inside one of those argument positions need not decrease the interpretation of the whole term. For example,

\[
G(F(Z,Z,Z),Z)\to G(Z,Z)
\]

has interpretation \(0\) on both sides. Hence this is not, as stated, a valid polynomial reduction order proving termination under arbitrary contexts.

It nevertheless uses no object-level assumptions beyond the TRS, so it remains within the benchmark boundary as an attempted method.

### C. KBO

The second rule violates KBO’s variable condition: \(y\) occurs once on the left but twice on the right. Moreover, with standard positive variable weight, its right-hand side also has greater total weight than its left-hand side. Thus the rule cannot be oriented by the stated KBO.

This is still a syntactic attempt based only on the TRS.

### D. Dependency pairs

The only dependency pair is

\[
F^\#(x,y,S(n))\to F^\#(x,y,n).
\]

Projecting to the third argument gives

\[
S(n)\mathrel{\triangleright}n,
\]

a strict proper-subterm decrease. Thus the subterm criterion establishes chain-freeness and termination. No datatype or sortedness assumption about \(n\) is needed.

### E. Direct measure

The proposed measure does decrease for root applications of the two rules, but not for every rewrite step in an arbitrary context. It ignores, for example, the first argument of \(G\) and the first two arguments of \(F\). Thus

\[
G(F(Z,Z,Z),Z)\to G(Z,Z)
\]

does not strictly decrease \(\mu\). Therefore the stated global-descent argument fails.

Under the literal, permissible reading that \(\operatorname{depth}_S\) is a syntactic function defined on all terms, the attempt stays within the boundary. If instead it assumes that the third argument is a Peano-natural term built only from \(S\) and \(Z\), that would import an unprovided sorting/datatype assumption and would violate the boundary.
