Here is the evaluation of each proposed proof method based on whether it successfully proves termination of the system and whether it stays within the designated benchmark boundary:

### **(A) Lexicographic Path Order (LPO) with precedence F > G > S > Z**
1. **Does it succeed?** **Yes.** 
   - For the first rule, $F(x, y, Z) \to x$, termination holds because $x$ is an immediate subterm of the LHS. 
   - For the second rule, $F(x, y, S(n)) \to G(y, F(x, y, n))$, since $F > G$, LPO requires the LHS to be strictly greater than all arguments of the RHS. 
     - $F(x, y, S(n)) >_{lpo} y$ holds because $y$ is a subterm of the LHS.
     - $F(x, y, S(n)) >_{lpo} F(x, y, n)$ holds by comparing lexicographically (the first two arguments are identical, and $S(n) >_{lpo} n$ by the subterm property) and verifying that the LHS is strictly greater than all arguments of the RHS ($x, y,$ and $n$ are all subterms of the LHS).
2. **Is it within the boundary?** **Yes.** LPO is a standard rewriting metatheorem that establishes strong normalization strictly from the syntactic rules themselves. No external object-level assumptions are imported.

### **(B) Polynomial interpretation over N**
1. **Does it succeed?** **No.** 
   For a standard polynomial interpretation over $\mathbb{N}$ to prove termination directly, the interpretation must be *strictly monotonic* in all arguments to guarantee closure under contexts (i.e., modifying a subterm strictly decreases the measure of the whole term). The proposed interpretation for $G$ is $[G(a,b)] = b$. This interpretation ignores its first argument $a$ and is therefore not strictly monotonic with respect to it. Because of this, a rewrite step occurring inside the first argument of $G$ would not yield a strict decrease in the evaluation of the whole term. 
2. **Is it within the boundary?** **Yes** (in terms of methodology), but it fundamentally fails the mathematical requirements of the technique.

### **(C) Knuth-Bendix Order (KBO) with uniform symbol weights**
1. **Does it succeed?** **No.**
   A fundamental requirement for applying KBO is the variable condition: for every rule $L \to R$, no variable can occur more times in $R$ than it does in $L$. In the second rule, $F(x, y, S(n)) \to G(y, F(x, y, n))$, the variable $y$ appears exactly once on the LHS, but **twice** on the RHS (once as the first argument of $G$, and once as the second argument of $F$). Thus, KBO cannot be applied to this system.
2. **Is it within the boundary?** **Yes** (methodologically), but the proof method fails technically. 

### **(D) Dependency pairs with subterm criterion, projecting to the third argument of F**
1. **Does it succeed?** **Yes.**
   The only defined symbol is $F$. Looking at the RHS of the rules, there is only one subterm headed by a defined symbol, which gives exactly one dependency pair: 
   $F^\#(x, y, S(n)) \to F^\#(x, y, n)$.
   Applying the subterm criterion by projecting to the third argument ($\pi_3$) yields $\pi_3(LHS) = S(n)$ and $\pi_3(RHS) = n$. Because $S(n)$ strictly contains $n$ as a proper subterm ($S(n) \rhd n$), the dependency pair is strictly oriented. This breaks any potential infinite chains and proves termination. 
2. **Is it within the boundary?** **Yes.** The dependency pair framework and the subterm criterion are standard metatheoretic tools for establishing strong normalization in TRSs and do not import outside structural/object-level assumptions.

### **(E) Direct descent measure on the third argument**
1. **Does it succeed?** **No.**
   The proposal incorrectly argues that *each rewrite step* strictly decreases $\mu$. A valid termination measure must be closed under contexts. If a rewrite step occurs deep inside a term—for instance, inside the first or second argument of $F$, such as $F(F(x,y,Z), y, n) \to F(x, y, n)$—the measure $\mu$ evaluates to $depth\_S(n) + 1$ for both the LHS and the RHS. Because the measure does not strictly decrease for this rewrite step, it fails to be a valid reduction order. 
2. **Is it within the boundary?** The method attempts to stay within the boundary, but relies on a mathematically invalid argument.

### **Conclusion**
The methods that successfully prove termination and satisfy both conditions (succeeding as stated and staying within the original benchmark boundary) are **(A)** and **(D)**.
