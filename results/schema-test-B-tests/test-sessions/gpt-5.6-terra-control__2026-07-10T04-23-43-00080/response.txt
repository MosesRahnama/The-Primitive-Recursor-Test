| Method | Proves termination? | Within benchmark boundary? | Reason |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| (A) LPO, \(F>G>S>Z\) | Yes | Yes | The rules are strictly oriented by LPO. |
| (B) Polynomial interpretation | No | Yes | It orients the displayed rules at the root, but is not strictly monotone in all arguments, so it does not decrease under arbitrary contexts. |
| (C) KBO, all weights 1 | No | Yes | The second rule violates KBO’s variable-occurrence condition: \(y\) occurs once on the left and twice on the right. |
| (D) Dependency pairs + third-argument subterm criterion | Yes | Yes | The sole recursive dependency pair strictly decreases from \(S(n)\) to \(n\) in the third argument. |
| (E) Direct \(\mu\)-measure as stated | No | Yes | \(\mu\) ignores some arguments and is not decreasing for arbitrary instantiated rules or context steps. |

Thus, the methods satisfying both conditions are **(A)** and **(D)**.

### (A) LPO

For the first rule,

\[
F(x,y,Z) \to x,
\]

the left-hand side is greater than \(x\) by the subterm property.

For the second rule,

\[
F(x,y,S(n)) \to G(y,F(x,y,n)),
\]

the root comparison gives \(F(\ldots) > G(\ldots)\), since \(F>G\). LPO then requires the left-hand side to exceed both arguments of the right-hand \(G\):

- \(F(x,y,S(n)) > y\), by subterm;
- \(F(x,y,S(n)) > F(x,y,n)\), because the first two arguments agree and \(S(n)>n\).

Hence both rules are strictly LPO-decreasing.

The precedence is a metatheoretic termination certificate, not an added rewrite rule, equation, or object-level assumption. Moreover, the essential comparison \(F>G\) is exactly the comparison needed by the second rule; the extra stated comparisons \(G>S>Z\) are not materially used.

### (B) Polynomial interpretation

At the roots, the interpretation gives:

\[
[F(x,y,Z)] = x+1 > x = [x],
\]

and

\[
[F(x,y,S(n))]=x+n+2 > x+n+1
= [G(y,F(x,y,n))].
\]

However, the interpretation is not strictly monotone in every argument:

\[
[G(a,b)] = b
\]

ignores its first argument, and

\[
[F(x,y,n)] = x+n+1
\]

ignores its second argument.

Therefore a rewrite inside such an ignored argument need not decrease the interpretation. For example, if a redex occurs in the first argument of \(G\), then rewriting it leaves the displayed numerical value of the whole \(G\)-term unchanged. Consequently this is not a globally decreasing interpretation for arbitrary rewrite steps.

This is a failure of the proposed proof, not a boundary violation.

### (C) KBO

The second rule cannot be oriented by KBO, independently of precedence or the equal weights, because of the variable condition:

\[
F(x,y,S(n)) \to G(y,F(x,y,n)).
\]

The variable \(y\) occurs once on the left but twice on the right. KBO requires every variable to occur at least as often on the left as on the right for a strict orientation from left to right. Thus KBO cannot orient this rule.

Again, the use of KBO weights is a proof-order proposal, not an imported object-level assumption; it simply does not work here.

### (D) Dependency pairs and third-argument projection

The relevant dependency pair is

\[
F^\#(x,y,S(n)) \to F^\#(x,y,n).
\]

Projecting to the third argument yields

\[
S(n) \triangleright n,
\]

a strict subterm decrease. Thus the subterm criterion eliminates the recursive dependency pair, proving that there is no infinite dependency-pair chain. The dependency-pair framework therefore proves termination.

The chosen projection is based directly on the recursive call structure displayed by the rule and introduces no additional rewrite-theoretic assumption.

### (E) Direct \(\mu\)-measure

The asserted claim that every rewrite step strictly decreases \(\mu\) is false.

Most directly, consider an instance of the first rule:

\[
F(s,y,Z) \to s.
\]

The proposed measure gives

\[
\mu(F(s,y,Z))=1,
\]

because the third argument is \(Z\). But \(s\) may itself be an arbitrarily large term, for example

\[
s = F(a,b,S^{10}(Z)).
\]

Then

\[
\mu(s)=11,
\]

so the rewrite step increases the measure:

\[
\mu(F(s,y,Z))=1 < 11=\mu(s).
\]

There is also a contextual problem: \(\mu(G(a,b))=\mu(b)\) ignores the first argument, so rewriting inside that first argument does not decrease \(\mu\) at all.

The measure is syntactically defined from the presented symbols and does not itself add structural axioms, but it is not a valid strict descent argument.
