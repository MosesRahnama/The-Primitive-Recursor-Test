**Assessment:** Yes – strong normalisation (SN) of the shown first‑order rewrite calculus can be proved from the calculus alone, without adding any structural axioms or hidden assumptions.

**Why the system terminates**

1. **Measure that decreases** – Define a lexicographic tuple  

   \[
   M(t)\;=\;\bigl(E(t)+R(t),\;A(t),\;|t|\bigr)
   \]

   where  

   * \(E(t)\) = number of occurrences of the constructor **eqW** in \(t\);  
   * \(R(t)\) = number of occurrences of **recΔ** whose third argument is a **delta**‑term (i.e. `recΔ _ _ (delta _)`);  
   * \(A(t)\) = sum of the sizes of those third arguments (the “delta‑arguments”) of all such **recΔ**;  
   * \(|t|\) = ordinary constructor‑size (total number of constructors).

   The first component counts the “expansion heads’’ (eqW and active recΔ).  
   The second component records how deep the remaining delta‑arguments are.  
   The third component is the total size.

2. **Effect of each rewrite rule** – A short case analysis shows that for every rule the tuple \(M\) decreases lexicographically:

   * **R_int_delta**, **R_merge_void_left/right**, **R_merge_cancel**, **R_rec_zero**, **R_eq_refl** – the first component does not increase, the second component stays the same, and the size strictly drops, so \(M\) drops.
   * **R_eq_diff** – removes one eqW, so \(E\) (hence the first component) drops by one; \(R\) and \(A\) are unchanged, therefore \(M\) drops.
   * **R_rec_succ** – the left‑hand side contains a recΔ with a delta‑argument, so \(R\) counts it. After the step we have a new recΔ whose third argument is the subterm \(n\) that was inside the delta.  
        *If \(n\) is not a delta‑term, \(R\) drops by 1.*  
        *If \(n\) still is a delta‑term, \(R\) stays the same, but the size of that delta‑argument shrinks by at least one constructor, i.e. \(A\) drops.*  
     In either case the first component \(E+R\) never increases, the second component \(A\) strictly decreases, so the whole tuple decreases lexicographically. The size may increase, but it is only consulted after the first two components are equal, which never happens for this rule.

   Hence every rewrite step makes \(M\) smaller in the lexicographic order.

3. **Key premise** – The premise required is that the lexicographic product of well‑founded orders (the natural numbers with the usual order) is itself well‑founded. This is a standard theorem of arithmetic and does not rely on any extra logical principles beyond the ordinary axioms for the natural numbers, which are already available in the meta‑theory of Lean 4. Since the measure \(M\) maps every term to a triple of natural numbers and strictly decreases for every rule, no infinite rewrite sequence can exist; therefore the system is strongly normalising.

**Duplication check (contract § 2)** – The only rule that might be thought to duplicate a subterm is **R_merge_cancel** (`merge t t → t`), but that rule *contracts* two copies of \(t\) into one; the RHS contains fewer copies of \(t\) than the LHS, so there is no additive increase of the measure. No rule creates a new occurrence of a subterm that was not present in the left‑hand side, so the “additive failure’’ situation never arises and no corrective fix is required.

**Conclusion:** Strong normalisation follows from the calculus alone; the proof uses a lexicographic measure that respects all rewrite rules and relies only on the standard well‑foundedness of the natural numbers. No extra axioms, structural or otherwise, are needed.
