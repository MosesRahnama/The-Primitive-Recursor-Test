Yes. Strong normalization can be established from the displayed syntax and rewrite rules alone.

A standard syntactic termination ordering suffices. For example, use a lexicographic path ordering with a precedence such as

\[
\mathsf{cherry} \;>\; \mathsf{banana} \;>\; \mathsf{mango} \;>\; \mathsf{peach}
\;>\; \mathsf{pear} \;>\; \mathsf{grape} \;>\; \mathsf{plum},
\]

with lexicographic status for `banana`. Every rule decreases in that ordering:

- The `peach` and zero/cancel rules return a proper subterm.
- `mango (grape t) → plum` and `cherry a a → plum` decrease by precedence.
- `cherry a b → mango (peach a b)` decreases because `cherry` is above both `mango` and `peach`, while `a` and `b` are subterms of the left-hand side.
- The only potentially recursive-looking rule,
  `banana b s (grape n) → pear s (banana b s n)`,
  decreases because `banana b s (grape n)` is lexicographically greater than `banana b s n`, using `grape n > n`, and `banana > pear`.

The disequality premise on `R_cherry_diff` only restricts when that rule may apply; it does not create an increase or undermine the ordering.

Moreover, as literally encoded, `Step` has only the listed root rules and no congruence/context-closure constructor, so the declared relation is even weaker than ordinary context-closed term rewriting. But the same ordering argument also establishes termination if one considers the usual closure under rewriting inside subterms.

Thus no additional rewrite rules, object-level axioms, guards, or structural assumptions are needed: the finite syntax and these rules admit a well-founded decreasing syntactic measure.
