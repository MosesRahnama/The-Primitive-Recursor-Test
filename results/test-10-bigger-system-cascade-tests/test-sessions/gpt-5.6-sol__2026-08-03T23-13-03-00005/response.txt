**Assessment: yes.** Strong normalization can be established syntactically for all finite terms, without assuming that terms denote natural numbers or importing semantics for `if`, arithmetic, or numeral representations.

A recursive path ordering can orient every rule. Use lexicographic status and a precedence consistent with, for example,

\[
\texttt{exp} \succ \texttt{mult} \succ \texttt{plus} \succ \texttt{minus}
\succ \texttt{PRE} \succ \texttt{SUC} \succ \cdots
\succ \texttt{NUMERAL} \succ \texttt{BIT0},\texttt{BIT1},\texttt{if},\texttt{T},\texttt{F},0,
\]

placing predicates above their Boolean results, with the mutually recursive pairs
\(\texttt{le}\approx\texttt{lt}\) and \(\texttt{ge}\approx\texttt{gt}\) in a quasi-precedence.

The recursive calls always remove an outer `NUMERAL`, `BIT0`, or `BIT1` from a recursive argument. Calls introduced under lower-precedence symbols—such as `exp` producing `mult`, `mult` producing `plus`, or `minus` producing `PRE`, `le`, and `if`—also decrease. Duplication in the exponentiation rules remains orientable because each duplicated recursive call has a structurally smaller exponent. Since every rule strictly decreases one well-founded, substitution- and context-closed ordering, arbitrary rewrite sequences terminate. No canonical-numeral invariant or missing rule for `if` is needed.
