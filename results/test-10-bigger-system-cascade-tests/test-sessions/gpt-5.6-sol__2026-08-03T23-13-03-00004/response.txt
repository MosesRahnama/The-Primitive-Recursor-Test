**Yes.** Strong normalization can be established purely syntactically, without assumptions about numerals, booleans, typing, or the intended meaning of `if`.

For example, all rules can be oriented by a multiset recursive path ordering using a quasi-precedence such as

\[
\begin{aligned}
\mathrm{exp} &\succ \mathrm{mult} \succ \mathrm{minus} \succ \mathrm{plus}
\succ \mathrm{SUC} \succ \mathrm{PRE} \succ \mathrm{EVEN} \succ \mathrm{ODD}\\
&\succ \{\mathrm{le},\mathrm{lt}\}
\succ \{\mathrm{ge},\mathrm{gt}\}
\succ \mathrm{eq} \succ \mathrm{if}
\succ \mathrm{BIT0} \succ \mathrm{BIT1}
\succ \mathrm{NUMERAL} \succ \mathrm{T} \succ \mathrm{F} \succ 0 ,
\end{aligned}
\]

where `le` and `lt` are precedence-equivalent, as are `ge` and `gt`, and all symbols use multiset status.

The recursive calls strictly remove surrounding `BIT0`, `BIT1`, or `NUMERAL` constructors from arguments. Expansive and duplicating right-hand sides, notably for `exp` and `mult`, remain smaller because their outer symbols have lower precedence and every recursive call has smaller arguments. The mutual `le`/`lt` and `ge`/`gt` calls are handled by their equivalent precedence together with strict argument decrease.

Thus every rule strictly decreases a well-founded, substitution- and context-closed reduction ordering, proving strong normalization for arbitrary first-order terms over the displayed signature.
