The helper strategy is not sound: both theorems are false for some syntactic arguments.

Let \(b = s = \texttt{void}\) and \(n = \texttt{delta void}\).

For `kappa_rec_delta_step`, the left-hand side is

\[
\begin{aligned}
\kappa(\texttt{recDelta void void (delta (delta void))})
&= \max(\max(0,0),\kappa(\texttt{delta void}))+1 \\
&= \max(0,0)+1 \\
&= 1.
\end{aligned}
\]

The right-hand side is

\[
\begin{aligned}
\kappa(\texttt{recDelta void void (delta void)})+1
&= \left(\max(\max(0,0),\kappa(\texttt{void}))+1\right)+1 \\
&= 2.
\end{aligned}
\]

Thus `kappa_rec_delta_step` asserts \(1=2\) in this instance.

The issue is that `kappa` itself adds `1` whenever the third argument is syntactically a `delta`. If `n` is already of the form `delta m`, then both sides count that extra `1`, and the theorem adds another one on the right.

The second theorem also fails for the same choice of arguments. Its left-hand side is

\[
\begin{aligned}
\kappa(\texttt{app void (recDelta void void (delta void))})
&= \max(0,1) \\
&= 1,
\end{aligned}
\]

while its right-hand side is

\[
\kappa(\texttt{recDelta void void (delta (delta void))}) = 1.
\]

So it claims \(1 < 1\), which is false.

Both statements would behave as intended only under a restriction ensuring that `n` is not syntactically a `delta` term. As universally quantified theorems, however, they cannot be proved soundly.
