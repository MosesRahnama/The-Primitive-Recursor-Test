## Assessment: **Yes**, strong normalization can be established from the given rules alone.

### Reasoning

The system consists of two rules over constructors `Z` (constant) and `S` (unary), with function symbols `F` (ternary) and `G` (binary):

1. `F(x, y, Z) → x`
2. `F(x, y, S(n)) → G(y, F(x, y, n))`

Strong normalization can be proven directly using a **Lexicographic Path Ordering (LPO)** with the simple precedence **F > G** (the ordering of S and Z is irrelevant).

**Rule 1:** `F(x, y, Z) → x`
The left-hand side is greater than `x` because `x` is a proper subterm of `F(x, y, Z)`. ✓

**Rule 2:** `F(x, y, S(n)) → G(y, F(x, y, n))`
Let `s = F(x, y, S(n))` and `t = G(y, F(x, y, n))`. For LPO with `F > G`, we need `s` to be greater than every argument of `G`:

- `s > y`: holds since `y` is a proper subterm of `s`. ✓
- `s > F(x, y, n)`: both sides share head symbol `F`, so we compare arguments lexicographically:
  - 1st: `x = x` (equal)
  - 2nd: `y = y` (equal)
  - 3rd: `S(n) > n` since `n` is a proper subterm of `S(n)` ✓

  Lexicographic comparison succeeds at the third position. ✓

Since both rules are strictly oriented by LPO (a well-founded reduction ordering closed under contexts and substitutions), the system is **strongly normalizing**.

### Intuition

The third argument of `F` acts as a descending counter: Rule 2 strips one `S` layer (producing `F(x, y, n)` from `F(x, y, S(n))`), and Rule 1 eliminates `F` entirely when the counter reaches `Z`. Although `y` is duplicated in Rule 2, the copies land under `G`, which has no rewrite rules and cannot re-trigger the duplication mechanism. This is essentially a primitive recursive iterator (analogous to Gödel's System T recursor), which is inherently terminating.
