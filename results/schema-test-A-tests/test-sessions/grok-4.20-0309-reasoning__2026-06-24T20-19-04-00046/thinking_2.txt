Thinking about the system:

The rules are:

F(x, y, Z) -> x

F(x, y, S(n)) -> G(y, F(x, y, n))

This is a first-order TRS with no variable binding.

To determine if it's strongly normalizing (SN): every term has finite reduction sequences, no infinite chains.

From my earlier calculation: when applying the rule F(x,y,S(n)) -> G(y,F(x,y,n)), if y contains S symbols, the total number of S symbols can increase because y is copied twice.

But S symbols are not necessarily in third arg positions.

To have a redex, the third arg must match Z or S( ).

If the third arg is not headed by S or Z, no reduction for that F.

To have infinite reduction, we'd need an infinite sequence of rule applications.

Since 1. the rules are leftmost or whatever, but for SN, all sequences terminate.

Importantly, the rules never introduce new S constructors in the third argument; they consume them.

But because of copying y, if y has F's that produce S in third args, it can duplicate those.

Let's try to find a counterexample for non-SN.

Suppose we have a term that allows creating a cycle or infinite descent.

Suppose we let the third arg be something that reduces to S, but F doesn't produce S.

F's RHS is x or G(y, F..), neither headed by S or Z.

So F never produces S or Z.

Therefore, the only S in third arg positions come from the initial term or from y being substituted with terms that have S in third arg of F.
