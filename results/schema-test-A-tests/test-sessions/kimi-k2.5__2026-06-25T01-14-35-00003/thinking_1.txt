This induction works! We prove that every term of the form F(s,t,u) is SN, assuming s, t, u are SN.
Actually, we need to be careful. 

Standard proof for primitive recursion:
We want to show: if s, t, u are SN, then F(s,t,u) is SN.

But u might not be a pure natural number. The rules only apply if u matches Z or S(n).

Let's formalize:

Lemma: For any terms s, t, u, if s, t, u ∈ SN, then F(s,t,u) ∈ SN.

Proof by induction on the pair (the set of reduction sequences of u, and the structure of u)... messy.

Actually, use the fact that SN is closed under contexts for passive positions.

Better: Use induction on the "depth" of u as a natural number expression.

Since Z and S are free constructors, any term u either:
- is a variable,
- is headed by G,
- is headed by F,
- is Z,
- is S(v).

Case analysis on u:

Case 1: u = Z.
F(s,t,Z) -> s.
If s ∈ SN, then F(s,t,Z) ∈ SN (one step to SN term).

Case 2: u = S(v).
F(s,t,S(v)) -> G(t, F(s,t,v)).
We need: if s,t,S(v) ∈ SN, then RHS ∈ SN.
Since S(v) ∈ SN, we have v ∈ SN.
By induction on v (v is structurally smaller than S(v)), F(s,t,v) ∈ SN.
Since t ∈ SN, and G is a constructor, G(t, F(s,t,v)) ∈ SN.
Thus F(s,t,S(v)) ∈ SN.

Case 3: u is not headed by Z or S (i.e., u is a variable, or G(...), or F(...)).
Then F(s,t,u) is a normal form (no rule applies), so ∈ SN.

This proves that for any SN terms s,t,u, we have F(s,t,u) ∈ SN.

Now, by structural induction on any term T, we can show T ∈ SN.

Base: T is a variable or Z. Normal form, SN.

Inductive: 
- T = S(u). By IH, u ∈ SN. S(u) is SN because the only reductions are inside u.
- T = G(u,v). By IH, u,v ∈ SN. G(u,v) is SN (only inner reductions).
- T = F(u,v,w). By IH, u,v,w ∈ SN. By the Lemma above, F(u,v,w) ∈ SN.

Therefore, every term is SN. The system is strongly normalizing.

So the answer is YES, strong normalization can be established from the rewrite system alone.

The reasoning is that this is a structural recursion on the third argument, which follows the well-founded structure of the natural number constructors Z and S. The recursion is well-founded because each recursive call peels off one S constructor, and the system has no rules that can create "infinite" third arguments or circumvent the structural descent.

Double-checking: Are there any tricky cases?

What about F(x, y, n) where n reduces to something else?
Example: n is a variable. F(x,y,n) is a normal form.
n reduces to... wait, variables don't reduce.
n is instantiated when we consider ground instances, but for the open term F(x,y,n), it's already a normal form if n is a variable.

What if n is F(s,t,Z)? 
F(x,y,F(s,t,Z)) - the outer F has third argument F(s,t,Z), which is not Z and not S(something), so no rule applies. It's a normal form.

Wait, but F(s,t,Z) reduces to s.
So we have F(x,y,F(s,t,Z)).
We can reduce the inner F: F(s,t,Z) -> s, so the whole term becomes F(x,y,s).
Now F(x,y,s) might be reducible if s is Z or S(...).

But in the original F(x,y,F(s,t,Z)), no rule applies to the outer F because the third argument is not headed by a constructor of the right type - it's headed by F.

This is crucial. The outer F is "stuck" until the inner F is reduced away.

Sequence: F(x,y,F(s,t,Z)) ->* F(x,y,s) (after reducing the inner F to s).
Then if s = S(v), we get G(y, F(x,y,v)).

This is fine. The reduction of inner redexes can only help the outer F become active, or not affect it.

Since all F's either:
1. Are stuck (third arg not Z/S)
2. Reduce to first arg (third arg Z)
3. Reduce to G-wrapped recursive call (third arg S(n))

And in case 3, the recursive call has a strictly smaller third argument (structurally), and the G has no rules, so we can't loop.

Therefore, yes, SN holds.

The answer is that strong normalization can indeed be established.

The system represents a primitive recursive function (iteration), and terminates because the recursion is structural on the third argument which belongs to a well-founded datatype (the naturals).
