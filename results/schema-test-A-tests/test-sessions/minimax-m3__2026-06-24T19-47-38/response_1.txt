**Reasoning:**

The system is essentially a structurally recursive definition of a two-argument function on the natural numbers (via the constructors `Z` and `S`):

- `F(x, y, Z) → x`  (base case)
- `F(x, y, S(n)) → G(y, F(x, y, n))`  (recursive case)

A direct termination argument requires no external axioms:

1. **The only redex form is `F(_, _, _)`.** There is no rule whose left-hand side matches a `G`-headed term, so `G(y, …)` is in normal form regardless of its argument. Hence any further reduction must occur inside that argument — but the only redex inside is the recursive call `F(x, y, n)`.

2. **The third argument strictly decreases.** In rule (2), the third argument goes from `S(n)` to `n`, which is an immediate structural subterm. In rule (1), it is the constructor `Z` with no further rewrite.

3. **Termination by induction on the structure of the third argument.** By structural induction on the third argument of `F`:
   - Base: if it is `Z`, rule (1) fires once and the term is normal.
   - Step: if it is `S(n)`, rule (2) applies once, producing a `G`-application whose only redex candidate is `F(x, y, n)`. By the induction hypothesis, that inner `F` is strongly normalizing, so the whole term is strongly normalizing.

4. **No other redexes are introduced.** The right-hand sides contain no `F`-redex other than the strictly smaller recursive call, and no `G`-rule exists.

This is a textbook example of a primitive-recursive / structurally recursive definition, and the SN proof is purely local to the rules as given — no induction axioms, well-foundedness of the natural numbers, or other object-level assumptions need to be imported beyond what is directly derivable from the rules themselves (e.g., via a polynomial/interpretation-based termination proof or a structural induction on the third argument).
