Yes — under the standard reading of a first-order TRS on finite terms, strong normalization can be established from these rules alone.

Briefly why:

- The only recursive rule is  
  `F(x, y, S(n)) -> G(y, F(x, y, n))`
  and the recursive call to `F` is on the strictly smaller third argument `n` rather than `S(n)`.

- There are no rules for `G`, `S`, or `Z`, so nothing can rebuild that stripped `S` or turn a `G` back into an `F`.

- Although rule 2 duplicates `y`, that does not create a loop here; it only copies an existing subterm. A standard reduction ordering still proves termination.

A clean formal proof is by a lexicographic path ordering (or similar recursive path ordering), e.g. with precedence

`F > G > S > Z`.

Then:

1. `F(x,y,Z) -> x` is decreasing because the right-hand side `x` is a proper subterm of the left-hand side.

2. `F(x,y,S(n)) -> G(y, F(x,y,n))` is decreasing because:
   - the left side has top symbol `F`, which is above `G`, and
   - the recursive occurrence `F(x,y,n)` is smaller than `F(x,y,S(n))` since the first two arguments are the same and `S(n) > n`.

So the rewrite relation is contained in a well-founded order. Hence the system is strongly normalizing, with no extra structural or object-level assumptions needed.
