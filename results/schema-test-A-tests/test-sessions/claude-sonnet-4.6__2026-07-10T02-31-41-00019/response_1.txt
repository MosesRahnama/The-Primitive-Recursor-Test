## Assessment

**Yes, strong normalization can be established from the presented rewrite system alone.**

### Reasoning

**Structure of the system:**

The two rules are:
1. `F(x, y, Z) -> x`
2. `F(x, y, S(n)) -> G(y, F(x, y, n))`

**Key observations:**

1. **The third argument strictly decreases.** In rule 2, `F(x, y, S(n))` rewrites with the recursive call `F(x, y, n)`. The third argument goes from `S(n)` to `n` — a strict structural decrease on a natural number term. This is a standard primitive recursive descent.

2. **`G` is not defined by any rewrite rule.** It appears only on the right-hand side of rule 2 and has no left-hand side rule of its own. This means `G(y, ...)` is a **constructor/irreducible** form — it never triggers further rewriting on its own.

3. **No interaction or overlap.** The two rules have disjoint left-hand sides (discriminated by `Z` vs `S(n)` in the third argument). There are no critical pairs.

4. **A simple lexicographic or polynomial interpretation works.** Assign the size of the third argument of `F` as a measure. Each application of rule 2 strictly reduces this measure by 1 (stripping one `S`), and rule 1 terminates immediately. Since the third argument is a finite term, this measure is always a natural number, and reduction must terminate.

5. **No self-referential or mutually recursive rules involving `G`.** Since `G` has no rewrite rules, calls to `G` are stuck/normal — they cannot generate new `F`-redexes.

### Conclusion

Strong normalization holds by a straightforward **structural induction on the third argument of `F`**, derivable entirely from the given rules without any external axioms.
