## Assessment

**Yes — strong normalization can be established from the presented system alone.**

## Reasoning

The system has two rules:

```
F(x, y, Z)    -> x
F(x, y, S(n)) -> G(y, F(x, y, n))
```

**Key structural observations derivable directly from the rules:**

1. The right-hand sides contain no new defined-function applications other than `F` itself (and the constructor `G`, which has no rules). `G` is a free constructor here — it cannot be rewritten.

2. In rule 2, the only recursive `F`-subterm on the right is `F(x, y, n)`, whose **third argument strictly decreases**: from `S(n)` to `n`.

3. Rule 1 simply projects to `x`, introducing no redexes that weren't already present.

**A simple terminating measure.** Define the measure of a term as the multiset (or sum) of the sizes of the third arguments of all `F`-occurrences in it:

- Rule 1 removes an `F`-redex entirely (and returns `x`, a subterm), strictly decreasing the count of `F`-symbols / size.
- Rule 2 replaces `F(x, y, S(n))` with `G(y, F(x, y, n))`. The single recursive call has third argument `n`, structurally smaller than `S(n)`. No `F`-occurrence increases its third-argument size, and we gain only a `G` (an irreducible constructor) plus a copy of `y` (which contains no extra `F` that could grow — any `F` inside `y` is just duplicated, but its third-argument sizes are unchanged, and the *triggering* redex strictly shrank).

More carefully, since `y` may itself contain `F`-subterms and rule 2 duplicates `y`... but note: duplication of `y` does **not** change the third arguments of the `F`s already inside `y`; they remain whatever they were, and the rule that fired strictly decreased a third argument. A standard well-founded ordering (e.g., a polynomial/recursive path ordering where `F`'s recursion descends on its third argument, with `S > Z`) handles this cleanly:

- **RPO / lexicographic path order**: take `F` to recurse on its third argument; `S(n) ▷ n` gives the descent, and `G`/`Z` are constructors. The right-hand side `G(y, F(x,y,n))` is strictly smaller than `F(x,y,S(n))` because the recursive `F`-subterm has a smaller third component.

Since each rewrite step strictly decreases this well-founded measure, there can be no infinite reduction sequence.

## Conclusion

Strong normalization is provable **from the rules alone**, with no need to import external structural axioms or object-level assumptions. The structural decrease (`S(n) → n`) in the only recursive position, combined with the absence of rules for the constructor `G`, suffices — e.g., via a recursive/lexicographic path ordering or a direct size measure on third arguments.
