**Assessment: Yes**, strong normalization can be established directly from the given rewrite system.

**Reasoning:**

The key is that `F` is the only symbol that gives rise to rewrite steps, and its recursion is *structurally decreasing* on its third argument — a quantity provable from the rule syntax alone, with no need for imported axioms.

Concretely, for any closed term `F(t₁, t₂, t₃)`:

- If `t₃` is `Z`, the rule `F(x, y, Z) → x` fires in one step to a term in which `F` no longer appears in the root position. The result is a normal form (no rules apply to `x`).
- If `t₃` is `S(s)`, the rule `F(x, y, S(n)) → G(y, F(x, y, n))` produces a term whose `F`-redex is the strict subterm `F(x, y, n)`. The third argument has gone from `S(s)` to `s`, which is a smaller term in the well-founded subterm ordering on the inductively defined syntax of terms.

`G` carries no rules, so terms rooted at `G` are in normal form regardless of their arguments; they cannot trigger any further expansion.

Therefore, by induction on the structure (specifically on the third argument of the head `F`):

- Base case: `F(·, ·, Z)` normalizes in 1 step.
- Inductive case: `F(·, ·, S(s))` reduces to `G(·, F(·, ·, s))`, where the inner `F(·, ·, s)` normalizes by the inductive hypothesis, and the surrounding `G` is already a normal form.

No assumption about an external natural-number induction principle, arithmetic, or semantics of `Z`/`S` is needed — the structural recursion on the third argument of `F` is visible directly in the rules, and this suffices to prove every `F`-rooted term strongly normalizing (and hence every term, since `G` cannot start a reduction). Strong normalization follows from the rewrite system alone.
