**Assessment: Yes, strong normalization can be established from the presented rewrite system alone.**

**Reasoning:**

The system consists of two rules over function symbols F (ternary), G (binary), S (unary), and Z (constant):

1. `F(x, y, Z) → x`
2. `F(x, y, S(n)) → G(y, F(x, y, n))`

Strong normalization can be proven using a **Multiset Path Ordering (MPO)** with the precedence **F > G > S** (Z's position in the precedence is irrelevant as it never appears as a root symbol on a right-hand side).

**Rule 1:** `F(x, y, Z) > x` holds directly by the subterm property (x is a direct subterm of the left-hand side).

**Rule 2:** `F(x, y, S(n)) > G(y, F(x, y, n))` is verified as follows:
- Since F > G in the precedence, it suffices to show the left-hand side is greater than each argument of G:
  - `F(x, y, S(n)) > y` — by the subterm property.
  - `F(x, y, S(n)) > F(x, y, n)` — since both have root symbol F, we compare argument multisets: `{x, y, S(n)} >_mul {x, y, n}`. After cancelling the common elements x and y, this reduces to `S(n) > n`, which holds by the subterm property. The required subterm conditions (`F(x,y,S(n))` greater than each of x, y, n) also all hold.

Since MPO is a well-founded simplification ordering and both rules are strictly oriented left-to-right, every reduction sequence must terminate. The essential intuition is that Rule 2 recurses on a structurally smaller third argument (n vs. S(n)), and while y is duplicated into the G-context, the precedence F > G ensures this duplication cannot cause non-termination.
