I picked structural (primitive) recursion on the natural number argument `i` because that is the *simplest method that is actually adequate* to the shape of the equations, and using anything heavier would be importing machinery the problem doesn't need — while using anything weaker wouldn't work. Specifically:

1. **The recursion pattern is literally the `nat` case split.** The two equations dispatch on `0` and `Suc i`, which are exactly the two constructors of `nat`. Whenever a function's equations are indexed by constructor patterns on one argument, structural recursion on that argument is the canonical, minimal-assumption termination method — it doesn't require inventing a measure function or a custom well-founded order, since the needed order (subterm/predecessor order on `nat`) is already built into what "the type `nat`" means, not an extra axiom.

2. **There is exactly one recursive call, and its argument is the immediate predecessor.** In the `Suc i` case, the only recursive occurrence is `extract_prefix w G i`, and `i` is precisely the substructure exposed by matching `Suc i`. This is the textbook signature of primitive recursion: recursive call on the immediate subterm of the matched constructor. When this pattern holds, structural recursion applies directly with no need for lexicographic combinations, multiset orders, or accumulator-based measures.

3. **The other arguments (`w`, `G`) are untouched by the recursion** — they're just threaded through unchanged. So there's no need for a multi-argument well-founded order (e.g. lexicographic on `(w,G,i)`); a single-argument measure (`i` itself) suffices, which is the weakest/simplest sufficient tool.

4. **`best_eclass_term` is used only as a black-box total function.** Since it's given as total and not recursively defined here, it cannot contribute to or threaten termination — it's just a value-producing step inside the body, like `@` (append) or `!` (indexing). I don't need to (and shouldn't) reason about its internals; treating it as an opaque total operation keeps the argument within the stated boundary (no hidden assumptions about its structure).

5. **I avoided fixpoint/domain-theoretic or measure-invention arguments** (e.g. defining a custom well-founded relation, or appealing to general recursion with an accumulator-based termination proof) because they'd be overkill and would risk implicitly smuggling in extra structure not licensed by the equations. Structural recursion is the minimal, syntax-driven justification that maps directly onto what's written: one base equation, one step equation with a single strictly-decreasing recursive call.

In short: the equations *are* a primitive recursion scheme on `nat`, so the proof method should just be "recognize it as such," rather than reaching for more general (and more assumption-laden) termination techniques.
