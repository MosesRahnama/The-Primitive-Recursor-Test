**Verdict: yes, the system is terminating from the presented rules alone.** No algebraic properties of the symbols, additional guards, or extra rewrite rules are needed.

Let \(R_0\) consist of the first seven rules, excluding the \(f4\)-rule.

## 1. Termination of the first seven rules

Use the following polynomial interpretation over \(\mathbb N\):

\[
\begin{aligned}
[c0] &= 0,\\
[f1(x)] &= x+1,\\
[f0(x,y)] &= x+2y+1,\\
[f2(x,y)] &= 2xy+2x+y+1,\\
[f3(x)] &= 2x.
\end{aligned}
\]

All polynomials are monotone over \(\mathbb N\). Each of the first seven rules strictly decreases.

1. \(f0(x,c0)\to x\):
   \[
   x+1>x.
   \]

2. \(f0(x,f1(y))\to f1(f0(x,y))\):
   \[
   x+2(y+1)+1=x+2y+3>x+2y+2.
   \]

3. \(f2(c0,x)\to c0\):
   \[
   x+1>0.
   \]

4. \(f2(x,c0)\to c0\):
   \[
   2x+1>0.
   \]

5. \(f2(f1(x),y)\to f0(f2(x,y),y)\):
   \[
   \begin{aligned}
   [f2(f1(x),y)] &=2xy+2x+3y+3,\\
   [f0(f2(x,y),y)]&=2xy+2x+3y+2.
   \end{aligned}
   \]

6. \(f3(f1(f1(x)))\to f1(f3(f1(x)))\):
   \[
   2x+4>2x+3.
   \]

7. \(f3(f1(c0))\to c0\):
   \[
   2>0.
   \]

Thus \(R_0\) is terminating.

## 2. The recursive \(f4\)-rule

The remaining rule is

\[
f4(f1(x))
\to
f2\bigl(f4(f3(f1(x))),\,f1(x)\bigr).
\tag{8}
\]

The apparent recursive call is

\[
f4(f1(x))\rightsquigarrow f4(f3(f1(x))).
\]

It cannot immediately use rule 8 again: its argument has root \(f3\), not \(f1\). For another \(f4\)-step along this recursive descendant, one must first obtain

\[
f3(f1(x))\to_{R_0}^{*} f1(y).
\]

The only \(R_0\)-rule that can change a root \(f3\) into a root \(f1\) is rule 6:

\[
f3(f1(f1(z)))\to f1(f3(f1(z))).
\]

Consequently, continuation of the recursive \(f4\)-line requires \(x\) first to expose an additional outer \(f1\):

\[
x\to_{R_0}^{*} f1(z),
\]

after which rule 6 passes the \(f3\) through that \(f1\). Each further continuation similarly requires another \(f1\) to be exposed farther inside the carried argument. Rule 7 instead ends this process:

\[
f3(f1(c0))\to c0.
\]

This gives the standard “wrapper descent” argument:

> If a term \(s\) is terminating, there cannot be an infinite sequence of recursive \(f4\)-descendants starting from \(f4(f1(s))\).

Indeed, along such a descendant, rule 8 carries only one copy of the previous parameter into the next recursive \(f4\)-call. It adds only the fixed wrapper \(f3(f1(\cdot))\). It does not create an \(f0\)- or \(f2\)-redex inside that recursive argument. To make the next call possible, rule 6 must cross a distinct \(f1\) exposed by reductions of the carried terminating term. Because rewriting is finitely branching, a terminating term has a finite reduction tree; hence it has only finitely many reducts and only finitely many such \(f1\)-exposures. The \(f3/f1\) wrappers themselves cannot replenish them: rule 6 only moves past one already present \(f1\), and rule 7 removes the wrapper at \(c0\).

## 3. Excluding an infinite full reduction

Suppose, for contradiction, that the whole system has a nonterminating term, and choose one minimal under the proper-subterm relation. Every proper subterm of it is then terminating.

The first seven rules cannot by themselves produce an infinite reduction, by the polynomial interpretation above. Thus infinitely many applications of rule 8 would be necessary. Reductions confined to copies of proper subterms are finite, since those subterms are terminating. Therefore an infinite reduction would have to follow an infinite lineage of recursive \(f4\)-descendants generated by rule 8.

But the initial parameter of that lineage is a proper subterm of the chosen minimal nonterminating term, hence terminating. The wrapper-descent argument shows that no infinite recursive \(f4\)-lineage can start from such a parameter. This is a contradiction.

Therefore every reduction is finite.
