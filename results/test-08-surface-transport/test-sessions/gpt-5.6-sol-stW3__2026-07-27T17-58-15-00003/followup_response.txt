I picked the hybrid method because the system has two very different termination mechanisms.

1. **The first seven rules are directly decreasing.**  
   A small polynomial interpretation or another standard reduction ordering handles them uniformly. Using a polynomial interpretation made it easy to check every one explicitly.

2. **The \(f4\)-rule is not syntactically decreasing:**
   \[
   f4(f1(x))\to f2(f4(f3(f1(x))),f1(x)).
   \]
   The recursive argument changes from \(f1(x)\) to \(f3(f1(x))\), which is structurally larger, and \(x\) is duplicated on the right. Thus a simple term-size measure, ordinary structural induction, or an uncomplicated simplification ordering is unlikely to orient this rule.

3. **Nevertheless, the recursion is gated.**  
   The newly produced call
   \[
   f4(f3(f1(x)))
   \]
   is not itself an \(f4\)-redex. It can become one only when the \(f3\)-rules move through an already available \(f1\):
   \[
   f3(f1(f1(z)))\to f1(f3(f1(z))).
   \]
   Each continuation therefore consumes another \(f1\)-layer supplied by the carried argument. This is the real well-founded quantity behind the \(f4\)-recursion, even though it is not captured by ordinary term size.

So the chosen proof separates:

- **local rewriting termination**, established algebraically for \(R_0\), from
- **recursive-call termination**, established by tracing the only possible lineage of \(f4\)-calls.

The minimal-counterexample step was chosen to make this separation legitimate: it ensures that reductions occurring inside the parameter cannot themselves run forever. Conceptually, the argument is close to a **dependency-pair proof**: the only significant recursive dependency is

\[
f4^\#(f1(x))\to f4^\#(f3(f1(x))),
\]

and the \(R_0\)-rules required to reconnect this pair can do so only by passing through finitely many exposed \(f1\)-layers. A dependency-pair presentation would have been more formal machinery for essentially the same reason, while the hybrid proof exposes the mechanism directly.
