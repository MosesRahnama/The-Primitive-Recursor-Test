I chose the two-part strategy because the system itself splits naturally along a fault line, and each part demanded a different tool for structural reasons — not out of preference but because the alternatives failed or were unnecessary.

**Why RPO for rules 1–7:**

1. **The rules are shape-recursive in an orderable way.** Each rule (except rule 8) either deletes a symbol (rules 1, 3, 4, 7 collapse to a subterm or constant) or pushes a smaller argument under an unchanged or lower-precedence head (rules 2, 5, 6). This is exactly the pattern RPO is built to detect: it doesn't require an a priori termination measure, just a precedence and the subterm/recursive-comparison machinery, so it's the lightest tool that can certify the whole cluster at once rather than proving each rule in isolation with ad hoc arguments.

2. **I checked for RPO-compatibility before committing.** The key test is always: does any rule require comparing a term against something that properly contains it (violating the subterm property)? For rules 1–7, no — every RHS is literally a subterm of the LHS, or built from strictly smaller pieces under a bounded precedence. So a simplification order suffices, and simplification orders are preferable when applicable because they give a very short, mechanically checkable certificate (a precedence + status), rather than needing a bespoke inductive argument.

3. **I picked precedence `f4>f2>f0>f3>f1>c0`** specifically because it's forced by the recursive calls: rule 5 needs `f2>f0`, rule 2 needs `f0>f1`, rule 6 needs `f3>f1`, and rule 8 (informally) wants `f4` on top since it's the "driver" function calling everything else. This is just reading off the necessary precedence constraints from each rule's recursive structure, then checking they're jointly satisfiable (they are, no cycle).

**Why I abandoned simplification orders for rule 8, and used explicit descent instead:**

1. **I tried to extend the same RPO/KBO argument to rule 8 first** (that's the standard move — try one method uniformly). It broke immediately: orienting `f4(f1(v0)) -> f2(f4(f3(f1(v0))), f1(v0))` requires, in the recursive `f4`-vs-`f4` comparison, `f1(v0) > f3(f1(v0))`. But `f1(v0)` sits *inside* `f3(f1(v0))` as its direct argument — so this is asking the order to declare a term greater than something containing it as a subterm. Every simplification order satisfies `f(x) ≥ x` (subterm property), which forces `f3(f1(v0)) ≥ f1(v0)`, the opposite inequality. This isn't a failure of a particular precedence or weight choice — it's a structural obstruction that rules out *all* simplification orders (RPO, LPO, KBO, polynomial interpretations that are monotone extensions of the subterm order, etc.) uniformly. So I stopped searching in that family, since further search there is provably futile.

2. **Because rule 8's obstruction is specifically about the subterm property, not about complexity of the recursion**, the natural next move is to look for a semantic reason why the argument to `f4` still shrinks in some *other* well-founded sense — i.e., go around the subterm order rather than through it. That's why I looked at what `f3(f1(v0))` actually computes: using the already-proven termination of rules 6–7 (a sub-system I could analyze independently, since it's closed and simpler), I could show it always reduces to something whose "leading-`f1`-depth" is strictly smaller than that of `f1(v0)`, or gets stuck. This gives a genuine natural-number measure (the depth `d`) that decreases across successive rule-8 firings, even though no single fixed order can see this decrease at the syntactic level directly (since the intermediate term temporarily grows via the subterm violation).

3. **This is a standard technique** (measure/depth induction combined with an auxiliary termination fact about a subsystem) — essentially a small dependency-pair-style decomposition: treat rules 1–7 as the "base" terminating system, then argue rule 8's recursive call is applied to a strictly smaller instance under a derived measure, using base-system termination as a lemma. I used it here specifically because the direct order-theoretic route was blocked for a provable structural reason, and this was the minimal additional machinery needed to close the gap without inventing new axioms or side conditions — the depth measure and the subsystem behavior are both derivable purely from the eight given rules.

In short: I always try the cheapest certificate (simplification order) first, applied it successfully to seven of the eight rules, diagnosed exactly why it must fail on the eighth (subterm-property violation), and then supplied the minimal well-founded argument (finite descent on a depth measure, backed by an independently terminating subsystem) that is forced by that specific obstruction.
