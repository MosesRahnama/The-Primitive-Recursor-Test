I chose **proper-subterm descent** because the termination evidence is visible directly in the syntax of the recursive equation:

\[
x \# y \# xs \quad\longrightarrow\quad y \# xs.
\]

The recursive argument is literally the tail, hence a proper syntactic subterm. This method was preferable because:

- It uses only the displayed equations and the inductive finite-list syntax.
- It requires no assumptions about `W`, `+`, `x`, `y`, or the element type.
- It handles the two base equations immediately because they contain no recursive calls.
- It avoids adding a length function or importing arithmetic facts about list lengths.

A length-based measure would give the same argument:

\[
\operatorname{length}(y\#xs)
<
\operatorname{length}(x\#y\#xs),
\]

but it introduces auxiliary machinery unnecessarily. Proper-subterm ordering expresses the syntactic decrease more directly.

The important qualification is that this choice relies on the ordinary inductive reading of list syntax: list terms are finite and proper-subterm descent is well-founded. If even that structural fact is forbidden as an external object-level axiom and is not supplied by the setting, then the equations by themselves do not state well-foundedness. Under that unusually strict reading, no all-input termination theorem follows solely from the three equalities. My method was chosen because well-foundedness of finite syntax is normally a metatheoretic property of the presented syntax, not an extra equation or assumption about the function.
