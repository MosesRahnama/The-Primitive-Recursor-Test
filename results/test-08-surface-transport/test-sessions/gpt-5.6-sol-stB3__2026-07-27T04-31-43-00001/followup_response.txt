I picked **lexicographic path ordering (LPO)** because the recursive rule has exactly the structural pattern LPO handles naturally:

\[
f3(v0,f0(v1,f0(v2,v3)))
\to
f1\bigl(f2(f2(v0,v1),v2),\,f3(v0,f0(v2,v3))\bigr).
\]

Two features matter:

1. **The recursive `f3` call gets a smaller second argument.**

   The second argument changes from

   \[
   f0(v1,f0(v2,v3))
   \]

   to its proper subterm

   \[
   f0(v2,v3).
   \]

   With lexicographic status for `f3`, the unchanged first argument and strictly smaller second argument directly orient the recursive call.

2. **The right-hand side introduces lower-precedence symbols.**

   The wrapper `f1` and the constructed `f2` terms can be placed below `f3` in the precedence. LPO then reduces the proof to showing that the left-hand side dominates each argument of those lower-rooted terms—which follows from the subterm property and the smaller recursive call.

The two base rules are also immediate because their right-hand side is the constant `c1`, placed below `f3`.

I preferred LPO over alternatives because it is:

- **Purely syntactic:** it assumes no meaning for `f0`, such as “list constructor.”
- **Substitution- and context-stable:** orienting the displayed rules suffices for all rewrite instances.
- **Well-founded on finite terms:** it yields termination directly.
- **A close fit to the rule shape:** it captures both the descent to a proper subterm and the precedence drop from `f3` to `f1`/`f2`.

A polynomial interpretation might also work, but finding and verifying suitable coefficients would be less direct. A simple term-size argument would not work transparently because the recursive rule duplicates material and makes the right-hand side larger in ordinary symbol count. LPO avoids that issue by measuring structural precedence and recursive descent rather than raw size.
