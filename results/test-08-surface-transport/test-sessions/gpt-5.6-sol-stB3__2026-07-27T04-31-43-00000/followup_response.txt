I chose **lexicographic path ordering (LPO)** because the third rule has exactly the pattern LPO handles well:

1. **The recursive call is structurally smaller.**  
   In
   \[
   f3(v0,f0(v1,f0(v2,v3)))
   \to \cdots f3(v0,f0(v2,v3)),
   \]
   the first argument remains \(v0\), while the second argument changes from
   \[
   f0(v1,f0(v2,v3))
   \]
   to its proper subterm
   \[
   f0(v2,v3).
   \]
   Lexicographic status for \(f3\) captures precisely this decrease.

2. **The recursive result is wrapped in other symbols.**  
   The right-hand side has root \(f1\) and contains \(f2\)-terms. By choosing
   \[
   f3\succ f1,\qquad f3\succ f2,
   \]
   LPO lets the original \(f3\)-term dominate that surrounding construction, provided it also dominates the construction’s arguments—which it does by the recursive decrease and subterm property.

3. **Simple size measures are unsuitable.**  
   The third rule increases ordinary term size and duplicates \(v0\) across the right-hand side. Thus counting symbols, depth, or variable occurrences would not directly orient it. LPO is insensitive to that kind of growth when the recursive call descends structurally and the new enclosing symbols have lower precedence.

4. **The base rules become immediate.**  
   Choosing \(f3\succ c1\) orients both rules ending in \(c1\).

5. **It stays within the requested boundary.**  
   LPO is only a meta-level well-founded ordering on the finite terms already given. Its subterm property is part of the definition of the ordering, not an added object-level axiom, rewrite rule, guard, or side condition.

So LPO was the natural choice because it simultaneously recognizes the structural descent in the recursive \(f3\)-call and tolerates the larger, branching \(f1/f2\) context introduced on the right-hand side.
