I picked the dependency-pair (DP) method with usable rules because a preliminary attempt at a direct simplification-order proof (RPO/LPO/KBO/subterm-respecting polynomial orders) revealed a structural obstruction that no such order can overcome, and DP/usable-rules is the standard technique designed precisely to get around that obstruction.

**The obstruction.** Rule 8,
`f4(f1(v0)) -> f2(f4(f3(f1(v0))), f1(v0))`,
has the same head symbol `f4` on both sides, feeding into itself via the subterm `f3(f1(v0))`. But `f3(f1(v0))` *syntactically contains* `f1(v0))` as a proper subterm. Any simplification order — and that includes all standard orders used for direct termination proofs (LPO, RPO, KBO, and polynomial orders satisfying the subterm property) — must always rank a term above its proper subterms. So such an order is forced to conclude `f3(f1(v0)) > f1(v0)`, which is the *opposite* of the decrease needed to justify the recursive call in rule 8. Hence no direct simplification-order argument can possibly work here; something has to give.

**Why DP + usable rules resolves it.**

1. **Decoupling head comparisons from full-rule comparisons.** DPs only require comparing the "recursive call instances" (the tuples of defined-symbol subterms on the right that trigger further reduction) against the left-hand side head, not the entire right-hand side of the rule as a whole term. This means I don't need to orient rule 8 as `f4(f1(v0)) > f2(f4(f3(f1(v0))), f1(v0))` in one go — I only need `F4(f1(v0)) > F4(f3(f1(v0)))` as a *separate*, independently choosable constraint (DP6), decoupled from justifying the `f2(...)` wrapper or the other argument `f1(v0)`.

2. **Usable rules let the interpretation be non-subterm-respecting locally.** Once decoupled, I'm free to interpret `f3` semantically rather than syntactically — e.g., as a predecessor-like operation `[f3](x) = max(x-1,0)` — which is *not* subterm-monotone but is perfectly valid as an algebra for a reduction pair, since reduction pairs (used in DP framework) need only be monotone and stable, not simplification orders. This lets `F4(f1(v0))` (value `v0+1`) exceed `F4(f3(f1(v0)))` (value `v0`) even though the underlying term `f3(f1(v0))` is syntactically bigger than `f1(v0)`.

3. **Modularity via SCC decomposition.** The dependency graph splits into independent SCCs, so each recursive cluster (`f0`'s unfolding, `f2`'s unfolding, `f3`'s unfolding, and `f4`'s self-call) can be handled by its own tailored interpretation, rather than needing one single global order compatible with all rules simultaneously. This is essential because rule 6 (`f3(f1(f1(v0))) -> f1(f3(f1(v0)))`) needs `f3` to behave in a subterm-increasing way (successor-like) at the rule level, while DP6 needs `f3` to behave in a subterm-decreasing way at the recursion-measure level — two seemingly conflicting demands that only the DP framework's separation of "usable rule constraints" (weak, non-strict) from "DP constraints" (strict) can reconcile: rule 6 only needs to hold *weakly* (`≥`) under the chosen algebra, while DP6 needs to hold *strictly* (`>`), and the predecessor-style interpretation satisfies both simultaneously (rule 6 becomes an equality, DP6 becomes a strict inequality).

In short: I diagnosed that the obstruction was local to one specific recursive step whose semantic behavior (an implicit "decrement") contradicts the syntactic subterm structure. Simplification orders cannot express "smaller in value but bigger as a term," but reduction pairs in the DP/usable-rules framework can, and the SCC decomposition lets me apply that non-standard interpretation only where needed without breaking the (easy, subterm-respecting) orientations required for the other rules.
