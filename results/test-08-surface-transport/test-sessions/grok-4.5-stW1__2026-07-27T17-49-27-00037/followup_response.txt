### Why this method

The problem statement imposed a strict boundary: termination had to be shown **from the presented equations and syntax alone**, with no imported structural axioms, no object-level assumptions beyond what the constructors and equations themselves give, and no strengthening of the definitions (extra equations, guards, measures, side-conditions, etc.).

Under that constraint the only well-founded relation that is immediately available is the subterm order on the free algebra generated by the constructors \(Z\) and \(S\).  That order is part of the syntax; it does not require an extra induction principle, a semantic size function, or any property that would have to be proved about the defined functions.  Consequently the most direct (and admissible) method is simply to inspect each equation and ask whether every recursive call is on an immediate subterm of the pattern being matched.  If the answer is yes for every equation, termination follows at once from well-foundedness of the subterm order.  If the answer is no for any equation, one must either

- exhibit another decreasing measure that can be justified solely by equational rewriting from the given equations, or  
- accept that termination is not established inside the permitted boundary.

No other standard technique (size-change termination with a semantic norm, well-founded induction on a custom relation, “predecessor decreases,” etc.) stays inside the boundary, because each of them silently relies on an extra fact—most commonly the lemma \(p\,(S\,n)=n\)—that is not an equational consequence of the displayed equations and must be obtained by an induction axiom on the naturals.  Importing that axiom would violate the problem’s explicit prohibition.  Hence the purely syntactic, subterm-based check is the only method that respects the stated constraints while still being a complete decision procedure for the fragment of definitions that are structurally recursive.
