Yes. The LPO proof complies with those requirements.

The selected **precedence and lexicographic statuses are proof machinery**, not additions to the rewrite system. They:

- do not add or alter rewrite rules;
- do not restrict when a rule may fire;
- introduce no guards or side conditions;
- assign no object-level meaning to the symbols;
- assume no equations such as associativity, commutativity, injectivity, or constructor properties.

The proof uses only:

1. The displayed function-symbol syntax and arities.
2. The standard setting of finite first-order terms and substitutions inherent in an ordinary first-order TRS.
3. A standard well-founded reduction ordering, LPO.
4. The syntactically visible facts that variables are subterms of the relevant terms and that \(v2\) is a proper subterm of \(f0(v2)\).

Choosing a precedence such as \(f5\succ f6\succ f2\succ\cdots\) does **not** assert an axiom like “\(f5\) is semantically greater than \(f6\).” It merely defines a metalevel measure used to prove that each permitted rewrite step decreases. Likewise, choosing lexicographic status does not impose an evaluation strategy or restrict rewriting.

The only ambient convention is that “first-order term rewriting system” means rewriting over **finite first-order terms**. That is part of the standard formal setting, not an imported object-level assumption. If infinitary terms or nonstandard rewriting semantics were intended, termination would be a different question, but nothing in the prompt indicates that setting.

Thus the method establishes termination for the TRS exactly as presented, without strengthening it.
