I picked **lexicographic path ordering (LPO)** because the rules have exactly the structure LPO handles well:

1. **Most rewrites descend in the root-symbol hierarchy.**
   - \(f5 \to c1\)
   - \(f5 \to f6\)
   - \(f6 \to f2\), with only lower-precedence symbols nested below \(f2\)

   A precedence such as \(f5 \succ f6 \succ f2 \succ \cdots\) directly captures that descent.

2. **The only recursive occurrence decreases lexicographically.**  
   In rule 2, the right side contains another \(f5\)-term:
   \[
   f5(v0,v1,f0(v2)) \quad\text{versus}\quad f5(v0,v1,v2).
   \]
   Their first two arguments are unchanged, while the third decreases because \(v2\) is a proper subterm of \(f0(v2)\). Lexicographic status therefore handles the recursive call immediately.

3. **LPO tolerates term growth and duplication.**  
   Rule 3 makes the term syntactically larger and duplicates \(v3\). A simple symbol-count or term-size measure would therefore fail. LPO can still orient the rule because the root changes from the higher-precedence \(f6\) to lower-precedence symbols, while the source dominates all required arguments.

4. **It stays entirely within the stated boundary.**  
   LPO is a standard syntactic simplification ordering. Its termination result relies only on finite first-order term syntax, the chosen precedence/status, and the displayed rules. It introduces no extra rewrite rules, guards, equations, typing assumptions, or interpretations of the function symbols.

Other methods could potentially work, but LPO is the most direct fit: it exposes the system’s essential pattern as **root-symbol descent plus a proper-subterm decrease in the sole recursive call**.
