Yes. The LPO proof complies with those requirements.

The method uses only:

- the given **finite first-order term syntax**;
- the syntactic proper-subterm relation;
- the three presented rewrite rules; and
- a standard, well-founded reduction ordering chosen at the **metalevel**.

The precedence

\[
\texttt{exec\_walk\_weight}\succ\texttt{add}\succ\texttt{app}\succ\texttt{cons}\succ\cdots
\]

and lexicographic status are not new object-level axioms or rewrite rules. They are parameters of the termination proof, used only to orient the existing rules.

In particular, the proof does **not** assume:

- that `nil` and `cons` denote mathematical lists;
- that every term is a well-formed list;
- that `cons` is injective or disjoint from `nil`;
- any length function or induction principle for lists;
- any meaning or algebraic properties for `add`, `app`, `zero`, or `w`;
- evaluation restrictions, typing assumptions, guards, or strategies.

The key decrease

\[
\texttt{cons}(y,xs)
\lhd
\texttt{cons}(x,\texttt{cons}(y,xs))
\]

is simply a proper-subterm fact visible in the raw syntax, not an imported structural axiom about lists. LPO’s standard closure under substitutions and contexts then handles arbitrary instances and rewriting at arbitrary positions, including substitutions where `w`, `x`, `y`, or `xs` are unusual or very large terms.

Thus the proof establishes termination of the unrestricted first-order rewrite relation generated by exactly those three rules, without strengthening the system or relying on hidden object-level assumptions.
