I chose **lexicographic path ordering (LPO)** because the rules have exactly the shape it handles well:

1. **The recursive call is structurally smaller.**  
   In the third rule,
   \[
   \texttt{cons}(y,xs)
   \]
   is a proper subterm of
   \[
   \texttt{cons}(x,\texttt{cons}(y,xs)).
   \]
   With lexicographic comparison of the arguments of `exec_walk_weight`, LPO directly recognizes that decrease.

2. **The recursive result is wrapped in other symbols.**  
   The right-hand side is headed by `add`, and also contains nested `app` terms. Giving `exec_walk_weight` higher precedence than `add` and `app` lets LPO prove that the whole left-hand side dominates those constructed terms.

3. **The rule duplicates `w`.**  
   A basic term-size argument is unsuitable because
   \[
   \texttt{app}(\texttt{app}(w,x),y)
   \]
   and the recursive call both contain `w`; under substitution, `w` may be arbitrarily large. LPO is designed to tolerate such duplication when the recursive structure still decreases.

4. **It is purely syntactic.**  
   LPO does not require interpreting `cons` as a genuine list constructor, `add` as arithmetic addition, or `app` as application with any semantic properties. The symbol precedence and lexicographic status are components of the proof ordering, not added object-level axioms or rewrite rules.

5. **It automatically covers rewriting under contexts and substitutions.**  
   Once every rule is strictly decreasing under LPO, standard properties of the ordering establish termination of the entire first-order rewrite relation—not merely root-level evaluation on well-formed lists.

A dependency-pair proof or a carefully designed interpretation could also work, but LPO is the most direct method here: it captures both the proper-subterm recursive descent and the lower-precedence wrappers in one standard reduction ordering.
