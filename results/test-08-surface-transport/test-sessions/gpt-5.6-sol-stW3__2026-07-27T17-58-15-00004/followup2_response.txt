**Yes.** The dependency-pair proof complies with those requirements.

The proof uses external mathematics only at the **meta-level**—the standard dependency-pair theorem, the proper-subterm relation, and a well-founded interpretation over \(\mathbb N\). Those are termination-proof tools, not object-level axioms added to the rewrite system.

In particular, the proof does **not**:

- add any rewrite rule;
- assume equations such as associativity, injectivity, or constructor freeness;
- add a guard or side condition to any rule;
- assume that `f3` is literally a predecessor operation under rewriting;
- assume that terms denote natural numbers.

The assignment
\[
[c0]=0,\qquad [f1(x)]=x+1,\qquad [f3(x)]=\max(x-1,0)
\]
is merely a chosen numerical measure. Its validity is established by checking the relevant given rules. It does not assert an additional equation like
\[
f3(f1(x))=x,
\]
which would indeed be an impermissible object-level assumption.

Likewise, “usable rules” do not impose guards on rewriting. They are part of a standard dependency-pair theorem identifying which existing rules can occur between calls in a particular dependency-pair component.

All rules are accounted for:

- The recursive `f0`, `f2`, and `f3` rules generate strictly descending dependency pairs.
- The `f4` rule generates the only non-syntactically-obvious recursive call, handled by the checked numerical interpretation.
- The remaining rules,
  \[
  f0(x,c0)\to x,\quad
  f2(c0,x)\to c0,\quad
  f2(x,c0)\to c0,\quad
  f3(f1(c0))\to c0,
  \]
  create no recursive dependency pairs. They are not being ignored: the dependency-pair theorem guarantees that any infinite reduction in a finite first-order TRS would induce an infinite dependency-pair chain. These rules cannot sustain such a chain.

One clarification to my earlier wording: saying the `f3` rules “justify viewing `f3` as decreasing an `f1`-height” should be understood only as describing the verified **meta-level measure**, not as claiming that predecessor behavior is derivable as an object-level equation.

Thus, under the ordinary definition of a first-order TRS over finite terms—which is inherent in the problem statement rather than an added axiom—the method stays within the stated boundary.
