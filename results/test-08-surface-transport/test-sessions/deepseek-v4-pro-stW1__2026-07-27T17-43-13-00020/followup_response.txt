The method I chose — the **dependency pair framework with a polynomial interpretation** — was selected because it meets all the constraints in the prompt without overstepping them, and it directly handles the one feature of the given functions that defeats simpler purely syntactic techniques.

### The core difficulty: recursive calls with defined symbols
Look at rules like
```text
times (S x) y → plus (times x y) y
fac (S x)   → times (fac (p (S x))) (S x)
```
In `times`, the recursive argument `x` is a **subterm of the left-hand side** (`S x`) — that’s fine for a structural ordering. But in `fac`, the recursive call is `fac (p (S x))`. The argument `p (S x)` is not a subterm of `S x`; it’s a term built from the *defined* symbol `p`. A purely syntactic termination order (like the subterm relation or a lexicographic path order) cannot prove its decrease without first “knowing” that `p (S x)` evaluates to something smaller — and that knowledge would require extra “structural” axioms or a semantics.

### Why dependency pairs + polynomial interpretation fits the brief
1. **No extra axioms needed** – The dependency pair method works directly with the given equations treated as rewrite rules. It extracts every recursive call as a separate “dependency pair” constraint (e.g., `FAC(S x) → FAC(p(S x))`). Proving termination then reduces to showing that each of these sharp pairs decreases in some well-founded order that is *compatible* with the original rules.

2. **We can use a *semantic* well-founded order derived solely from the term syntax** – I chose an interpretation into ℕ with the usual `>` order. This interpretation is built from the constructors `Z` and `S` (interpreted as 0 and successor) and simple arithmetic on them — no imported object-level axioms about naturals, no induction principle, no hidden side conditions. The only thing required is the existence of a well-founded set ℕ, which is a standard “proof method” and not an external assumption about the data type.

3. **Handles nested defined symbols gracefully** – Under the interpretation, `p(S x)` is mapped to `max(0, x)`, and `fac` is mapped to factorial. The strict decrease `FAC(S x) > FAC(p(S x))` becomes `(x+3)! > (max(0,x)+2)!`, a purely arithmetic fact that holds without any added equations. Similarly, all other dependency pairs (like `TIMES(S x, y) > TIMES(x, y)`) follow from the interpretation. Because the interpretation is strictly monotone and the dependency pairs all strictly decrease while the rules are oriented as `≥`, we get termination.

4. **Standard and self-contained** – The dependency pair framework is a well-established technique for proving termination of rewrite systems, and polynomial interpretations are its most common instantiation. No strengthening of the defining equations is required — we don’t add new rules, guards, or lemmas; we only demonstrate that the existing rules and recursion are well-founded under a chosen measure.

In short, the choice was driven by necessity: the `fac` rule forces us to go beyond structural recursion, and the dependency pair method with a natural semantic interpretation is the minimal, cleanest way to obtain a solid termination argument while staying exactly within the “equations alone” constraint.
