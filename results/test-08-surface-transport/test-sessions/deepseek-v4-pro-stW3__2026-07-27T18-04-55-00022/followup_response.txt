I chose the dependency pair framework with a polynomial interpretation because the system mixes straightforward structural recursion (in f0, f2) with a more intricate recursive call in f4:

```
f4(f1(v0)) -> f2(f4(f3(f1(v0))), f1(v0))
```

Here the recursive argument is `f3(f1(v0))`, not a direct syntactic subterm of `f1(v0)`. Simple syntactic methods (e.g., recursive path orders) would typically require `f3` to reduce its argument immediately and expose a subterm, or they would need a complex precedence and status that may not be obvious. The dependency pair method separates the recursive calls into standalone inequalities, and then a **polynomial interpretation over ℕ** (using factorial for f4, addition/multiplication for others) gives a clean numeric decrease for every dependency pair, even for the non‑structural recursion — `f3(f1(v0))` numerically becomes `max(0,v0)` which is smaller than `v0+1`, making the factorial product drop sharply.

This approach is:

- **Self‑contained:** It uses only the given rewrite rules, no external axioms.
- **Mechanically checkable:** DPs are extracted algorithmically, and the polynomial interpretation is a standard reduction pair.
- **Strong enough for this system:** It handles all rules and dependency pairs without needing complex orderings or tricky rule permutations.

In short, the system’s non‑obvious recursion demanded a method that can reason about numeric size decreases rather than pure syntactic subterm relations, and the dependency pair framework provides exactly that separation.
