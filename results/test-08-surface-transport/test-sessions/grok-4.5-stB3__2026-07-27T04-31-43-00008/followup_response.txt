### Why polynomial interpretation?

The TRS is small, unconditional, and first-order, with a clear recursive structure on the second argument of `f3` (the two base cases collapse to a constant; the recursive case peels one `f0` constructor and makes a single recursive call on a proper subterm of that argument while only building a linear combination of the remaining variables with `f1`/`f2`).  

Polynomial interpretations are a natural fit for exactly this shape:
- they are elementary to write down and to check by hand (only arithmetic on \(\mathbb{N}\));
- weak monotonicity is immediate from non-negative coefficients;
- a multiplicative factor \((x+1)\cdot y\) for `f3` directly encodes “the measure is proportional to the size of the second argument,” which makes the recursive call strictly smaller while the base cases become trivial;
- the same interpretation simultaneously orients the two non-recursive rules without any extra constants, precedences, or status flags.

Other standard methods (RPO/LPO with a suitable precedence and lexicographic status, or KBO with appropriate weights) can also succeed, but they require additional trial-and-error choices (precedence, status, weight function) that are unnecessary once a simple polynomial solution is already visible. Matrix interpretations or dependency-pair processors would work as well, yet they are heavier than needed for a three-rule system that already admits a direct, fully expansive decrease into a well-founded domain.  

Thus the polynomial order is the lightest method that stays strictly inside the given rules, needs no hidden axioms, and yields an immediately verifiable termination proof.
