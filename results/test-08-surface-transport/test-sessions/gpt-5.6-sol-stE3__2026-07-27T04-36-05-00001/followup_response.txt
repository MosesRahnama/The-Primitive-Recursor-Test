I chose **lexicographic path ordering (LPO)** because the rules have exactly the structural pattern LPO handles well:

1. **Most rules replace a defined symbol by lower-precedence symbols.**
   - Rule 1 replaces an \(f5\)-term by \(c1\).
   - Rule 3 replaces an \(f6\)-term by a term built from \(f2,f1,f4,f3\).

   These are oriented directly by choosing \(f5\) and \(f6\) above the symbols appearing on their right-hand sides.

2. **Rule 2 contains a recursive \(f5\)-call, but on a syntactically smaller argument.**
   \[
   f5(v0,v1,f0(v2))\to
   f6(v0,v1,v2,f5(v0,v1,v2)).
   \]
   The recursive call changes the third argument from \(f0(v2)\) to its proper subterm \(v2\). Lexicographic status for \(f5\) captures this immediately:
   \[
   f5(v0,v1,f0(v2))>f5(v0,v1,v2).
   \]

3. **LPO handles the nested right-hand sides compositionally.**
   Once the left side is greater than every argument of a lower-precedence root symbol, it is greater than the whole term. This is especially convenient for the deeply nested right side of rule 3.

4. **Variable duplication makes a simple size argument unattractive.**
   In rule 2, \(v0,v1,v2\) occur both as direct arguments of \(f6\) and inside the recursive \(f5\)-call. Consequently, ordinary symbol count or term size need not decrease after substitution. LPO is stable under arbitrary substitutions and is not defeated by that duplication.

5. **It stays entirely within the given syntax.**
   LPO is a standard well-founded reduction order on finite first-order terms. It requires no interpretation of the symbols, equations about them, typing assumptions, guards, or extra rewrite rules.

So the choice was driven by the system’s visible structure: **precedence decrease between different root symbols, plus lexicographic decrease in the third argument of the sole recursive \(f5\)-call**. LPO packages both forms of decrease into one substitution-stable termination proof.
