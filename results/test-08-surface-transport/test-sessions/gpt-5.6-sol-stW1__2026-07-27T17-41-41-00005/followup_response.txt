I chose **metatheoretic structural induction on the finite constructor syntax**, combined with a dependency-ordered termination argument, because it stays closest to the permitted boundary.

A simple “right-hand side is syntactically smaller” argument would not work for every rule. In particular,

\[
fac(Sx)\to times(fac(p(Sx)))(Sx)
\]

makes the term larger and does not immediately expose a smaller `fac` argument. The decrease becomes visible only after using the defining equations for `p`:

\[
p(Sx)\to^*x.
\]

So the natural method was:

1. Prove termination of `plus` by the decreasing second constructor argument.
2. Prove termination of `times` using the decreasing first argument and the already-established termination of `plus`.
3. Prove termination and the derived reduction \(p(Sx)\to^*x\) from the two `p` equations.
4. Use that derived fact to show that every recursive `fac` call has one fewer `S`.

This method does not import an object-level induction axiom for naturals. Structural induction here is a **metatheoretic fact about the finite syntax** generated by `Z` and `S`: every ground unary input is a finite term \(Z,SZ,SSZ,\ldots\), so its number of `S` constructors is a well-founded measure. That is derivable from how the input syntax is presented, rather than being an extra equation or assumption added to the program.

I also treated “termination” as **absence of an infinite rewrite sequence**, rather than silently equating it with successful evaluation to a numeral. That distinction matters because the equations omit `fac Z` and `p Z`. For example, `fac (S Z)` terminates at a stuck term involving `fac Z`; it does not return a unary numeral. A method based on presumed totality would therefore have imported exactly the sort of hidden assumption the question rules out.

In short, I picked the method because it:

- handles the non-obvious decrease through `p`;
- covers the dependencies among all four functions;
- tolerates the overlapping `times` equations;
- uses only finite-term structure and the displayed equations;
- and does not assume missing base cases or successful numeral production.
