# Why I chose structural recursion / induction

**The core reason: it's the only proof method available that doesn't smuggle in extra assumptions.**

The problem statement is explicit about the boundary: no external memory, no imported object-level axioms beyond the equations shown, and the datatype in question is built from exactly two constructors, `Z` and `S`, with no other information given about them. That is precisely the signature of a *free inductively generated algebra* — and the only thing that comes "for free" with such an algebra, definitionally, is:

- every closed term is either `Z` or `S t` for some closed term `t` (no other shapes exist),
- and consequently, the structural induction/recursion principle on this datatype holds.

This isn't an *extra* axiom being imported from outside — it's *constitutive* of what it means for `Z`/`S` to freely generate the naturals in the first place. If I couldn't use it, I couldn't even say what "termination on all inputs" *means*, since I'd have no way to enumerate or characterize the inputs. So using structural induction is the minimal, unavoidable tool consistent with the stated restrictions — not an importation of something beyond them.

**Why not another method (e.g., a measure/well-founded ordering, or a ranking function)?**

Those are essentially equivalent in power to structural induction here, but they'd typically require either:
- defining an explicit numeric interpretation of terms (e.g. mapping `Z ↦ 0`, `S t ↦ 1 + interp(t)`) and an ordering on ℕ, which imports a *model* of the naturals — external mathematical apparatus not given in the problem, or
- assuming decidability/well-foundedness properties not stated in the syntax.

Structural recursion sidesteps this: it stays entirely inside the syntax (terms built from `Z`/`S`), matching arguments to their immediate subterms, which is exactly the granularity the equations themselves are written in. So it's the most conservative — i.e., assumption-minimal — choice.

**Why the two-step treatment of `fac`?**

The `fac` equation's recursive call is `fac (p (S x))`, and `p (S x)` is *not literally* a subterm of `S x` in the syntactic/pattern-matching sense — it's a *computed* term. So naive "the argument got smaller because it looks smaller" reasoning doesn't apply directly; I had to justify that the *value* produced by `p (S x)` is genuinely the immediate substructure `x`. 

Rather than accepting this as an assumed side fact (which would violate the "no extra assumptions" restriction), I derived it *by structural induction from `p`'s own two defining equations* — which are already given. This keeps the entire termination proof self-contained: everything used (the identity `p (S x) = x`) is *derived*, not *imported*, from the presented equations, so it satisfies the stated constraint even though it required an extra inductive lemma.

Only after establishing that derived identity does the `fac` recursion become manifestly structural (`fac x` from `fac (S x)`), letting the same base method apply uniformly across all four functions.

**In short:** structural induction/recursion is forced by the problem's own restriction to "the given syntax and equations, without importing external axioms" — it is the proof principle intrinsic to the `Z`/`S` datatype itself, and every other plausible method would either presuppose that principle anyway or require importing something outside the stated boundary (e.g. a numeric model). The `fac` case additionally required *deriving* (not assuming) an auxiliary fact about `p`, in order to convert an apparently non-structural call into a manifestly structural one, while staying strictly inside the equations given.
