I chose the **Lexicographic Path Ordering (LPO)** because the syntactic structure of your rules perfectly aligns with the specific strengths of LPO, while immediately ruling out or complicating other standard termination methods. 

Here is the exact breakdown of why LPO was the most direct, elegant, and robust choice for this specific system:

### 1. It handles structural recursion perfectly (Rule 2)
Look at the recursive call in the second rule:
`f5(v0, v1, f0(v2)) -> f6(v0, v1, v2, f5(v0, v1, v2))`

To prove this terminates, we need a mathematical way to say "the inner call to `f5` is strictly smaller than the outer call." LPO is designed to compare terms lexicographically (left-to-right). When it compares `f5(v0, v1, f0(v2))` with `f5(v0, v1, v2)`, it sees:
*   Argument 1: `v0` equals `v0`
*   Argument 2: `v1` equals `v1`
*   Argument 3: `f0(v2)` is strictly larger than `v2` (by the subterm property).

Because it finds a strict decrease before running out of arguments, LPO guarantees termination for this recursive step natively, without requiring any complex arithmetic mapping.

### 2. It ignores variable duplication (Unlike KBO)
Notice that Rule 2 duplicates variables: `v0`, `v1`, and `v2` appear *once* on the left side, but *twice* on the right side (once inside `f6`, once inside `f5`). 

This immediately makes weight-based methods like the **Knuth-Bendix Ordering (KBO)** very difficult to apply. KBO requires the total "weight" (symbol count) of variables on the right to be less than or equal to the left. To make KBO work with duplication, you have to assign a weight of zero to variables, which forces very strict restrictions on the rest of the system. LPO is purely based on a hierarchy of symbols, so it completely ignores how many times a smaller subterm is duplicated.

### 3. It handles deep "macro expansions" effortlessly (Rule 3)
Look at the third rule:
`f6(v0,v1,v2,v3) -> f2(v3,f1(f4(v0,v3,f3(v1,v2)),c1))`

This rule doesn't loop; it just replaces `f6` with a massive, nested tree of other symbols (`f2`, `f1`, `f4`, `f3`, `c1`). LPO handles this type of rewrite trivially through **precedence**. By simply declaring that `f6` sits higher in the hierarchy than the new symbols, LPO allows a single "heavy" symbol to be replaced by any number of "lighter" symbols, no matter how deeply nested they are, provided no new variables are introduced.

### 4. It avoids the math of Polynomial Interpretations
While we could theoretically map this system to polynomials over natural numbers, finding the right coefficients would be tedious. Because Rule 2 duplicates variables and nests a recursive call inside another function (`f6`), the polynomial for `f5` would have to grow exponentially to cover the right-hand side. LPO keeps the proof entirely in the realm of syntax, avoiding arithmetic altogether.

### 5. It satisfies your strict constraints
Your prompt explicitly demanded no imported object-level axioms, no external memory, and reliance only on the presented syntax. LPO is a purely syntactic simplification ordering. It requires absolutely nothing but the rules themselves and a declared ranking of the symbols, making it perfectly tailored to your boundary conditions.
