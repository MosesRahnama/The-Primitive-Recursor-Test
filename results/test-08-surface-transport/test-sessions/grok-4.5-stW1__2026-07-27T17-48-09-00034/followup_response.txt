### Why this method

The problem imposed a hard boundary: decide totality **solely** from the eight equations + the free syntax of unary naturals (constructors \(Z\), \(S\)), with no imported structural induction principles, no well-founded-order axioms, no extra equations, no guards, and no hidden domain restrictions.  

Under that constraint the most direct and self-contained method is simply to read the equations as a first-order rewrite system and ask two purely syntactic questions for every function:

1. **Coverage** – does every ground constructor pattern appear on the left-hand side of some equation?  
2. **Decrease** – when a recursive call occurs, is its argument a proper subterm of the original argument (so the call graph is acyclic on ground terms)?

- If both answers are “yes” for a function, every ground term reduces in finitely many steps to a numeral; the equations themselves already supply the termination argument.  
- If any ground pattern is missing, the corresponding term is irreducible yet not a numeral; that single stuck term is already a counter-example to totality, again read off directly from the given syntax.

This check is exhaustive (it inspects every equation exactly once), uses nothing beyond the symbols that appear in the prompt, and needs no external lemmas. More sophisticated techniques (size-change termination, dependency pairs, interpretation into \(\mathbb{N}\), etc.) either import extra mathematical machinery or silently strengthen the definition—both forbidden by the problem statement. The elementary coverage+decrease inspection is therefore the strongest method that still respects the stated boundary, and it is decisive: it proves totality for `plus` and `times` and simultaneously exhibits the stuck terms that refute totality for `p` and `fac`.
