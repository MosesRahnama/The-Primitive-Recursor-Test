I chose the dependency‑pair method with a natural‑numbers algebra because it is a lightweight, self‑contained technique that fits the system’s structure perfectly.

**Why dependency pairs?**  
The system has defined symbols (`f0`, `f2`, `f3`, `f4`) that nest recursive calls inside constructor contexts (e.g., `f2(f1(v0),v1) → f0(f2(v0,v1),v1)`). Dependency pairs isolate just the recursive calls (the “dependency graph”) from the surrounding rewrite context. This lets me prove termination by showing that each recursive SCC strictly decreases on some well‑founded order, independent of the original rule’s right‑hand side context. No extra lemmas or structural induction on terms are needed.

**Why a simple ℕ‑algebra?**  
All recursion patterns are structural: `f0` recurses on its second argument; `f2` on its first; `f3` strips a constructor; `f4` calls itself on a smaller argument (`f3(f1(v0))` is strictly smaller than `f1(v0)` under a suitable size measure). I could therefore map every function directly to monotone arithmetic functions on ℕ (addition, multiplication, factorial, max) that satisfy all original rules weakly (≥) and make the dependency pairs strictly decrease (>) where required. This is a clean, direct interpretation that avoids the complexity of polynomial orders, path orderings, or termination provers that might import external reasoning. Since ℕ’s natural order is well‑founded and all functions are clearly monotone, the proof is fully contained and transparent.
