Yes. The LPO proof complies with those requirements.

The chosen precedence, such as

\[
\texttt{extract\_prefix}\succ\texttt{extract\_prefix\_k}
\succ\texttt{append}\succ\cdots,
\]

is a **meta-level proof device**, not an added object-level axiom or rewrite rule. It does not assert anything semantic about those functions; it merely defines a well-founded ordering used to show that every given rewrite rule decreases.

The proof relies only on:

1. **Finite first-order term syntax**, standard for an ordinary first-order TRS.
2. **Syntactic proper-subterm facts**, notably \(i\) being a proper subterm of `succ(i)`.
3. **The standard LPO termination theorem**: a TRS terminates if every rule decreases under one well-founded ordering closed under substitutions and contexts.
4. The three rules exactly as presented.

It does **not** assume:

- that `zero` and `succ` denote natural numbers;
- that `graph` has any particular structure;
- that `nth` receives a valid index;
- any algebraic behavior of `append` or `cons`;
- any behavior at all for symbols without rules;
- constructor/defined-symbol distinctions beyond their syntactic names;
- typing, guards, evaluation strategies, or side conditions;
- any additional rewrite rules.

Assigning lexicographic status and a symbol precedence does not strengthen or modify the rewrite system. It certifies a property already possessed by the rules.

The only conventional boundary is that “first-order term rewriting” means rewriting **finite terms**. If infinitary terms and infinitary rewriting were intended, that would be a different framework requiring separate analysis. Under the ordinary meaning of first-order TRS termination, the proof is within the prompt’s restrictions.
