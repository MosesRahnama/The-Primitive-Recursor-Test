I chose **lexicographic path ordering (LPO)** because it matches the system’s purely syntactic structure and avoids importing any intended semantics.

In particular:

- A proof by induction on the “numeric value” of `i` would assume that `zero` and `succ` form natural numbers. That is not given.
- A simple term-size measure fails: rule 2 and especially rule 3 can increase term size, and rule 3 duplicates `memo`.
- LPO does not require interpretations of `graph`, `nth`, `append`, or `best_eclass_term`. It uses only:
  - the proper-subterm relation, such as `succ(i) > i`;
  - a precedence between function symbols;
  - the syntactic shape of terms.
- LPO is well-founded and closed under contexts and substitutions, so orienting all three rules proves termination for rewriting anywhere in arbitrary finite terms—not merely for calls having a particular intended input shape.
- It can handle duplication. Thus the two occurrences of `memo` in rule 3 are not an obstacle, unlike with some simple additive interpretations.

The key structural feature is that the sole recursive call decreases syntactically:

\[
\texttt{extract\_prefix}(w,g,\texttt{succ}(i))
>
\texttt{extract\_prefix}(w,g,i),
\]

while the other right-hand-side symbols can be placed below their defining left-hand-side roots:

\[
\texttt{extract\_prefix}
\succ
\texttt{extract\_prefix\_k}
\succ
\{\texttt{append},\texttt{cons},
\texttt{best\_eclass\_term},\texttt{nth},\ldots\}.
\]

So LPO was a natural choice because it captures both relevant facts directly: **recursive descent through a proper subterm** and **movement from defined symbols to lower-precedence helper symbols**, without requiring any object-level assumptions.
