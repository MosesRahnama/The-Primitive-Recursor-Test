I chose the approach in two stages, each for a specific reason:

**1. LPO for rules 1–7:** These rules are textbook structural recursion — every recursive call in the RHS has its "active" argument as a literal subterm (or immediately subterm-comparable) of the LHS. That's exactly the pattern LPO/simplification orders are designed for: it's the cheapest, most mechanical method available, it requires no creativity or auxiliary lemmas, and it directly matches the subterm-property structure visible in rules 1–5 (f0/f2, a standard add/multiply pattern) and rules 6–7 (f3, a standard predecessor-peeling pattern). Since a single precedence (f2 > f0 > f3 > f1 > c0) orients all of them simultaneously, there was no reason to reach for anything heavier for this block.

**2. Why I abandoned LPO for rule 8 and switched methods:** I checked rule 8 against the same order and found a structural obstruction, not just a precedence issue: the recursive call is `f4(f3(f1(v0)))`, and `f3(f1(v0))` *properly contains* `f1(v0)`. Any simplification order (LPO, MPO, KBO, polynomial orders with the subterm property) is forced to rank `f3(f1(v0)) > f1(v0)`, which is the wrong direction for proving the outer f4-recursion decreases. This isn't a fixable precedence choice — it's inherent to the shape of the rule (the recursive argument is *built up* via f3 before being fed back into f4). So no single well-founded order compatible with the subterm property can orient this rule on its own; this is a known obstruction pattern (it resembles the classic reason "dependency pairs" or semantic termination methods exist).

Given that syntactic/simplification orders are provably insufficient here, the next standard move (without inventing new rules or side conditions) is to argue **semantically/modularly**: use the fact that I'd *already* proven termination of the f3-subsystem (rules 6–7) to bound how many times the trigger condition for rule 8 (an f1-headed argument) can keep recurring. This is a standard technique — reduce termination of a "hard" rule to termination of an already-established, more elementary subsystem, rather than trying to force a single global order over the whole signature. I picked it specifically because:

- It reuses Step 1–2 results directly, keeping the proof modular and each piece as simple as possible.
- It doesn't require introducing new axioms, guards, or restrictions — it only uses well-foundedness facts already derived from the given rules.
- It matches the actual reason rule 8 terminates: not a syntactic subterm decrease, but the exhaustion of a finite process (f3 peeling f1-layers) that is itself independently well-founded.

So the overall strategy — simplification order where the syntax supports it, semantic/modular argument where it structurally cannot — was chosen to match the proof technique to the actual shape of the obstruction in each rule, rather than forcing one uniform (and in rule 8's case, provably inadequate) method across the whole system.
