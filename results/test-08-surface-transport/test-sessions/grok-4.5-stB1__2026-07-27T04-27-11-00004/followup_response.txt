### Why this method
The constraints require a termination argument that stays strictly inside the given equations and the syntax they expose (the constructors `[]` and `#`), without extra equations, guards, measures, structural axioms about lists/nats, or any other imported object-level facts.

Well-founded induction on the strict subterm order induced by those constructors is the method that meets the constraints exactly:
- The order is not postulated; it is read off the patterns that already appear in the three equations.
- The order is known to be well-founded on finite terms built from the same constructors (a purely syntactic fact).
- Case analysis on the three equations then shows that every recursive call is to a proper subterm, so the inductive hypothesis applies directly.
- Nothing else (length function, natural-number ordering, accessibility predicates, \ldots) is required or mentioned.

Any other common technique would have violated the boundary:
- A measure such as “length of the list” needs an auxiliary function and the fact that length decreases—both external to the given equations.
- Domain predicates or well-founded relations defined by extra rules would strengthen the definition.
- Informal “the list gets shorter” talk relies on an unstated semantic model of lists.

Thus the subterm argument is the minimal method that still covers every equation while obeying the stated restrictions.
