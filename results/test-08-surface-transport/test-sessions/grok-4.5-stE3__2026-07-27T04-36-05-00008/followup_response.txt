### Why LPO?

I chose the lexicographic path order (LPO) for three concrete reasons that fit this exact system and the constraints you set:

1. **Direct fit to the rule shape**  
   The three rules have a transparent “outermost constructor decreases, then recurse on a structurally smaller argument” pattern:
   - \(f_5\) either disappears entirely (first rule) or is replaced by the strictly smaller symbol \(f_6\) while the recursive call occurs on the immediate subterm of an \(f_0\)-argument (second rule);
   - \(f_6\) is replaced by a term built only from the still-smaller symbols \(f_2,f_1,f_4,f_3\).  
   LPO is purpose-built for exactly this situation: a single precedence on the function symbols plus the subterm property orients every rule at once. No coefficients, no matrices, no dependency pairs have to be invented.

2. **Zero extra machinery**  
   You required a proof that stays inside the given syntax and rewrite rules. LPO is a pure simplification order; once the precedence
   \[
   f_5>f_6>f_2>f_1>f_4>f_3>f_0>c_1>c_0
   \]
   is written down, the orientation checks are purely syntactic and use nothing beyond the inductive definition of LPO. No guards, no sorts, no equational theory, no semantic interpretation of the constants is required.

3. **Economy and readability**  
   For a three-rule system the LPO argument is only a few lines long and can be verified by hand in seconds. A polynomial interpretation (e.g., \(f_5\mapsto x+y+2z+1\), \(f_6\mapsto x+y+z+w+2\), \ldots) also works, but forces the reader to check arithmetic inequalities; Knuth–Bendix orders or dependency-pair processors are heavier still. LPO gives the shortest self-contained certificate that still covers every rule.

In short, LPO is the lightest standard well-founded order that matches the call hierarchy already present in the rules, satisfies the “nothing extra” constraint, and yields an immediately checkable proof.
