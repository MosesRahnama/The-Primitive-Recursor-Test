I chose the lexicographic product order on \(\mathbb{N}\times\mathbb{N}\) because the system’s rules exhibit two distinct, overlapping “kinds” of decrease that cannot be expressed easily with a single numeric measure, but can be separated and prioritized lexicographically.

1. **Mixed behavior across rules**  
   - Some rules reduce structural size in a simple way (e.g., \(f_0(v_0,c_0)\to v_0\), \(f_2(c_0,v_0)\to c_0\)).  
   - Others perform a kind of distribution that increases count of constructors but decreases a “weight” (e.g., \(f_0(v_0,f_1(v_1))\to f_1(f_0(v_0,v_1))\) adds an extra \(f_1\) outside).  
   - The \(f_3\) rules resemble a decrementing counter:  
     \(f_3(f_1(f_1(v_0)))\to f_1(f_3(f_1(v_0)))\) decreases the number of outer \(f_1\)’s under \(f_3\), yet increases the total term size.  
   - The \(f_4\) rule is a complex recursive scheme that requires a fast-growing rank to guarantee termination.

   A single natural-number measure (like a polynomial interpretation over \(\mathbb{N}\)) would struggle to capture both the distribution (which often increases total size) and the genuine recursive decrease that guarantees termination. Lexicographic ordering allows us to separate these concerns: the first component reflects the “recursive size” (number of nested constructor-like symbols, a kind of termination height), and the second component reflects a secondary weight (like the total number of \(f_1\)’s) that can grow safely once the primary size has decreased.

2. **Natural modelling of numerals**  
   The system uses \(c_0\) and \(f_1\) in a way that strongly suggests a Peano-like representation of natural numbers (\(c_0 = 0\), \(f_1 = \text{successor}\)). The rules for \(f_3\) and \(f_0\) manipulate these numbers. A lexicographic pair \((a,b)\) can directly model a numeric value \(a\) (the number of \(f_1\)’s, roughly) plus an extra weight \(b\). Because the lexicographic order compares the numeric part first, it can ignore increases in the second component when the “counter” decreases – exactly what happens in rules like \(f_3(f_1(f_1(v)))\to f_1(f_3(f_1(v)))\).

3. **Handling the \(f_4\) rule**  
   The rule  
   \[
   f_4(f_1(v_0)) \to f_2(f_4(f_3(f_1(v_0))),f_1(v_0))
   \]
   reduces the argument of \(f_4\) from \(f_1(v_0)\) to \(f_3(f_1(v_0))\), which is a “smaller number” according to the semantics of \(f_3\). That decrease is captured by a numeric rank function (the fast-growing \(A\) in the interpretation). Placing that rank in the first component guarantees the left-hand side is larger, irrespective of any enormous growth in the second component caused by the surrounding \(f_2\). A single-component polynomial would need an extremely high degree, whereas a lexicographic pair cleanly separates the rank from the rest.

In summary, the lexicographic order naturally accommodates the system’s distinct decrease patterns—structural/numeric size vs. secondary weight—without needing to force everything into a single numeric measure. That’s why it was the method of choice.
