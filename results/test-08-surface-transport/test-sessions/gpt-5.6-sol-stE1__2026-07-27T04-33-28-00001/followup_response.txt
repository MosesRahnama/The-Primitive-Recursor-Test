I chose **structural induction / a decreasing natural-number measure** because the recursive definition directly follows the constructors of its third argument:

- `0` makes no recursive call.
- `Suc i` makes exactly one recursive call at `i`.

Thus the third argument itself supplies an explicit well-founded measure:  
\[
\mu(w,G,n)=n,
\]
and in the recursive equation,
\[
\mu(w,G,i)=i < \operatorname{Suc}(i)=\mu(w,G,\operatorname{Suc}(i)).
\]

This method is preferable here because it:

1. **Matches the equations exactly.** Both the `0` and `Suc` cases correspond directly to natural-number induction.
2. **Requires no semantic facts about the other arguments.** Neither `w`, `G`, nor `memo` affects the recursive descent.
3. **Uses only the given totality of the helper.** Once the recursive call returns, `best_eclass_term` cannot introduce nontermination.
4. **Avoids extra invariants or assumptions.** There is no need to assume that `memo` has a particular length, that `i` is in bounds, or that `best_eclass_term` decreases anything.
5. **Covers all recursive calls.** There is only one, and its measure strictly decreases.

A more elaborate method—such as lexicographic measures, size-change termination, or a custom well-founded relation—would prove the same fact but add machinery unsupported by any complexity in the definition. Structural recursion is the narrowest proof method justified by the presented equations.
