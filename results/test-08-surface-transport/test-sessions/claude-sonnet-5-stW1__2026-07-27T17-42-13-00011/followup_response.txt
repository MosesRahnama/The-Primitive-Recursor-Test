# Why I chose structural induction as the method

**The core constraint of the problem** was: use only what's derivable from the given syntax and equations, no imported structural axioms or hidden assumptions. That ruling-out clause pointed directly at *which* proof method was even admissible, so let me explain the reasoning behind each choice.

## Why structural induction on the argument, specifically

The data type here — built from `Z` and `S` — only supports one native notion of "getting smaller": the subterm relation induced by the constructors. There is no external well-founded order, no explicit measure function, no fuel/clock argument given anywhere in the problem. So any termination argument had to be grounded in something *intrinsic* to the syntax itself, rather than something I supply from outside.

Structural induction (equivalently, well-founded recursion on the proper-subterm order for `Z`/`S`-terms) is exactly the induction principle that comes *for free* with an inductively defined datatype — it's not an "extra axiom," it's the defining property of what it means for `Z`/`S` to generate the naturals inductively. This made it the only method consistent with the "no imported axioms" restriction.

## Why I checked each equation against that criterion

For each function, I went equation-by-equation and asked: *does the recursive call's argument sit strictly inside the calling argument, as a syntactic subterm?*

- `plus`: yes, trivially (`y` inside `S y`).
- `p`: yes, trivially (`S x` inside `S (S x)`).
- `times`: yes, on the first argument (`x` inside `S x`), while noting the equations already split all constructor cases so nothing is left undefined.
- `fac`: this is where it got interesting — `p (S x)` is *not* literally a subterm of `S x`. A naive subterm check fails.

## Why I introduced the derived lemma for `fac`

Since the plain subterm check failed for `fac`, I had two options: either declare termination not derivable, or see whether the *existing* equations (for `p`) already force `p (S x)` to reduce to `x`. I chose to check the latter first, because giving up prematurely would ignore information already present in the problem — and the instructions permitted using "any standard proof method" as long as I didn't strengthen the definitions with new equations or side conditions. Proving `p (S x) →* x` is not a new equation or assumption; it's a semantic consequence of the two equations already given, provable by the same structural induction already justified above.

Once that lemma was in hand, the `fac` recursion could be seen as *really* recursing on `x` (via `S x → x`), restoring the straightforward structural-decrease pattern — so the same induction principle covered it, without needing to add anything to the rewrite system.

## Why I didn't reach for other methods

- **Measure/size functions**: would require defining an external numeric measure — arguably fine in general, but structural induction already suffices and is more minimal/native to the given syntax, so introducing a separate measure would be adding machinery not licensed by "no more than what's given."
- **General well-founded recursion via an arbitrary order**: same objection — subterm order is the *canonical* order here, not an imported one.
- **Reduction/rewriting termination techniques (RPO, dependency pairs, etc.)**: overkill and would implicitly import ordering axioms beyond the immediate subterm structure; not needed since direct structural induction worked once the `p`-lemma was established.

So the method was chosen because it was the *minimal, intrinsic* induction principle available given the constructors, it applied directly to three of the four functions, and — after deriving one lemma using nothing but the same principle — it extended cleanly to the fourth.
