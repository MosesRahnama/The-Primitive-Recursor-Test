I chose a **dependency-pair argument combined with a semantic interpretation** because the rules have two conflicting features:

1. Several rules are naturally **value-preserving arithmetic recursions**:
   - \(f0\) behaves like addition,
   - \(f2\) like multiplication,
   - \(f3\) like predecessor,
   - \(f4\) like factorial.

   Interpreting them that way makes every rewrite rule weakly decreasing—indeed, equal in value.

2. Whole terms can grow substantially. In particular,

\[
f4(f1(x))\to
f2(f4(f3(f1(x))),f1(x))
\]

introduces both an \(f2\) and another \(f4\), so ordinary term size does not decrease. A direct polynomial or simple path-order proof is therefore not apparent. There is also a precedence tension in path-order approaches: the \(f4\)-rule suggests placing \(f4\) above \(f2\), while comparing the nested \(f4\)-call requires showing that \(f3(f1(x))\) is smaller than \(f1(x)\); the \(f3\)-rules impose their own conflicting structural requirements.

The dependency-pair method separates these two issues:

- The semantic interpretation shows that rewriting **between recursive calls cannot increase** the chosen numerical measure.
- Dependency pairs retain only the potentially infinite sequence of defined-symbol calls.
- In each recursive strongly connected component, a simple argument projection strictly decreases:
  - \(F0(x,f1(y))\to F0(x,y)\): second argument decreases.
  - \(F2(f1(x),y)\to F2(x,y)\): first argument decreases.
  - \(F3(f1(f1(x)))\to F3(f1(x))\): argument decreases.
  - \(F4(f1(x))\to F4(f3(f1(x)))\): the interpreted argument decreases from \(x+1\) to \(x\).

Calls from \(F2\) to \(F0\), and from \(F4\) to \(F2\) or \(F3\), do not return to their source components, so they cannot generate a cycle by themselves.

Thus this method was chosen because it handles exactly the obstacle in the system: **rewrite steps may expand syntax while recursive calls still descend semantically**. The factorial interpretation is only a meta-level termination interpretation, not an added rewrite rule or object-level axiom.
