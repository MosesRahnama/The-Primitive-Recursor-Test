I chose **lexicographic path ordering (LPO)** because the termination mechanism is entirely syntactic:

- The recursive call retains the same first argument \(w\).
- Its second argument changes from
  \[
  \mathsf{cons}(x,\mathsf{cons}(y,xs))
  \]
  to the proper subterm
  \[
  \mathsf{cons}(y,xs).
  \]
- The recursive call is then embedded beneath `add`, while the other branch uses only lower-precedence symbols and subterms of the left-hand side.

LPO is well suited to exactly this pattern. With `exec_walk_weight` above `add` and `app` in the precedence, it can simultaneously show that the left-hand side exceeds both components of the right-hand side and therefore the entire `add` term.

It also avoids assumptions that a more informal “list-length decreases” proof might accidentally introduce. In particular, LPO:

- applies to **all finite first-order terms**, not just well-formed lists;
- requires no semantic interpretation of `nil`, `cons`, `add`, or `app`;
- is stable under substitutions and contexts, so orienting every rule proves termination of the whole TRS;
- handles the duplication of \(w\) on the right syntactically;
- orients the two base rules immediately by precedence.

Thus LPO was the most direct standard method that exposes the proper-subterm descent while staying strictly within the given syntax and rules.
