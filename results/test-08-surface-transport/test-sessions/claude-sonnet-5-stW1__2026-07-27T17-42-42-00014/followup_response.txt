I picked structural induction on the `Z`/`S` datatype (with one auxiliary lemma proved the same way) for a few concrete reasons tied to the constraints of the problem.

**1. The constraints rule out the usual "easy" tools.**
Standard automated approaches to termination — e.g. building a well-founded order via a termination checker's default measure, using lexicographic/multiset orders on argument tuples, or invoking a semantic size function as an external axiom — all implicitly import extra structure (a numeric interpretation of `Z`/`S`, a size axiom, or a global well-founded ordering not derivable from the equations themselves). The prompt explicitly forbids importing structural axioms or side assumptions beyond what's in the given equations. The one thing that *is* legitimately available "for free" in a system with constructors `Z`, `S` and no other axioms is the induction principle for that free algebra itself — this is just the proof principle that comes with an inductively defined datatype, not an added assumption. So structural induction was the natural (and essentially only) tool that stays inside the stated boundary.

**2. Three of the four functions are literally structural — no argument needed beyond "look at the equations."**
For `plus`, `times`, and `p`, each recursive call in each equation is made on a proper subterm of the argument being pattern-matched (`y` from `S y`, `x` from `S x`, etc.). This is the textbook shape that structural recursion is designed for, so induction on the relevant argument was the immediate, minimal-commitment choice — I didn't need to invent a measure or well-founded order; the datatype's own subterm relation suffices.

**3. `fac` is the interesting case, and it dictated the "lemma + induction" strategy.**
The call `fac (p (S x))` is *not* syntactically on a subterm of `S x`, since `p (S x)` is a compound expression, not a pattern-matched piece of the input. So naive structural recursion doesn't directly apply, and I had to justify that `p (S x)` eventually behaves like `x` for the purposes of termination. Rather than asserting this as a new axiom (which would break the rules), I derived it as a lemma proved by ordinary induction on `x`, using *only* the two given equations for `p`. This keeps everything inside the equations-only sandbox: no new equation, guard, or assumption is introduced — it's a proved consequence, not an import.

**4. Once the lemma is in hand, everything reduces to structural recursion again.**
With `p (S x) → x` established, the call `fac (p (S x))` becomes (up to reduction) `fac x`, restoring the subterm relationship needed for a clean structural induction on `fac`'s argument. This lets me finish with the same style of argument used for the other three functions, rather than needing a different or heavier method (e.g., a general well-founded/measure-based termination proof) for `fac` alone.

**5. Why not something heavier, like a general well-founded relation or a size-based measure?**
Such methods would work too, but they either presuppose a numeric/size interpretation of `S`/`Z` terms (an added structure not licensed by the bare equations) or amount to the same subterm-order argument in disguise. Since the subterm relation on the free term algebra is already sufficient — every recursive call, directly or after the `p`-lemma, targets a strictly smaller term — invoking a fancier ordering would be unnecessary machinery, and might arguably cross into "importing" extra structure the problem statement wants avoided. Structural induction gives the minimal, self-contained argument that addresses every equation without going beyond what's derivable from the syntax and equations themselves.
