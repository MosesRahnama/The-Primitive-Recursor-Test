The reason I chose the **dependency pair method** with an **algebraic interpretation** is that the system contains a rule—namely (8)—which behaves like an identity under a natural interpretation. Direct termination techniques (like polynomial interpretations or LPO) would be forced to orient every rule with a strict decrease. However, rule (8):

`f4(f1(v0)) -> f2(f4(f3(f1(v0))), f1(v0))`

can be seen, under the interpretation \(f4(x) = x!\), as the factorial identity

\[
(x+1)! = (x+1) \cdot x!
\]

which is an exact equality, not a strict decrease. If I were to try to assign a polynomial interpretation where this rule strictly decreases, I would fail because any polynomial that satisfies this equality forces the left‑hand side to grow no slower than the right‑hand side for all natural numbers. Consequently, no simplification ordering can prove termination directly from the rules alone without adding extra “smart” interpretations that are not monotonically strictly decreasing on that rule.

The dependency pair framework separates the recursive calls (the dependency pairs) from the rules. This allows me to interpret the rewrite rules as **weakly decreasing (equalities)**, while I only have to find a **strict decrease on the dependency pairs**. The troublesome rule (8) then becomes harmless: it is just an equality, and the associated dependency pairs

```
F4(f1(v0)) -> F4(f3(f1(v0)))
F4(f1(v0)) -> F3(f1(v0))
F4(f1(v0)) -> F2(f4(f3(f1(v0))), f1(v0))
```

can be oriented strictly with a carefully chosen reduction pair (the factorial interpretation for `F4` combined with a simple additive/multiplicative interpretation for the other symbols). Thus the method directly addresses the obstacle posed by rule (8), without needing to strengthen the system or assume anything beyond the given syntax and rules.
