Write \(S(t)\) for \(f1(t)\) and \(0\) for \(c0\).

### A syntactic measure

For every terminating term \(t\), define

\[
P(t)=\max\{\,n\mid t\to^* S^n(u)\text{ for some }u\,\}.
\]

This maximum exists: rewriting is finitely branching, so a terminating term has only a finite reduction tree.

Directly from the syntax,

\[
P(S(t))=1+P(t).
\]

The important observation about \(f3\) is

\[
P(f3(S(t)))\le P(t). \tag{1}
\]

Indeed, a reduct of \(f3(S(t))\) can acquire an outer \(S\) only through

\[
f3(S(S(u)))\to S(f3(S(u))).
\]

Before this step, \(t\) must have reduced to \(S(u)\). Each such root step of \(f3\) consumes one exposed \(S\) from the argument while producing one outside. Consequently it cannot produce more leading \(S\)'s than were available in \(t\). The other \(f3\)-rule produces \(0\), so it does not increase \(P\).

### The auxiliary symbols preserve termination

If their arguments terminate, then terms headed by \(f0\), \(f2\), and \(f3\) terminate:

- For
  \[
  f0(x,0)\to x,
  \]
  the result is an argument.

  In
  \[
  f0(x,S(y))\to S(f0(x,y)),
  \]
  the recursive \(f0\)-call has lost one exposed \(S\) from its second argument. Thus repeated recursive \(f0\)-steps strictly decrease \(P\) of that argument.

- Both
  \[
  f2(0,x)\to0,\qquad f2(x,0)\to0
  \]
  terminate immediately at the root.

  In
  \[
  f2(S(x),y)\to f0(f2(x,y),y),
  \]
  the recursive \(f2\)-call has lost one exposed \(S\) from its first argument. The surrounding \(f0\) terminates by the preceding point. Duplication of \(y\) causes no problem: only finitely many copies are introduced at each step, and each copy is terminating.

- For
  \[
  f3(S(S(x)))\to S(f3(S(x))),
  \]
  the recursive \(f3\)-call has one fewer exposed \(S\) in its argument. The rule
  \[
  f3(S(0))\to0
  \]
  is terminating immediately.

These statements can be formalized by induction on the finite reduction trees of the arguments, with \(P\) as the secondary induction measure for root-recursive steps.

### The \(f4\)-rule

The only remaining rule is

\[
f4(S(x))\to f2(f4(f3(S(x))),S(x)).
\]

Assume, toward contradiction, that there is an infinite reduction, and follow the nested \(f4\)-calls responsible for it. The preceding closure result for \(f2\) implies that if the right-hand side is nonterminating while \(S(x)\) terminates, then the recursive term

\[
f4(f3(S(x)))
\]

must be nonterminating.

For another \(f4\)-root step to occur, its argument must reduce to an \(S\)-headed term:

\[
f3(S(x))\to^* S(y).
\]

By (1),

\[
P(S(y))
 \le P(f3(S(x)))
 \le P(x)
 < 1+P(x)
 =P(S(x)).
\]

Thus every successive activation of the \(f4\)-rule strictly decreases the natural number \(P\) of its \(S\)-headed argument. Infinitely many such activations would therefore yield an infinite strictly descending sequence of natural numbers, which is impossible.

If only finitely many \(f4\)-steps occur, the remaining reduction uses only \(f0,f2,f3\), already shown terminating. Hence no infinite reduction exists.

Therefore **all eight rules are covered and the entire rewrite system is terminating**.
