### Explanation and Proof

We can prove strong normalization using a standard **Lexicographic Path Ordering (LPO)**, which is a well-founded simplification ordering. 

1. **Define the Precedence and Status:**
   * Set the operator precedence as: $F \succ G$
   * Assign a lexicographic status to $F$ that prioritizes its third argument first.

2. **Verify Rule 1:**  
   $$F(x, y, Z) \rightarrow x$$
   * By the subterm property of LPO, any term is strictly greater than its proper subterms. Since $x$ is a subterm of $F(x, y, Z)$, we have:
     $$F(x, y, Z) \succ_{lpo} x$$

3. **Verify Rule 2:**  
   $$F(x, y, S(n)) \rightarrow G(y, y, F(x, y, n))$$

     1. **For the argument $y$:** $F(x, y, S(n)) \succ_{lpo} y$ holds by the subterm property.
     2. **For the argument $F(x, y, n)$:** We compare $F(x, y, S(n))$ and $F(x, y, n)$ which share the same root symbol $F$. 
        * Following the status of $F$, we compare their third arguments first: $S(n)$ and $n$.
        * Since $S(n) \succ_{lpo} n$ (by the subterm property), it follows that:
          $$F(x, y, S(n)) \succ_{lpo} F(x, y, n)$$
   * Consequently, the entire left-hand side is strictly greater than the right-hand side:
     $$F(x, y, S(n)) \succ_{lpo} G(y, y, F(x, y, n))$$

Because both rewrite rules strictly decrease under a well-founded LPO, the rewrite system is strongly normalizing. No external or structural assumptions beyond the rules themselves are required.
