### Reasoning and Proof

We can prove strong normalization using a **Lexicographic Path Ordering (LPO)**, which is a well-known simplification order. Since LPO is a reduction order that is well-founded, any term rewriting system compatible with an LPO is strongly normalizing.

Let us define the precedence on the function symbols as:
$$F > G > S > Z$$

Using standard left-to-right lexicographic comparison for LPO, we check both rules:

1. **Rule 1: $F(x, y, Z) \rightarrow x$**
   * This is immediately satisfied because $x$ is a strict subterm of the left-hand side:
     $$F(x, y, Z) >_{lpo} x$$

2. **Rule 2: $F(x, y, S(n)) \rightarrow G(y, y, F(x, y, n))$**
   * Since we defined the precedence $F > G$, to prove $F(x, y, S(n)) >_{lpo} G(y, y, F(x, y, n))$, we must show that the left-hand side is strictly greater than all arguments of $G$ on the right-hand side:
     1. $F(x, y, S(n)) >_{lpo} y$ (True, since $y$ is a direct subterm of the LHS).
     2. $F(x, y, S(n)) >_{lpo} F(x, y, n)$. To show this:
        * The root symbols are identical ($F = F$).
        * We compare their arguments lexicographically from left to right: the first two arguments are equal ($x = x$ and $y = y$), and for the third argument, $S(n) >_{lpo} n$ (since $n$ is a strict subterm of $S(n)$).
        * Furthermore, the LHS $F(x, y, S(n))$ is strictly greater than all individual arguments of the RHS subterm $\{x, y, n\}$ because they are all subterms of the LHS.

Since both rules are strictly decreasing under the defined LPO, the system is compatible with a well-founded simplification order and is therefore **strongly normalizing**.
