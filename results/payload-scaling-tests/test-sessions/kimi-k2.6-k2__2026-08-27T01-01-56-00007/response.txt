**Assessment: Yes.** Strong normalization can be established from the given rules alone.

**Reasoning:**  
The system is terminating (hence strongly normalizing) and this can be proved by standard syntactic means without assuming any axioms for `S` and `Z` beyond their role as uninterpreted function symbols in the rewrite rules.

- Rule 1 (`F(x, y, Z) → x`) deletes the symbol `F` entirely.
- Rule 2 (`F(x, y, S(n)) → G(y, y, F(x, y, n))`) preserves one occurrence of `F`, but moves it to the third argument `n`, which is a proper subterm of `S(n)`. The outer `S` is consumed and `G` (which has no rewrite rules) wraps the new call.

This is exactly the recursion pattern of a primitive recursor: each invocation either terminates (rule 1) or recurses on a structurally smaller third argument (rule 2). Although rule 2 duplicates `y`, the duplicated material is placed inside the inactive symbol `G` and does not create new recursive calls to `F` with larger arguments. 

A concrete reduction ordering establishes this formally. For example, a polynomial interpretation over the naturals:
- `[Z] = 1`, `[S](X) = X + 1`, `[G](X,Y,Z) = X + Y + Z + 1`,
- `[F](X,Y,Z) = (2Y + 2)·Z + X + Y + 1`

is strictly monotone and satisfies both rules with a strict decrease (`[lhs] > [rhs]`). Because every rewrite step strictly lowers a natural-number measure, no infinite reduction sequence exists.

Equivalently, the lexicographic path ordering with precedence `F > G, S, Z` also orients both rules. Since these are purely combinatorial, syntactic orderings, the proof imports no structural axioms (such as injectivity of `S`, disjointness of `Z` and `S`, or induction) and no object-level assumptions beyond the two rewrite rules themselves.
