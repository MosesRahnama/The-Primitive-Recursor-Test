## Assessment: **Yes**, strong normalization can be established from the given rules alone.

## Reasoning

The system is strongly normalizing, provable directly via the **Recursive Path Ordering (RPO)** — a standard, self-contained termination technique requiring no external axioms.

**Rules:**
1. `F(x, y, Z) → x`
2. `F(x, y, S(n)) → G(y, y, F(x, y, n))`

**RPO Setup:** Choose precedence **F > G** (other symbols irrelevant) and lexicographic status for `F` comparing left-to-right.

**Rule 1:** `F(x, y, Z) >_rpo x`
Trivial: `x` is a proper subterm of the LHS.

**Rule 2:** `F(x, y, S(n)) >_rpo G(y, y, F(x, y, n))`
Since `F > G` in the precedence, it suffices to show the LHS is greater than each argument of `G`:

- `F(x, y, S(n)) >_rpo y` — `y` is a proper subterm of the LHS. ✓
- `F(x, y, S(n)) >_rpo F(x, y, n)` — Same root symbol `F`, so compare arguments lexicographically:
  - `x = x`, `y = y` (equal), and `S(n) >_rpo n` (since `n` is a proper subterm of `S(n)`).
  - Additionally, the LHS dominates each argument of the inner `F` by the subterm property (`x`, `y`, and `n` are all proper subterms of the LHS). ✓

Since RPO is a **well-founded reduction ordering** (closed under contexts and substitutions), every rewrite step strictly decreases the term in this ordering. Well-foundedness then guarantees no infinite reduction sequences exist.

**Key intuition:** Although rule 2 duplicates `y` (potentially increasing term size), the third argument of `F` strictly decreases from `S(n)` to `n` in the subterm ordering. The RPO captures this: the recursive call `F(x, y, n)` is smaller than `F(x, y, S(n))`, and the wrapping in `G` is handled by the precedence `F > G`.
