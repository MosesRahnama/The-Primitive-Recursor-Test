## Assessment: Yes, strong normalization can be established from the rules alone.

### Reasoning

This is a first-order TRS with two rules:

1. `F(x, y, Z) → x`
2. `F(x, y, S(n)) → G(y, y, y, y, y, y, y, y, F(x, y, n))`

**No rule exists for `G`**, so `G`-headed terms are normal forms at the root. The only redexes are `F`-terms whose third argument is headed by `Z` or `S`.

**Termination proof via Recursive Path Ordering (RPO):**

Choose the precedence **F > G** and give `F` a **multiset** (or lexicographic-from-right) status on its arguments.

- **Rule 1:** `F(x, y, Z) → x`. The RHS `x` is a proper subterm of the LHS, so `F(x, y, Z) >_rpo x` by the subterm property. ✓

- **Rule 2:** `F(x, y, S(n)) → G(y, y, y, y, y, y, y, y, F(x, y, n))`.
  - Root comparison: `F > G` in precedence, so we just need the LHS to be strictly greater than every argument of `G`.
  - LHS `>_rpo y` — since `y` is a proper subterm. ✓
  - LHS `>_rpo F(x, y, n)` — same root symbol `F`, so compare argument multisets: `{x, y, S(n)}` vs `{x, y, n}`. Since `S(n) >_rpo n` (subterm property), the multiset strictly decreases. We also verify the LHS is greater than each argument of the inner `F` (i.e., `x`, `y`, `n`), all of which are proper subterms. ✓

Since RPO is a well-founded simplification ordering, every rewrite step strictly decreases the term in this ordering. No infinite reduction sequence is possible — for ground or non-ground terms alike.

**Intuition:** The third argument of `F` strictly decreases structurally (`S(n)` → `n`), and no rule can regenerate `S`-structure. Although rule 2 duplicates `y` eight times inside `G`, `G` has no rewrite rules, so duplicated subterms can only independently normalize. The recursion is fundamentally bounded by the structural depth of the third argument.
