## Why LPO here

I picked the **Lexicographic Path Order** because it is, for this particular system, essentially the cheapest thing that works. The reasoning has a few layers.

### 1. The system is plain first-order

There is no binding, no $\lambda$, no let-rec, no side conditions, no AC operators, and no `if-then-else`. It is just constructor symbols `s` and `0` with two defined symbols `plus` and `times` whose rules are constructor-matching. For systems of this exact shape — small, first-order, fully terminating recursion on a successor argument — LPO is the textbook hammer.

### 2. The proofs via the standard structural orders actually fail

A naive thing to try is "prove a well-founded measure exists, e.g. pair (size, size)." That fails because of the canonical buggy rules, but more importantly it forces you to think about the second component, which is fragile. The LPO argument only needs the heads `times > plus > s > 0`, which mirrors the obvious fact that the system is "expanding" `times` into `plus`, and `plus` into `s`. That is exactly the structure the rules exhibit. So the precedence is not a choice I am imposing; it is read off the rules.

### 3. The subterm rule does the heavy lifting for the inner unfolding

Notice that rules 1, 3, 4 are all subterm rewrites — the RHS is literally a subterm of the LHS. LPO captures those for free via the (Subterm) clause, no reasoning needed. The interesting rules are 2, 5, 6, and for those the head-symbol argument handles the case analysis: in each one the head strictly decreases in the precedence (`times → plus` and `plus → s`), and you only have to discharge the recursive argument pair, which is again smaller via subterm comparison on the successor side. That is mechanical.

### 4. I could have used other methods, but LPO was the most "free"

Alternatives that would also work and stay within the rules:

- **Recursive path order (RPO) / polynomial interpretation** — equivalent in power here, but the polynomial interpretation needs me to find numbers like $[s](x) = x+1$, $[plus](x,y) = x+y$, $[times](x,y) = x \cdot y$ and check each rule. That is more arithmetic, same logical status.
- **A hand-built semantic labelling argument** (e.g. map `times` to multiplication, `plus` to addition, `s` to +1, `0` to 0) — also works, and is even more "obvious" once you see the rules, but it leans on the fact that $(\mathbb{N}, +, \cdot)$ is well-founded under the natural order on tuples. LPO is more elementary: it is a theorem about the term algebra itself, with no need to import $\mathbb{N}$.
- **Knuth–Bendix completion / dependency pair analysis** — overkill. The system is non-overlapping and the dependency graph is trivial.

I picked LPO because it (a) is a standard, published termination technique, (b) needs nothing more than the six rules and a four-symbol precedence, and (c) gives a one-or-two-line justification per rule, which is what the question is asking for.

### 5. What I deliberately did not do

- I did not import a "natural-number model" with an external order.
- I did not extend the rules with a guard, a counter, or a second pass.
- I did not use the "canonical buggy rules" framing at all — that came in only after you asked me to defend the choice, and it is a red herring for the termination question: termination is about whether rewrite *sequences* are finite, not about whether `times` is the "right" multiplication. The system as written terminates regardless of what the author intended.

So in short: LPO is the smallest standard tool that turns "look at the rules" into a proof, and the proof stays entirely inside the boundary you set.
