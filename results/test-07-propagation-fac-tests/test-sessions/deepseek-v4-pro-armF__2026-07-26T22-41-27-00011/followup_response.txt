I chose the Recursive Path Ordering (RPO) with multiset status because it is a purely syntactic method that directly captures the structural decrease visible in the rules, without needing any arithmetic or semantic interpretation of the natural numbers.

Here’s the reasoning behind that choice:

- **No external assumptions:** The problem explicitly forbids importing object‑level axioms (like Peano arithmetic) or adding extra rules. RPO only uses the term structure and a simple precedence on symbols, so it stays entirely within the given syntax and rewrite rules.

- **Obvious decreases are argument‑wise:** Each recursive call in the system replaces a term like `s(y)` with `y` or `s(x)` with `x` inside a function symbol. A lexicographic or polynomial ordering could also work, but RPO with multiset status mirrors these decreases cleanly: the multiset comparison `{s(y)} > {y}` is exactly the structural subterm decrease we need for rules like `plus(x,s(y)) -> s(plus(x,y))` and `times(x,s(y)) -> plus(times(x,y),x)`.

- **Simplicity:** Setting the precedence `times > plus > s > 0` is sufficient to orient all six rules. No complex polynomial interpretations or custom well-founded orders are needed. The subterm property of RPO handles the base cases (e.g., `plus(x,0) -> x`), and the decrease on recursive arguments follows directly from the precedence and multiset status.

In short, RPO was the simplest, most direct tool that could prove termination without appealing to any external theory—just what the syntax and rules provide.
