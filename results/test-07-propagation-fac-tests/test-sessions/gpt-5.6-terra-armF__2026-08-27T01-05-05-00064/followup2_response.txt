Yes. The method complies with those requirements.

LPO is a **meta-level termination proof technique**, not an added object-level axiom or rewrite rule. Choosing:

\[
\mathsf{times} \succ \mathsf{plus} \succ \mathsf{s} \succ 0
\]

and lexicographic argument status does not alter the TRS. It merely defines a well-founded ordering on the finite first-order terms already present in the syntax.

In particular, the proof does **not** assume any of the following:

- that `plus` is associative, commutative, or has an identity;
- that `times` or `plus` denote arithmetic operations;
- that `s` is injective or represents natural-number successor;
- induction over an intended natural-number domain;
- confluence, normalization, or any unlisted rewrite rule;
- guards such as “`y` must be a numeral”;
- an implicit simplification such as `plus(x,0) = x` except where it is explicitly one of the given rewrite rules.

The facts used, such as

\[
s(y) > y
\quad\text{and}\quad
\mathsf{times}(x,s(y)) > x,
\]

are not object-level equations or assumptions. They arise from the **definition of LPO**: a term is ordered above its proper subterms. Similarly, the conclusion that the ordering is well-founded is the standard metatheorem about LPO on finite first-order terms over a finite signature with a well-founded symbol precedence.

Thus the proof is conditional only on the ordinary framework already implicit in calling the displayed system a first-order TRS: terms are finite syntax trees, rewriting uses the six shown rules and ordinary substitutions, and termination means absence of an infinite rewrite sequence.

So the answer remains: **termination is established from the presented rules alone**, with LPO serving only as an external proof instrument, not as a strengthening of the rewrite system.
