I picked LPO because the rules have exactly the shape that a recursive-path-style ordering handles well:

- The recursive calls are structurally smaller:
  - `times(x, s(y))` contains the recursive call `times(x, y)`;
  - `plus(x, s(y))` contains `plus(x, y)`;
  - `plus(s(x), y)` contains `plus(x, y)`.

- The right-hand sides may wrap those smaller calls in a lower-precedence symbol:
  - `times(...)` rewrites to `plus(...)`;
  - `plus(...)` rewrites to `s(...)`.

Thus the precedence

\[
\mathsf{times} \succ \mathsf{plus} \succ \mathsf{s}
\]

matches the visible dependency structure of the rules: multiplication calls multiplication on a smaller second argument but produces addition; addition calls addition on a smaller argument but produces a successor wrapper.

LPO also directly handles the projection-like rules

\[
\mathsf{plus}(x,0)\to x,\qquad
\mathsf{plus}(0,x)\to x,
\]

because its subterm property makes every compound term larger than its arguments. No special measure or semantic interpretation is required.

I could instead have used a custom natural-number measure based on term structure—for example, a lexicographic measure tracking the amount of `times` recursion before `plus` recursion—but that would be more ad hoc and would require more bookkeeping to ensure that rule 2 does not accidentally increase the measure through the introduced `plus`. LPO packages that bookkeeping into a standard well-founded ordering and proves all six rules uniformly, using only the syntax and the displayed rewrite rules.
