## Why polynomial interpretation?

I chose it because it's the **most direct standard method** for this shape of system. Specifically:

**1. The system is constructor-rich with no critical complications.**

Looking at the signature, the only "destructors" are `plus` and `times` (which both have left-constructor patterns like `plus(_, 0)`, `plus(_, s(_))`, `times(s(_), _)`, `times(_, 0)`), plus the unary `p`. There are:
- No overlapping rules
- No mutual recursion between `plus`/`times` in a tricky way
- No non-linear patterns
- No variable binding, no AC, no higher-order stuff
- No `μ`-recursion or induction principles needed

Polynomial interpretations handle this class essentially by construction.

**2. Polynomial interpretation works without requiring an ordering proof.**

I considered the alternatives:
- **Recursive path ordering / lexicographic path ordering:** Would need to pick an argument order, justify a quasi-commutation for Rule 5 (since `times(s(x), y)` recurses on `x` but also references `y` on the RHS via `plus(_, y)`, and the LPO would need to know `y` is "smaller enough"). This is doable but more bookkeeping.
- **Size-change termination / matrix interpretation:** Overkill for a system this small.
- **Dependency pairs + reduction pairs:** More machinery, same endgame.
- **Ad-hoc well-founded orderings:** You'd essentially reinvent the polynomial order with extra steps.

**3. The exponentials come from `times`.**

The choice of `2^(x+y+1)` wasn't arbitrary. The product-like structure of `times` (with `times(0,y)→0` *and* `times(x,0)→0`, plus the recursive `times(s(x),y)→plus(times(x,y),y)`) means any interpretation must have `times` grow at least multiplicatively in its arguments. If I'd tried `times = x·y` or linear interpretations, Rule 3 or Rule 5 would fail. I had to reach for something exponential. A matrix interpretation would have worked too (it's a special case of polynomial interpretation), but for two arguments the closed-form `2^...` is cleaner.

**4. The method is local and verifiable per-rule.**

The whole proof reduces to checking seven inequalities, each by elementary algebra. No induction on derivations, no compatibility lemmas, no case analysis beyond the rules themselves. That makes the argument self-contained and short — appropriate for a "raw assessment."

**5. It needs no object-level assumptions.**

This was your explicit constraint. Polynomial interpretation is a *semantic* method: it assigns meanings in a well-founded algebra (here, ℕ with the standard order) and checks that each rule strictly decreases the meaning. No additional rewrite rules, no guards, no structural axioms — just a monotone map into a well-founded set. Exactly the "standard proof method, no strengthening" you allowed.

**Honest caveat:** I should note that polynomial interpretation does require picking the right interpretation — there's no fully automatic recipe (though the coefficients are constrained enough that you can often find one by solving linear inequalities when the shape is right). The non-uniqueness of the choice is the main place where human/heuristic judgment enters. But the method itself is textbook (Lankford, 1979) and the proof is purely from the presented rules.
