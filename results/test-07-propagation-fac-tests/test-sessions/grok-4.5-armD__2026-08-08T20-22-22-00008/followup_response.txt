### Why LPO?

I chose the lexicographic path order (LPO) because it is one of the simplest, purely syntactic, standard simplification orders that orients every rule of this TRS directly, with no extra machinery.

- The rules are the classic primitive-recursive definitions of addition and multiplication on a unary numeral system, plus two straightforward “predecessor” rules for `p`. Their recursive structure immediately suggests a precedence `times > plus > p > s > 0` (each defined function is larger than the functions/constructors it calls). With left-to-right lexicographic status, the seven elementary LPO comparisons go through by the usual subterm and lexicographic arguments; nothing else is required.
- LPO is a well-founded simplification order on the free term algebra, so orientation of the rules alone yields termination. No dependency pairs, no usable rules, no argument filter, no semantic labeling, no models, and no additional rewrite rules or side conditions are needed—exactly the boundary the question asked for.
- Other standard methods also work (e.g., a simple linear polynomial interpretation, or KBO with appropriate weights), but they require choosing numeric coefficients or weights, which is slightly more bookkeeping. LPO needs only the precedence, which is read off the call graph of the rules, so it is the most economical choice that still stays inside “ordinary first-order term rewriting.”

In short: the TRS is a textbook example on which a basic path order succeeds at once; that is why I picked it.
