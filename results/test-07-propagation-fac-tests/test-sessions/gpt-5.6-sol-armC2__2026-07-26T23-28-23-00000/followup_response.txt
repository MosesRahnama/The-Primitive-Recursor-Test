I chose **dependency pairs** because the only real difficulty is the `fac` rule:

```text
fac(s(x)) -> times(fac(p(s(x))), s(x))
```

Its recursive argument, `p(s(x))`, is not a syntactic subterm of `s(x)`. Therefore, a simple structural-subterm argument cannot directly show that the recursive call decreases. Also, the whole right-hand side can be much larger than the left, so a basic term-size measure is unsuitable.

Dependency pairs separate two concerns:

1. **Recursive-call descent:**  
   They isolate the call

   ```text
   FAC(s(x)) -> FAC(p(s(x)))
   ```

   The given `p` rules justify measuring `p(s(x))` as one less than `s(x)`.

2. **Nonrecursive computation around the call:**  
   `times` and `plus` may enlarge the term, but their own recursion decreases in a clear argument, and calls from `fac` to `times` do not lead back to `fac`.

This method also handles the system modularly through its call graph:

- `fac` recursively calls `fac` on a smaller interpreted argument;
- `times` recursively decreases its first argument;
- `plus` recursively decreases its second argument;
- `p` recursively decreases the number of leading `s` constructors;
- cross-calls such as `fac → times → plus` do not form cycles.

One precision about my previous answer: the interpretations of **marked dependency-pair symbols** should be stated separately. The ordinary `fac` can be interpreted as \(0\) to make the original rules weakly decreasing, while the marked symbol `FAC` is interpreted by its argument, so that

\[
FAC(s(x))=x+1 > x=FAC(p(s(x))).
\]

Thus dependency pairs were chosen because they expose exactly the recursive descent that whole-term size or a straightforward structural ordering obscures.
