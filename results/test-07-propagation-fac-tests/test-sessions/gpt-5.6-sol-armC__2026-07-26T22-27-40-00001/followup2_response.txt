**Yes, with one qualification:** the method complies, provided it is understood as a standard **dependency-pair termination proof** over ordinary finite first-order terms.

The numerical valuation is a **meta-level interpretation of syntax**, not an object-level assumption that terms denote natural numbers. Its properties are verified directly for the displayed rules. No new rewrite rules, guards, equations, or assumptions about constructors are added.

More formally, the cyclic dependency pairs are:

- `plus#(x,s(y)) → plus#(x,y)`
- `times#(s(x),y) → times#(x,y)`
- `p#(s(s(x))) → p#(s(x))`
- `fac#(s(x)) → fac#(p(s(x)))`

The other dependency pairs only move down the acyclic hierarchy
`fac → times → plus` and `fac → p`.

Using the syntactic interpretation
\[
|0|=0,\quad |s(t)|=|t|+1,\quad
|p(t)|=\max(|t|-1,0),
\]
with the given interpretations for `plus`, `times`, and `fac`, all original rules are weakly oriented—in fact, they preserve the value—while each cyclic dependency pair strictly decreases its relevant argument:

- `plus`: \(|s(y)|>|y|\)
- `times`: \(|s(x)|>|x|\)
- `p`: \(|s(s(x))|>|s(x)|\)
- `fac`: \(|s(x)|>|p(s(x))|\)

Thus the proof depends only on the syntax and rules given.

The qualification is that my original answer compressed this into “recursive dependency” language. The valuation alone would **not** prove termination, since the rewrite rules generally preserve its value. The dependency-pair component is essential and should have been stated explicitly. Ordinary finiteness of first-order terms is part of the standard TRS setting, not an imported object-level axiom.
