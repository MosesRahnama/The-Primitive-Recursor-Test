The choice of the dependency pair framework with the subterm criterion and a polynomial interpretation was driven directly by the structure of the rules.  The system has two kinds of recursion:

1. **Direct structural recursion on a constructor argument** – the rules for `plus`, `times`, and `p` all recurse on an argument of the form `s(…)` that becomes a proper subterm in the recursive call.  
   - `plus(x, s(y))` recurses to `plus(x, y)`.  
   - `times(s(x), y)` recurses to `times(x, y)`.  
   - `p(s(s(x)))` recurses to `p(s(x))`.  

   This pattern is exactly what the **subterm criterion** captures: by projecting the corresponding dependency pairs onto the recursive argument position, the right‑hand side of the DP is a proper subterm of the left‑hand side, so termination follows without any arithmetic interpretation.

2. **Indirect recursion through a defined symbol** – `fac(s(x))` recurses to `fac(p(s(x)))`.  The argument is not a direct subterm; it is the result of applying `p`.  This cannot be handled by the subterm criterion because the argument is transformed, not peeled.  After removing the other DPs, this single DP remains, and we need a measure that decreases when `s(x)` is replaced by `p(s(x))`.  A simple **polynomial interpretation** (size measure) works: interpret `s` as `+1`, `p` as truncating subtraction, and `fac` as the identity on its argument.  Then `[s(x)] = [x]+1` and `[p(s(x))] = [x]`, so the measure drops strictly.

The dependency pair framework is the natural umbrella because it isolates the recursive calls, allows modular treatment (separating the easy recursively‑decreasing parts from the harder ones), and combines different termination criteria (subterm, reduction pairs) without strengthening the rules or adding axioms.  The method was picked because it matches the problem’s structure perfectly and stays entirely within the given rewrite system.
