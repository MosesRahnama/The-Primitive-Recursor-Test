I picked **lexicographic path ordering (LPO)** because the second rule has two features that LPO handles naturally:

\[
F(x,y,S(n)) \to G(y,F(x,y,n)).
\]

1. **The recursive call decreases structurally.**  
   The inner call changes from third argument \(S(n)\) to its proper subterm \(n\):

   \[
   F(x,y,S(n)) > F(x,y,n).
   \]

   Giving \(F\) lexicographic status makes that decrease directly visible after the unchanged arguments \(x,y\).

2. **The right-hand side grows and changes its root symbol.**  
   A simple term-size measure does not work: the right-hand side is larger than the left. LPO can nevertheless orient the rule by setting

   \[
   F \succ G.
   \]

   It then suffices to show that the left-hand side exceeds each argument of the new outer \(G\): it exceeds \(y\) by the subterm property and exceeds \(F(x,y,n)\) by the recursive-argument decrease.

The first rule,

\[
F(x,y,Z)\to x,
\]

is immediate from LPO’s subterm property.

So LPO was chosen because one standard ordering simultaneously captures the two relevant syntactic facts: **descent from \(S(n)\) to \(n\)** and **precedence of \(F\) over the newly introduced \(G\)**. It proves termination directly without interpreting `Z` or `S` as naturals or adding any assumptions or rules. A polynomial or matrix interpretation might also work, but would require inventing and checking numerical interpretations; LPO gives the shortest structural proof.
