Yes. The LPO proof complies with those requirements.

The choices

\[
F \succ G
\]

and lexicographic status for \(F\) are **meta-level components of a standard reduction ordering**, not new object-level axioms, rewrite rules, guards, or assumptions about the meanings of the symbols. They do not modify which terms can be formed or which reductions can occur.

The proof uses only:

- the syntactic subterm relation, e.g. \(S(n)\) contains \(n\);
- the syntactic argument positions of \(F\) and \(G\);
- the two presented rewrite rules;
- the standard properties of LPO: well-foundedness, substitution stability, context closure, and the subterm property.

In particular, it does **not** assume:

- that `Z` denotes zero;
- that `S` denotes arithmetic successor;
- that \(n\) ranges only over natural-number-shaped terms;
- that `S` is injective or disjoint from `Z`;
- that `F` or `G` satisfies any equations;
- that arguments are evaluated in a particular order.

Arbitrary substitutions for \(x,y,n\) are covered. Even if \(n\) contains occurrences of \(F\), the syntactic relation \(S(n)>n\) remains valid under LPO.

The only background convention is the standard one inherent in an ordinary first-order TRS: terms are finite syntax trees. If infinitary terms were intended, that would be a different rewriting framework and would require explicit treatment. Under the stated first-order term-rewriting setting, the argument stays within the requested boundary.
