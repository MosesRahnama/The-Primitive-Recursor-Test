I chose this particular combination (structural subterm property + a well-founded measure on the `Z`/`S` counter, combined lexicographically) for several concrete reasons tied to the shape of this specific system, rather than defaulting to a heavier general-purpose technique. Let me walk through the actual decision process.

## Why not just try the simplest standard tool first?

The most standard "first thing to try" for a small first-order TRS like this is usually **simplification/recursive path ordering (RPO)** with some precedence on function symbols, since RPO proofs are mechanical and often succeed on toy systems. I considered this, but noticed a structural obstruction:

- Rule (2) is `F(x, y, S(n)) -> G(y, F(x, y, n))`.
- For RPO with `F` having *some* precedence over `G`, the right-hand side's outermost symbol `G` would need to be ≤ `F` in precedence, and then we'd compare arguments — but the right side contains `y` as a top-level argument of `G`, and `y` is a *variable* coming from the second argument of the original `F(x,y,S(n))`. Under RPO, this is fine (variables are handled by the subterm/variable condition), **but** the real subtlety is that `G` is introduced as a brand new head symbol with **no rules of its own** — it's a "free" constructor as far as the rewrite system is concerned. Standard RPO would still work here in principle, but it requires committing to a precedence and status for `G` vs `F` vs `S` vs `Z`, which is more machinery than the problem actually demands.

So RPO is *available* but overkill: it forces me to invent a precedence structure that isn't really doing any work, since the actual "decrease" driving termination is extremely simple and localized to one argument position.

## Why the measure/subterm split instead

Looking at the two rules individually, I noticed they play **completely different structural roles**:

1. **Rule (1)** `F(x,y,Z) -> x` is a pure *projection/erasure* rule — the entire left-hand side collapses to one of its own subterms. This is the textbook case where the **subterm property** (Σ ⊐ any of its proper subterms) trivially guarantees termination *for that rule in isolation* — no measure, interpretation, or ordering machinery is needed at all. Recognizing this let me dispatch rule (1) "for free" instead of folding it into a more complex uniform argument.

2. **Rule (2)** `F(x,y,S(n)) -> G(y, F(x,y,n))` is a *recursive descent* rule: it's not an erasure, since the right side is not a subterm of the left side (it introduces `G` at the top). But it has the classic shape of "peel off one `S` and recurse" — this is exactly the pattern a **simple counter/measure argument** on the `Z`/`S` structure was built to handle, since `Z`/`S` are literally acting as unary natural numbers here, and the third argument strictly shrinks (`S(n) → n`) with each unfolding.

Given that the two rules are doing structurally unrelated jobs (one erases, one recurses on a numeral), it seemed more transparent and more *tightly justified by the syntax actually present* to treat them with two different, minimal arguments and glue them with a lexicographic combination, rather than searching for one uniform interpretation (e.g., a single polynomial interpretation) that has to simultaneously explain both behaviors.

## Why not a polynomial interpretation instead

A polynomial/semantic interpretation is another standard option: assign natural number interpretations to `Z`, `S`, `F`, `G` and check that each rule strictly decreases the interpretation. I considered this too — e.g., `[Z]=0`, `[S](n)=n+1`, `[F](x,y,n) = n`, `[G](y,z) = z` or similar. This actually *would* work and is arguably even simpler to state. However, I preferred the subterm+measure route because:

- It makes explicit *why* the system terminates in terms of the **actual role of `G`**: the key subtlety in this problem is that `G` has no rules of its own, so it cannot itself be a source of new redexes — it just wraps things. A polynomial interpretation would silently encode this fact inside the numeric assignment to `[G]`, without making it conceptually visible. I wanted to explicitly flag "no rule rewrites `G(...)`, so it can't loop" as a load-bearing observation, since it's the thing that would break the argument if the system were extended.
- The subterm argument for rule (1) is *provably tighter* than a polynomial bound: it doesn't just decrease some numeric interpretation, it shows the reduct is literally embedded in the redex, which is the strongest possible termination witness for a single rule.

## Summary of the decision

I picked the layered approach (subterm property for the erasure rule + a well-founded measure on the unary-numeral argument for the recursive rule, combined lexicographically) because:

1. It matches the **actual distinct structural role** each rule plays (erasure vs. bounded recursion), rather than forcing a one-size-fits-all interpretation.
2. It lets me use the **strongest, most primitive** justification available for rule (1) (subterm property) instead of manufacturing a numeric interpretation for a case that doesn't need one.
3. It makes the necessary but easy-to-overlook fact — that `G` has no rewrite rules and thus cannot itself sustain reduction — an explicit part of the proof, since that fact is exactly what's needed to make sure "wrapping in `G`" doesn't secretly reintroduce nontermination.
4. It stays strictly inside the boundary of the problem: both components (subterm order, and a multiset order over a measure on `Z`/`S`-nesting) are derivable directly from the grammar and rules given, with no extra assumptions, precedence choices, or external axioms required.
