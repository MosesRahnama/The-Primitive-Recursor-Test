Great question! The choice of termination proof method depends on the **structure of the rewrite rules** and the **interactions between them**. Here’s why I picked the methods I did for this system:

---

### **1. Why Lexicographic Path Order (LPO) for `plus` and `times`?**
#### **Applicability:**
- LPO is a **syntactic termination method** that works well for **constructor-based systems** (like Peano arithmetic), where terms are built from constructors (`0`, `s`) and defined functions (`plus`, `times`).
- The `plus` and `times` rules are **recursive in a single argument** (the second argument for `plus`, the first for `times`), and the right-hand sides are "smaller" in a lexicographic sense.

#### **Why not other methods?**
- **Polynomial interpretations** would also work (e.g., `[plus](x,y) = x + y`, `[times](x,y) = x * y`), but LPO is simpler for these rules because:
  - The rules are **non-overlapping** (no critical pairs), so no need for semantic interpretations.
  - LPO directly captures the "recursive structure" of the rules (e.g., `plus(x, s(y)) → s(plus(x, y))` is decreasing because `s` is smaller than `plus` in the precedence).
- **Dependency pairs (DP)** are overkill for `plus` and `times` because they don’t have nested recursion (e.g., `fac` does, but `plus` and `times` don’t).

#### **Precedence choice:**
- I picked `plus > s` and `times > plus, s` because:
  - `plus` and `times` are "bigger" operations than `s` (successor).
  - `times` is "bigger" than `plus` because multiplication is defined in terms of addition.

---

### **2. Why Direct Size Decrease for `p`?**
#### **Applicability:**
- The `p` (predecessor) rules are **non-recursive in a way that strictly reduces term size**:
  - `p(s(s(x))) → s(p(s(x)))`: The argument to `p` loses one `s` constructor.
  - `p(s(0)) → 0`: The term shrinks to a constant.
- This is a **trivial case** for termination: the rules are **simply reducing the number of symbols** in the term.

#### **Why not LPO or DP?**
- LPO would work (with `p > s`), but it’s unnecessary because the size decrease is obvious.
- DP is overkill because there’s no recursion (the right-hand side doesn’t call `p` again).

---

### **3. Why Dependency Pairs (DP) + Polynomial Interpretation for `fac`?**
#### **Applicability:**
- The `fac` rule is **mutually recursive** with `p` and `times`:
  `fac(s(x)) → times(fac(p(s(x))), s(x))`.
  This is a **nested recursion** (the recursive call `fac(p(s(x)))` is inside `times`), so we need a method that can handle **indirect recursion**.
- **Dependency pairs (DP)** are designed for this: they extract the recursive calls into a separate "dependency graph" and let us analyze them independently.
- **Polynomial interpretations** are then used to show that the recursive calls are on "smaller" arguments.

#### **Why not LPO?**
- LPO fails for `fac` because:
  - The right-hand side `times(fac(p(s(x))), s(x))` is not lexicographically smaller than the left-hand side `fac(s(x))` under any reasonable precedence.
  - For example, if `fac > times`, then `times(fac(...), ...)` is not smaller than `fac(s(x))` because `times` is not a subterm of `fac`.
  - If `times > fac`, then `times(fac(...), ...)` is larger than `fac(s(x))` because `times` is bigger.

#### **Why polynomial interpretations?**
- They provide a **semantic** way to measure term size, which is necessary for `fac` because the syntactic methods (LPO, RPO) fail.
- The interpretation `[p](x) = max(0, x - 1)` captures the intuition that `p` decreases its argument by 1 (modulo 0).
- The interpretation for `fac` doesn’t matter as long as it’s monotonic (we only care about the argument to `fac`).

#### **Why not other methods?**
- **Matrix interpretations** or **arctic interpretations** could also work, but they’re more complex and unnecessary here.
- **Path orders (RPO, KBO)** fail for the same reasons as LPO.

---

### **4. Why Not a Single Method for the Whole System?**
- The system is **heterogeneous**: different rules require different methods.
  - `plus` and `times` are best handled by LPO.
  - `p` is trivial (size decrease).
  - `fac` needs DP + polynomial interpretations.
- No single method (LPO, RPO, KBO, etc.) can handle all rules simultaneously because:
  - LPO/RPO/KBO fail for `fac`.
  - Polynomial interpretations alone would require defining interpretations for all symbols, which is tedious and unnecessary for `plus`/`times`/`p`.

---

### **5. Why Not Use a Tool Like AProVE or TTT2?**
- The question asked for a **raw assessment** using standard proof methods, not automated tools.
- Tools like AProVE or TTT2 would likely use the same methods (DP + polynomial interpretations for `fac`, LPO for `plus`/`times`), but the goal here was to **manually justify termination**.

---

### **Summary of Method Choices**
| Rule(s)          | Method                     | Reason                                                                 |
|------------------|----------------------------|------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| `plus`, `times`  | Lexicographic Path Order   | Simple, syntactic, works for constructor-based recursion.              |
| `p`              | Direct size decrease       | Trivial case: rules shrink terms.                                      |
| `fac`            | Dependency Pairs + PolyInt | Nested recursion requires semantic methods; LPO/RPO fail.              |

This combination is **standard practice** in termination analysis:
1. Use **syntactic methods (LPO/RPO/KBO)** where possible (they’re fast and don’t require guessing interpretations).
2. Use **semantic methods (polynomial interpretations)** for rules where syntactic methods fail.
3. Use **dependency pairs** to handle nested recursion.
