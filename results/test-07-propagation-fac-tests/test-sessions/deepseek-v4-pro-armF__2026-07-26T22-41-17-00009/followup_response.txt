I chose the **lexicographic path ordering (LPO)** because it directly mirrors the syntactic structure of the rules and the way recursion decreases the arguments.  

All three defined functions (`times`, `plus`, `s`) follow a simple pattern: every recursive call is on a **strict subterm** of the original argument (e.g., `times(x, s(y))` calls `times(x, y)` with the second argument `y` appearing as a direct subterm of `s(y)`). LPO is specifically designed to capture such “structural descent” by comparing subterms lexicographically according to a precedence on function symbols.  

Here, the precedence `times > plus > s > 0` immediately yields a descending chain for every rule without any need for arithmetic interpretation or polynomial weight functions. The method is purely syntactic, requires no external axioms or invented well-founded orders, and its correctness follows straight from the definition of LPO being a simplification order (hence well-founded).  

Because the termination problem is so transparently structural, LPO is the most direct mechanical proof: it aligns exactly with the intuition that the system is terminating because each rewrite reduces the height or size of a term.
