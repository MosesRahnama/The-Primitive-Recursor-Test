I chose **lexicographic path ordering (LPO)** because the rules have two features that make simpler termination measures awkward:

- Some right-hand sides are not smaller by term size, especially  
  \[
  \mathsf{times}(\mathsf{s}(x),y)\to
  \mathsf{plus}(\mathsf{times}(x,y),y),
  \]
  which expands the term and duplicates \(y\).
- The recursive calls nevertheless have a clear **syntactic argument decrease**:
  - \(\mathsf{plus}(x,\mathsf{s}(y))\) calls \(\mathsf{plus}(x,y)\);
  - \(\mathsf{times}(\mathsf{s}(x),y)\) calls \(\mathsf{times}(x,y)\);
  - \(\mathsf{p}(\mathsf{s}(\mathsf{s}(x)))\) calls \(\mathsf{p}(\mathsf{s}(x))\).

LPO is designed to combine exactly those decreases with a precedence on root symbols. The essential precedence constraints are

\[
\mathsf{times}\succ\mathsf{plus},\qquad
\mathsf{plus}\succ\mathsf{s},\qquad
\mathsf{p}\succ\mathsf{s}.
\]

The total chain I gave,

\[
\mathsf{times}\succ\mathsf{plus}\succ\mathsf{p}\succ\mathsf{s}\succ0,
\]

is merely a convenient extension; not every comparison in that chain is needed.

Lexicographic status then recognizes the recursive argument decreases, while root precedence handles the surrounding constructor on the right. The projection/base rules are handled immediately by LPO’s subterm property.

This method is also particularly suitable for the stated boundary: it is a purely syntactic, substitution- and context-stable well-founded ordering on finite first-order terms. It does not interpret \(0\) and \(\mathsf{s}\) as natural numbers, assume induction equations, add guards, or require extra rewrite rules. A raw size measure would fail, and a standard Knuth–Bendix ordering is inconvenienced by duplication of \(y\) in the `times` rule, whereas LPO handles it directly.
