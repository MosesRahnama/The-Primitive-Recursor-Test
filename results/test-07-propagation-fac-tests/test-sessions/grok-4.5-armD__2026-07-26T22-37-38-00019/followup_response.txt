### Why LPO?

I chose the lexicographic path order (LPO) because it is a purely syntactic, well-founded simplification order that is complete for many simple first-order TRSs of this kind and requires nothing beyond the given signature and rules.

Concretely:

- The system is small, untyped, first-order, and free of AC symbols, associative-commutative theories, conditional rules, or binders. LPO is therefore directly applicable and needs no extensions.
- A total precedence on the five function symbols is enough to orient every rule; the checks are local (root comparison + recursive descent on arguments) and can be carried out by hand in a few lines, as shown in the previous answer.
- LPO is known to be stable under contexts and substitutions and to contain the subterm relation, so once the seven inequalities hold, termination follows at once from the well-foundedness of the order—no additional semantic interpretation, polynomial ranking, or dependency-pair analysis is required.
- Other common methods (recursive path order with multiset status, Knuth-Bendix order, polynomial interpretations, \ldots) also work, but they are either notationally heavier or demand extra arithmetic justifications that are unnecessary here. LPO is the lightest instrument that still stays strictly inside the “rules alone” boundary you imposed.

In short, LPO gives a self-contained, finitary certificate that inspects each rule exactly once and uses only the syntactic material supplied in the problem statement.
