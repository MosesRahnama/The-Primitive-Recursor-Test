I chose **Polynomial Interpretation over $\mathbb{N}$** for two main reasons: first, as a general proof technique well-suited for arithmetic rewrite systems, and second, because the specific polynomials can be systematically reverse-engineered from the algebraic constraints imposed by the rules. 

Here is the detailed breakdown of why this method was selected and how the specific interpretations were derived.

### 1. Why Polynomial Interpretation over Path Orderings (LPO/KBO)?
While syntactic methods like the Lexicographic Path Ordering (LPO) or Knuth-Bendix Ordering (KBO) could potentially prove this system terminating (e.g., using a precedence like `times > plus > p > s`), they require guessing the correct precedence relations and argument statuses (lexicographic vs. multiset). 

Polynomial interpretation, by contrast, is a **semantic method**. Because this TRS is fundamentally defining arithmetic operations (`plus`, `times`, predecessor `p`), mapping the syntactic constructors to actual arithmetic growth rates is the most natural fit. It translates the problem of finding a well-founded syntactic ordering into solving a system of simple algebraic inequalities, which is deterministic, easy to verify by hand, and leaves no ambiguity about precedence.

### 2. How the Specific Polynomials were Derived
The polynomials were not chosen at random; they are the simplest solutions to the inequality constraints forced by the rewrite rules. Here is the reverse-engineering process:

#### The `plus` constraints
We assume a linear form: `⟦plus(x, y)⟧ = x + c·y + d`.
* **Rule:** `plus(x, s(y)) -> s(plus(x, y))`
  * **Inequality:** $x + c(y + 1) + d > (x + cy + d) + 1$
  * **Simplifies to:** $c > 1$. 
  * **Choice:** The smallest integer satisfying this is **$c = 2$**.
* **Rule:** `plus(x, 0) -> x`
  * **Inequality:** $x + c(0) + d > x \implies d > 0$.
  * **Choice:** The smallest integer is **$d = 1$**.
* **Result:** `⟦plus(x, y)⟧ = x + 2y + 1`

#### The `times` constraints
Because multiplication grows quadratically, we assume a bilinear form. Furthermore, we need to ensure that base cases like `times(0, y)` evaluate to strictly greater than `0`, so we add $+1$ offsets to prevent zero-collapse. We test the form: `⟦times(x, y)⟧ = (a·x + 1)(b·y + 1)`.
* **Rule:** `times(s(x), y) -> plus(times(x, y), y)`
  * **Inequality:** $(a(x+1) + 1)(by + 1) > ⟦plus⟧( (ax+1)(by+1), y )$
  * **Substitute `plus`:** $(ax + a + 1)(by + 1) > (ax + 1)(by + 1) + 2y + 1$
  * **Expand LHS:** $(ax + 1)(by + 1) + a(by + 1)$
  * **Cancel common terms:** $a(by + 1) > 2y + 1 \implies aby + a > 2y + 1$
  * **Satisfying the inequality:** To make this hold for all $y \ge 0$, we need the $y$ coefficient to dominate: $ab \ge 2$, and the constant to dominate: $a \ge 2$. 
  * **Choice:** Setting **$a = 2$** and **$b = 2$** perfectly satisfies both ($4y + 2 > 2y + 1$).
* **Result:** `⟦times(x, y)⟧ = (2x + 1)(2y + 1)`

#### The `p` (predecessor) constraints
We assume a linear form: `⟦p(x)⟧ = k·x + m`.
* **Rule:** `p(s(s(x))) -> s(p(s(x)))`
  * **Inequality:** $k(x + 2) + m > (k(x + 1) + m) + 1$
  * **Simplifies to:** $2k > k + 1 \implies k > 1$.
  * **Choice:** The smallest integer is **$k = 2$**.
* **Rule:** `p(s(0)) -> 0`
  * **Inequality:** $k(0 + 1) + m > 0 \implies 2 + m > 0$.
  * **Choice:** **$m = 1$** keeps it strictly positive and simple.
* **Result:** `⟦p(x)⟧ = 2x + 1`

### Summary
I picked Polynomial Interpretation because it allows the termination proof to be constructed by **solving for the coefficients** required to make the left-hand side strictly greater than the right-hand side. The $+1$ and $+2$ offsets in the interpretations are not arbitrary; they are the exact minimal integer solutions required to absorb the structural growth introduced by the `s()` (successor) wrappers on the right-hand sides of the rules.
