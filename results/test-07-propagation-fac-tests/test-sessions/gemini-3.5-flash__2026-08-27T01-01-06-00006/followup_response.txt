The choice of the **Dependency Pair (DP) Framework** (combined with polynomial interpretations) was driven by specific structural features of your term rewriting system that make simpler, global termination proof methods fail. 

Here is the technical breakdown of why this method was chosen over others:

---

### 1. The Precedence Conflict (Why Path Orderings like LPO/RPO Fail)
Standard syntactic simplification orderings, such as the **Lexicographic Path Ordering (LPO)** or **Recursive Path Ordering (RPO)**, require defining a global precedence relation ($>_k$) over the function symbols. 

Your system contains a classic conflict that makes a global path ordering impossible:

* **From the predecessor rules:** 
  To orient the rule `p(s(s(x))) -> s(p(s(x)))`, any standard path ordering requires the defined symbol `p` to be greater than the constructor `s` in the precedence ($p >_k s$), because `p` is at the root of the left-hand side and must dominate the `s` on the right-hand side.
* **From the factorial rule:** 
  To orient the rule `fac(s(x)) -> times(fac(p(s(x))), s(x))` directly, we must show that the arguments of the recursive call decrease. Specifically, we must show that $s(x) >_{lpo} p(s(x))$. Under LPO/RPO, because $p >_k s$, the term $p(s(x))$ will always be strictly larger than $s(x)$, leading to a contradiction ($s(x) \not>_{lpo} p(s(x))$).

Because of this precedence conflict ($p >_k s$ vs. $s >_k p$), **LPO and RPO cannot prove termination of this system on their own.**

---

### 2. Modularity via SCC Decomposition
The DP Framework is **modular**. Instead of finding one single, monolithic reduction ordering that satisfies all rewrite rules simultaneously, the DP framework allows us to:
1. Identify the cycles of recursion (Strongly Connected Components, or SCCs).
2. Eliminate them one by one.

This completely bypasses the global constraints of path orderings. For example, we could isolate the `PLUS` and `TIMES` loops, which are trivially terminating, and focus the more complex machinery only on the interaction between `fac` and `p`.

---

### 3. Handling the Defined Predecessor Function `p`
In the rule:
$$\text{fac}(s(x)) \to \text{times}(\text{fac}(p(s(x))), s(x))$$
the argument of `fac` decreases from $s(x)$ to $p(s(x))$. 

To prove this decreases, we must structurally "know" that $p(s(x))$ evaluates to something smaller than $s(x)$ (specifically, $x$). 
* A global polynomial interpretation on the rules directly is extremely difficult to construct because $p(x)$ must be strictly monotonic, meaning we cannot easily define $[p](x) = x-1$ over the natural numbers without running into negative numbers or violating strict monotonicity requirements.
* In the DP Framework, we use **usable rules**. When analyzing the `FAC` dependency pair, we only need to weakly orient ($\ge$) the rules for `p`. This allowed us to use the interpretation $[p](x) = \max(1, x-1)$ over the domain $[1, \infty)$. Because the framework only requires *weak* decrease for the usable rules of `p`, we successfully proved that $[s(x)] > [p(s(x))]$ (i.e., $x+1 > x$) without violating any monotonicity constraints.

### Summary
The DP Framework was chosen because **it is the only standard method capable of resolving the mutual dependency and precedence conflicts** between the predecessor function `p` and the recursive factorial function `fac`.
