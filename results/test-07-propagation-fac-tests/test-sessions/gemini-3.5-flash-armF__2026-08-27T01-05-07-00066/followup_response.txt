I chose **Lexicographic Path Ordering (LPO)** for this specific system for several key reasons, rooted in the structural properties of your rewrite rules and the constraints of the prompt:

### 1. The Rules Have a Clear Hierarchical Structure (Precedence)
LPO is designed for systems where the operations have a natural "definition hierarchy." Looking at your system:
* `times` is defined in terms of itself and `plus`.
* `plus` is defined in terms of itself and `s` (successor).
* `s` is a constructor that does not depend on any other function.

This strict dependency chain immediately suggests a symbol precedence: $\text{times} \succ \text{plus} \succ \text{s} \succ 0$. LPO leverages this precedence to prove that even when a term grows in size (e.g., `times` expanding to a larger term containing `plus`), it is still "smaller" in the ordering because the dominant operator has been replaced by a strictly lower one in the precedence.

### 2. Lexicographic Status Perfectly Captures Argument Decrease
In recursive rules like:
* $\text{plus}(x, \text{s}(y)) \to \text{s}(\text{plus}(x, y))$
* $\text{times}(x, \text{s}(y)) \to \text{plus}(\text{times}(x, y), x)$

The recursive calls ($\text{plus}(x, y)$ and $\text{times}(x, y)$) are applied to arguments that are structurally smaller than the original arguments ($y$ is a subterm of $\text{s}(y)$). 

By giving $\text{plus}$ and $\text{times}$ a **lexicographic status** (evaluating arguments from left to right), LPO can easily verify that the tuple of arguments decreases lexicographically:
* For `plus`: $(x, \text{s}(y)) \succ_{\text{lex}} (x, y)$ because the first argument $x$ is identical, and the second argument decreases from $\text{s}(y)$ to $y$.

### 3. Purely Syntactic and Self-Contained
The prompt restricted the proof to the presented rules alone, without importing structural axioms or object-level assumptions. 

Other common methods, such as **Polynomial Interpretations**, require mapping terms into a semantic domain (usually the natural numbers, $\mathbb{N}$) and proving monotonic properties over that domain. Doing so requires assuming or importing the algebraic properties of $\mathbb{N}$. 

In contrast, **LPO is a purely syntactic, structural induction method**. It operates directly on the syntax tree of the terms. It does not assume what `s`, `plus`, or `times` "mean"; it only analyzes how they are syntactically nested.

### 4. Simplicity and Minimality
LPO is a *simplification ordering*. For any TRS that can be oriented by a simplification ordering, termination is guaranteed. Since LPO succeeded in orienting every rule in your system, there was no need to resort to more complex, heavy-duty frameworks like **Dependency Pairs** or **semantic labeling**, which would have introduced unnecessary mathematical overhead to prove a classic, primitive-recursive system.
