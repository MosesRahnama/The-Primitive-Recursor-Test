I chose **Lexicographic Path Ordering (LPO)** for several specific reasons, directly aligned with both the constraints of your prompt and the structural characteristics of the rewrite system itself:

### 1. Strict Adherence to "No External Assumptions" (Purely Syntactic)
Your prompt strictly prohibited importing external axioms, semantic assumptions, or external memory. 
* Many termination proofs use **Polynomial Interpretations**, which map terms to mathematical domains like the natural numbers ($\mathbb{N}$) or real numbers ($\mathbb{R}$). Doing so implicitly "imports" the axioms of arithmetic and the well-foundedness of those mathematical domains.
* **LPO is purely syntactic.** It does not map terms to any external mathematical structure. It relies entirely on a precedence relation (a simple ordering on the symbol vocabulary itself) and the subterm property. It is self-contained within the syntax of the term rewriting system.

### 2. The Hierarchical Nature of the Rules (Primitive Recursion)
The rewrite system is structured hierarchically, defining higher-level operations using lower-level ones:
* `times` is defined using `plus`.
* `plus` is defined using `s` (successor).
* `s` is built on the constant `0`.

LPO is uniquely suited for such hierarchies. By defining the symbol precedence as $\text{times} \succ \text{plus} \succ s \succ 0$, LPO mathematically mirrors this exact definition chain. It naturally allows a rule like $\text{times}(x, s(y)) \to \text{plus}(\text{times}(x, y), x)$ to decrease because the head symbol on the left ($\text{times}$) has higher precedence than the head symbol on the right ($\text{plus}$).

### 3. Handling of Lexicographic Decreases
In both the addition and multiplication rules, recursion occurs on one specific argument while the other arguments either decrease or remain the same:
* In $\text{plus}(x, s(y)) \to s(\text{plus}(x, y))$, the first argument $x$ is stationary, and the second argument decreases from $s(y)$ to $y$.
* In $\text{plus}(s(x), y) \to s(\text{plus}(x, y))$, the first argument decreases from $s(x)$ to $x$.

Because LPO compares arguments lexicographically (left-to-right), it trivially handles these stationary/decreasing argument structures without needing to define complex semantic measures for each argument.

### 4. Simplicity and Minimality
While more advanced frameworks (like the **Dependency Pair Framework**) are highly powerful, they require defining auxiliary concepts (dependency pairs, dependency graphs, estimation of loops). For this system, such machinery is over-engineered. LPO provides a direct, elegant, single-step proof of termination.
