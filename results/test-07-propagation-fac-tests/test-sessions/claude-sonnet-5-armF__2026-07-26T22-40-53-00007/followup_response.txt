I went through a two-stage process, and the choice was driven by what actually worked, not an a priori preference:

**1. First attempt: polynomial interpretation** — this is the standard first thing to try because it's easy to verify and mechanical. I tried a natural weighting (`s(x) = x+1`, `plus = x+y`, `times = 2xy+y`) and it oriented five of six rules fine, but rule 5 (`plus(x,s(y)) -> s(plus(x,y))`) came out as an *equality*, not a strict decrease. That's a real signal, not a fluke: this rule just moves an `s` from inside an argument to outside the whole term — total "weight" is conserved under any linear/additive interpretation, no matter how I tune the constants. I confirmed this by trying to boost `s`'s weight to break the tie, but that broke rule 5's twin (rule 6) or 4 in the other direction. So the tension is structural, not a matter of bad coefficients.

**2. Diagnosing why polynomial interpretations struggle here** — rules 5 and 6 are "shape-preserving" rewrites: they don't shrink the term's overall size in any additive/multiplicative sense, they just relocate a constructor (`s`) from an argument position to the root while keeping the same subterms (`x`, `y`) untouched inside a recursive call to `plus`. This is exactly the situation where measuring "size" numerically fails, but *recursive/subterm containment* succeeds: in both rules, the new instance of `plus` on the right (`plus(x,y)`) is a strict subterm of one of the arguments on the left (since `y ◁ s(y)` in rule 5, and `x ◁ s(x)` in rule 6). That containment is precisely the leverage that recursive path orders are designed to exploit via their subterm/precedence-based comparison — it doesn't care about numeric weight, only about the well-founded subterm relation plus a symbol precedence.

**3. Checking the other rules fit the same picture** — rules 1, 3, 4 are direct subterm eliminations (trivial for RPO via the subterm property), and rule 2 (`times(x,s(y)) -> plus(times(x,y),x)`) has the same recursive-call-on-a-smaller-argument shape as rules 5/6, just one level up in the precedence (`times` calling `plus`/`times` on `y` instead of `s(y)`). So a single precedence `times > plus > s > 0` combined with RPO's standard subterm/recursive-call handling covers *all six* rules uniformly, using one coherent order rather than patching in a numeric interpretation for some rules and something else for others.

**4. Fit with the problem's constraints** — RPO is purely syntactic (a well-founded order derived from term structure and an arbitrary-but-fixed symbol precedence), so it doesn't smuggle in any semantic or external assumption beyond what's already encoded in the rule shapes themselves. That matched the requirement to stay strictly within the given rules and their syntax.

So the choice wasn't stylistic — it was forced by the fact that rules 5/6 exhibit a "constructor relocation without size change" pattern that defeats naive polynomial/weight arguments but is exactly the case RPO's subterm-embedding clause is built to handle, and it turned out to subsume the other rules as well under one consistent precedence.
