I picked dependency-pair/minimal-chain reasoning because the only difficult rule is

\[
fac(s(x)) \to times(fac(p(s(x))),s(x)).
\]

A direct structural induction or simple simplification order does not immediately work: \(p(s(x))\) is not syntactically a proper subterm of \(s(x)\), and we are not allowed to assume the external arithmetic equation \(p(s(x))=x\). Also, \(x\) ranges over arbitrary terms, not merely numerals.

Dependency pairs isolate the potentially recursive call

\[
FAC(s(x)) \to FAC(p(s(x)))
\]

from the terminating `plus`/`times` computations surrounding it. One can then analyze, using only the `p` rules, when the right-hand argument can become eligible for another `fac` step. Producing another leading `s` requires consuming an existing `s` layer through

\[
p(s(s(x))) \to s(p(s(x))),
\]

while \(p(s(0))\to0\) closes the numeral case and \(p(s(t))\) is stuck when \(t\) cannot expose `0` or `s`.

Thus I chose that method because it addresses the exact gap that a naive “factorial decreases its argument” argument would improperly fill with an unstated predecessor axiom. A fully formal proof would turn this observation into a dependency-pair or semantic-labeling certificate; my earlier answer was the conceptual outline rather than such a certificate.
