I chose the **Lexicographic Path Ordering (LPO)** because it is the most direct, purely syntactic method that perfectly aligns with the specific structural recursion pattern of this Term Rewriting System (TRS). 

Here is the step-by-step breakdown of why I selected this specific method over the alternatives.

### 1. Why Path Orderings (over Polynomial Interpretations)
The given TRS is a standard definition of addition and multiplication over Peano naturals. The recursive calls (`times(x, y)` and `plus(x, y)`) strictly deconstruct the `s(...)` constructors of their arguments. 

Because the termination relies entirely on this **structural descent** (peeling off `s` constructors) rather than numerical magnitude, syntactic path orderings are the natural fit. Using a semantic method like Polynomial Interpretations would require artificially guessing or computing numerical coefficients (e.g., mapping `times` to $x \cdot y + x + y$), which is overkill and obscures the purely structural nature of the recursion.

### 2. Why Lexicographic (LPO) over Multiset (MPO/RPO)
This is the most critical technical reason. When using Path Orderings, you must choose a status for the function symbols: either **Multiset** (MPO) or **Lexicographic** (LPO). 

**Multiset Path Ordering (MPO) fails on Rule 2.** 
Look at the multiplication rule:
`times(x, s(y)) -> plus(times(x, y), x)`

In MPO, to prove the left-hand side is greater than the right-hand side, the multiset of LHS arguments `{x, s(y)}` must strictly cover the multiset of RHS arguments `{times(x, y), x}`. 
* The `x` on both sides cancels out.
* This leaves the requirement that **`s(y) > times(x, y)`**.
* However, `times` is a "higher-level" function that calls `plus`, which calls `s`. Therefore, in any valid precedence, `times` must be strictly greater than `s`. It is impossible for `s(y)` to be greater than `times(x, y)` under MPO. Thus, MPO cannot prove this system terminating.

**Lexicographic Path Ordering (LPO) succeeds.**
LPO solves this by comparing arguments from left to right. 
* For `times(x, s(y)) > times(x, y)`, LPO compares the first arguments: `x` and `x`. They are identical, so it moves to the second arguments: `s(y)` and `y`. 
* Since `s(y) > y` by the subterm property, the lexicographic comparison succeeds immediately. LPO perfectly captures the pattern of "recursing on the second argument while holding the first argument constant."

### 3. Why LPO over Knuth-Bendix Ordering (KBO)
Knuth-Bendix Ordering is another excellent syntactic method, and it *could* technically be forced to work here. However, KBO requires assigning integer weights to every function symbol and variable, and then doing arithmetic to ensure the LHS weight is strictly greater than the RHS weight. 

For Rule 2, because the RHS introduces an extra variable `x` (which adds to the weight), you would have to assign a disproportionately large integer weight to the `s` constructor to ensure the LHS weight outweighs the RHS. Furthermore, standard KBO has strict admissibility conditions (e.g., binary symbols usually must have a weight $\ge 1$). LPO is purely qualitative and symbolic—it only requires a simple precedence hierarchy—making the proof much cleaner and easier to verify by hand without doing integer arithmetic.

### 4. How the Precedence was Chosen
The precedence `times > plus > s > 0` was not guessed; it is directly derived from the **dependency graph** of the rewrite rules:
1. **`times > plus`**: Rule 2 shows `times` rewriting to a term containing `plus`. Therefore, `times` must be strictly greater than `plus` to ensure the "outer" function is larger than the "inner" function it generates.
2. **`plus > s`**: Rules 5 and 6 show `plus` rewriting to a term with `s` on the outside (`s(plus(...))`). Therefore, `plus` must be strictly greater than `s`.
3. **`s > 0`**: `0` is a base constant with no arguments, naturally sitting at the bottom of the structural hierarchy.

### Summary
I chose LPO because the system is structurally recursive (ruling out the need for polynomials), it features recursion on a secondary argument while holding the primary argument constant (which breaks Multiset orderings and mandates Lexicographic order), and it avoids the tedious integer-weight bookkeeping required by Knuth-Bendix.
