Use the **lexicographic path ordering (LPO)** with lexicographic status for all symbols and precedence

\[
\mathsf{times} \succ \mathsf{plus} \succ \mathsf{s} \succ 0.
\]

LPO is a well-founded reduction ordering on finite first-order terms, closed under contexts and substitutions. It also has the strict subterm property.

Let \(>\) denote this ordering.

1. \(\mathsf{times}(x,0)\to 0\)

   The right-hand side is a proper subterm of the left-hand side, so

   \[
   \mathsf{times}(x,0)>0.
   \]

2. \(\mathsf{times}(x,\mathsf{s}(y))
   \to \mathsf{plus}(\mathsf{times}(x,y),x)\)

   First,

   \[
   \mathsf{times}(x,\mathsf{s}(y))>\mathsf{times}(x,y),
   \]

   because the roots agree, the first arguments agree, and
   \(\mathsf{s}(y)>y\) by the subterm property. Also,

   \[
   \mathsf{times}(x,\mathsf{s}(y))>x
   \]

   by the subterm property. Since \(\mathsf{times}\succ\mathsf{plus}\), the left-hand side is therefore greater than the entire right-hand side.

3. \(\mathsf{plus}(x,0)\to x\)

   Here \(x\) is a proper subterm:

   \[
   \mathsf{plus}(x,0)>x.
   \]

4. \(\mathsf{plus}(0,x)\to x\)

   Again, by the subterm property:

   \[
   \mathsf{plus}(0,x)>x.
   \]

5. \(\mathsf{plus}(x,\mathsf{s}(y))
   \to \mathsf{s}(\mathsf{plus}(x,y))\)

   Lexicographically,

   \[
   \mathsf{plus}(x,\mathsf{s}(y))>\mathsf{plus}(x,y)
   \]

   because \(\mathsf{s}(y)>y\). Since \(\mathsf{plus}\succ\mathsf{s}\), this gives

   \[
   \mathsf{plus}(x,\mathsf{s}(y))
   >\mathsf{s}(\mathsf{plus}(x,y)).
   \]

6. \(\mathsf{plus}(\mathsf{s}(x),y)
   \to \mathsf{s}(\mathsf{plus}(x,y))\)

   Lexicographically,

   \[
   \mathsf{plus}(\mathsf{s}(x),y)>\mathsf{plus}(x,y)
   \]

   because \(\mathsf{s}(x)>x\). Again using
   \(\mathsf{plus}\succ\mathsf{s}\),

   \[
   \mathsf{plus}(\mathsf{s}(x),y)
   >\mathsf{s}(\mathsf{plus}(x,y)).
   \]

Thus every rewrite step strictly decreases the same well-founded reduction ordering. Consequently, there can be no infinite rewrite sequence, so the system is terminating on all first-order terms.
