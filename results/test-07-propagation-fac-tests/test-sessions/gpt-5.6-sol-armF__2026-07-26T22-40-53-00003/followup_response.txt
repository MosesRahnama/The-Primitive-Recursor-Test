I picked **lexicographic path ordering (LPO)** because the rules have exactly the syntactic shape LPO handles well:

1. **Recursive calls decrease a constructor argument.**
   - `times(x, s(y))` recursively uses `times(x, y)`.
   - `plus(x, s(y))` recursively uses `plus(x, y)`.
   - `plus(s(x), y)` recursively uses `plus(x, y)`.

   The subterm property gives \(s(y)>y\) and \(s(x)>x\), while lexicographic comparison transfers those decreases to the enclosing `times` or `plus` terms.

2. **Recursive calls may be wrapped in lower-precedence symbols.**
   - The recursive `times` call is placed under `plus`.
   - The recursive `plus` calls are placed under `s`.

   The precedence

   \[
   \mathsf{times}\succ\mathsf{plus}\succ\mathsf{s}\succ 0
   \]

   directly reflects this nesting: a call headed by a higher-precedence defined symbol dominates an expression constructed around smaller recursive calls using lower-precedence symbols.

3. **The base rules are immediate by the subterm property.**
   Rules such as `plus(x, 0) -> x` require no semantic interpretation; the right side is simply a subterm of the left side.

4. **Simpler measures such as raw term size do not work uniformly.**
   For example,

   \[
   \mathsf{times}(x,\mathsf{s}(y))
   \to
   \mathsf{plus}(\mathsf{times}(x,y),x)
   \]

   can increase term size because `x` is duplicated. LPO can nevertheless prove a decrease because it tracks recursive structure and symbol precedence rather than merely counting symbols.

5. **It stays fully within the requested boundary.**
   LPO treats `0`, `s`, `plus`, and `times` as uninterpreted first-order symbols. It does not assume that terms denote natural numbers, nor use associativity, commutativity, arithmetic induction, constructor disjointness, or any unlisted equations.

Other methods—such as dependency pairs or a carefully designed interpretation—might also work, but LPO is the most direct fit: one precedence and lexicographic status orient all six rules uniformly and syntactically.
