I chose the **Recursive Path Ordering (RPO)** (specifically with a lexicographic status, often referred to as LPO) because the given Term Rewriting System (TRS) is a textbook example of **stratified primitive recursion**, and RPO is the most natural, lightweight, and structurally aligned tool for this exact pattern. 

Here is the detailed breakdown of why this method was the optimal choice:

### 1. The Precedence is Dictated by the Stratification
The system defines multiplication (`times`) in terms of addition (`plus`), and addition in terms of the successor function (`s`). This strict stratification naturally forces the precedence relation:
* Look at the `times` rule: `times(...) -> plus(...)`. For any path ordering to orient this, the root symbol of the LHS must be strictly greater than the root symbol of the RHS. This demands **`times > plus`**.
* Look at the `plus` rules: `plus(...) -> s(...)`. By the exact same logic, this demands **`plus > s`**.

Because the rules themselves practically spell out the required precedence (`times > plus > s`), RPO is the path of least resistance. It allows the higher-precedence symbol to "distribute" over the lower-precedence one without needing complex arithmetic.

### 2. RPO Elegantly Handles Variable "Duplication"
Consider the recursive rule for multiplication:
`times(x, s(y)) -> plus(times(x, y), x)`

Notice that the variable `x` appears **once** on the left-hand side, but **twice** on the right-hand side (once inside the recursive `times` call, and once as the second argument to `plus`). 
* If you tried to use a simple **symbol-counting metric** (e.g., "the LHS must have more symbols than the RHS"), it would fail. If `x` is instantiated with a massively deep term, the RHS will have more symbols than the LHS due to the duplicated `x`.
* RPO bypasses this entirely. It does not count symbols; it only requires that the recursive arguments strictly decrease (`s(y) > y`) and that the root symbols respect the precedence (`times > plus`). It is completely blind to how many times a variable is copied, making it perfectly suited for this rule.

### 3. "Constructor Peeling" Matches RPO's Subterm Logic
The rules for `plus` (Rules 5 and 6) work by "peeling" an `s` off of an argument and moving it to the outside of the term:
`plus(x, s(y)) -> s(plus(x, y))`

Path orderings are explicitly designed to handle this structural manipulation. Because `plus > s`, RPO allows the `s` to be moved to the outside as long as the arguments inside the recursive `plus` call are strictly smaller than the arguments of the original `plus` call. The lexicographic comparison of the arguments `(x, s(y))` vs `(x, y)` trivially proves that the arguments are shrinking, satisfying the ordering conditions effortlessly.

### 4. Parsimony: Avoiding Heavier Machinery
When selecting a termination proof method, it is best to use the lightest tool that gets the job done. I considered and rejected other standard methods for the following reasons:
* **Polynomial Interpretations:** This would work (e.g., interpreting `plus` as $x+y$ and `times` as $x \cdot y + x$), but it requires guessing the correct polynomials, setting up algebraic inequalities, and verifying them. It is tedious to write out and verify by hand compared to a simple precedence check.
* **Knuth-Bendix Ordering (KBO):** KBO would also work, but it requires defining *both* a precedence relation and a set of numerical weights for every function symbol. RPO achieves the proof using *only* the precedence relation, making it more parsimonious.
* **Dependency Pairs:** This is a heavy-duty, advanced framework used when simple orderings fail (e.g., when arguments are permuted in complex ways, or when there is mutual recursion that isn't strictly stratified). Because our recursion is strictly stratified and arguments only shrink in a straightforward lexicographic way, Dependency Pairs would be massive overkill.

### Summary
I picked RPO because it is the "golden hammer" for primitive recursive arithmetic definitions. The syntax of the rules naturally dictates the precedence (`times > plus > s`), the structural recursion perfectly aligns with RPO's subterm and lexicographic conditions, and it effortlessly sidesteps the variable-duplication trap that defeats simpler size-based metrics.
