Great question! The choice of termination-proof method depends on the structure of the rewrite system and the nature of the recursive calls. Here’s why I leaned toward **recursive path orders (RPO/LPO)** and **polynomial interpretations** for this system:

---

### **1. Why Recursive Path Orders (RPO/LPO)?**
#### **Advantages for this system:**
- **Natural fit for constructor-based recursion**:
  The system is built around constructors (`0`, `s`) and defined functions (`plus`, `times`, `p`, `fac`) that recurse on subterms. RPO/LPO are designed to handle such cases by comparing terms based on a **precedence** (priority order) of function symbols and the structure of their arguments.

- **Handles lexicographic and multiset recursion**:
  - The `times` rule (`times(s(x), y) → plus(times(x, y), y)`) recurses on the first argument (`x` vs. `s(x)`), which is a **lexicographic** decrease (first argument gets smaller).
  - The `fac` rule (`fac(s(x)) → times(fac(p(s(x))), s(x))`) recurses on `p(s(x))`, which is smaller than `s(x)` in the number of `s` constructors. This is also a lexicographic decrease.
  RPO/LPO can capture both types of recursion.

- **No need for numeric interpretations**:
  Unlike polynomial interpretations, RPO/LPO don’t require assigning numbers to terms or proving inequalities. They work purely syntactically by comparing term structure.

#### **How it would work here:**
- **Precedence**: `fac > times > plus > p > s > 0`.
  - This ensures that `fac` is "bigger" than `times`, `times` is bigger than `plus`, etc.
- **Status**:
  - `plus` and `times` would use **lexicographic status** (left-to-right argument comparison) because their recursion depends on the first or second argument.
  - `p` and `fac` would use **multiset status** (since their recursion is on a single argument that shrinks in size).

#### **Example for `fac`:**
- Compare `fac(s(x))` (LHS) and `times(fac(p(s(x))), s(x))` (RHS).
  - `fac > times` in precedence, so LHS > RHS by the subterm property (since `s(x)` is a subterm of `fac(s(x))` and `times` is smaller than `fac`).
  - The recursive call `fac(p(s(x)))` is smaller than `fac(s(x))` because `p(s(x))` is a proper subterm of `s(x)` (and `p < fac` in precedence).

---

### **2. Why Polynomial Interpretations?**
#### **Advantages for this system:**
- **Intuitive for arithmetic-like functions**:
  The system defines arithmetic operations (`plus`, `times`, `fac`), which naturally map to polynomial interpretations. For example:
  - `0` → `0`, `s(x)` → `x + 1`, `plus(x, y)` → `x + y`, `times(x, y)` → `x * y`.
  - `p(x)` can be interpreted as `max(x - 1, 0)` (to handle `p(0)`).
  - `fac(x)` can be interpreted as `x!` (factorial), which grows faster than any polynomial but is still well-founded.

- **Handles nested recursion**:
  The `fac` rule involves nested calls to `p` and `times`, but polynomial interpretations can account for this by ensuring the interpretation of the RHS is strictly smaller than the LHS.

#### **Example for `fac`:**
- Interpret `fac(x)` as `x!` (factorial).
- The rule `fac(s(x)) → times(fac(p(s(x))), s(x))` becomes:
  - LHS: `(x + 1)!`
  - RHS: `p(s(x))! * (x + 1) = x! * (x + 1) = (x + 1)!`.
  - This seems like an equality, but we can adjust the interpretation to ensure strict decrease. For example:
    - Interpret `fac(x)` as `2^(2^x)` (a rapidly growing function).
    - Then `fac(s(x)) = 2^(2^(x+1))` and `times(fac(p(s(x))), s(x)) = 2^(2^x) * (x + 1) < 2^(2^(x+1))` for all `x ≥ 0`.

#### **Why not use this as the primary method?**
- Polynomial interpretations require more "guesswork" (choosing the right polynomials) and can be less intuitive for non-arithmetic functions.
- They may not always work if the system has non-arithmetic recursion (though this one does).

---

### **3. Why Not Other Methods?**
#### **Dependency Pairs (DP):**
- DP is powerful for complex systems with mutual recursion, but this system is simple enough that RPO/LPO or polynomial interpretations suffice.
- DP would be overkill here and require more effort to set up.

#### **Semantic Methods (e.g., well-founded algebras):**
- These are more general but also more abstract. For this system, syntactic methods (RPO/LPO) or simple polynomial interpretations are easier to apply.

#### **Simplification Orders (e.g., Knuth-Bendix Order):**
- These require assigning weights to function symbols, which is less intuitive than RPO/LPO for this case.

---

### **4. Why I Chose RPO/LPO as the Primary Method**
1. **Simplicity**: RPO/LPO are purely syntactic and don’t require numeric interpretations. They work by comparing term structure, which is natural for constructor-based systems.
2. **Directness**: The system’s recursion is clearly oriented toward decreasing arguments (e.g., `s(x)` → `x` in `p`, `s(x)` → `x` in `times`), which RPO/LPO can capture with a simple precedence.
3. **No guesswork**: Unlike polynomial interpretations, RPO/LPO don’t require choosing specific polynomials or proving inequalities.
4. **Standard tool**: RPO/LPO are the "go-to" methods for proving termination of first-order rewrite systems, especially when the rules are oriented toward constructor recursion.

---

### **5. Why Polynomial Interpretations Are Also Viable**
- They provide a **semantic** proof of termination, which can be more intuitive for arithmetic-like functions.
- They can handle cases where RPO/LPO might struggle (e.g., if the system had more complex interactions between functions).
- For this system, they work but require slightly more effort to set up.

---

### **Final Answer:**
I picked **RPO/LPO** as the primary method because:
1. The system is constructor-based and recursively defined, which aligns perfectly with RPO/LPO’s strengths.
2. The recursion is straightforward (arguments decrease in size or lexicographic order), and RPO/LPO can capture this with a simple precedence.
3. It’s the most direct and standard approach for such systems, avoiding the need for numeric interpretations or guesswork.

Polynomial interpretations are also valid but are better suited for cases where RPO/LPO might not apply (e.g., if the system had more complex interactions or non-arithmetic recursion). For this system, RPO/LPO are the "right tool for the job."
