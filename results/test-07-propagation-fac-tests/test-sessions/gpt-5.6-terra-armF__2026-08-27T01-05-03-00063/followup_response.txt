I picked LPO because the rules have the characteristic shape that LPO handles directly:

- Recursive calls occur under symbols of lower precedence:
  - `times(...)` rewrites to a `plus(...)` expression.
  - `plus(...)` rewrites to an `s(...)` expression.
- Where the recursive call keeps the same root symbol, one argument becomes structurally smaller:
  - `times(x, s(y))` contains `times(x, y)`.
  - `plus(x, s(y))` contains `plus(x, y)`.
  - `plus(s(x), y)` contains `plus(x, y)`.
- The remaining rules reduce directly to a variable or constant.

Thus the precedence

\[
\mathsf{times} > \mathsf{plus} > \mathsf{s} > \mathsf{0}
\]

matches the syntactic “call hierarchy” visible in the rules themselves. No semantic interpretation of `0`, `s`, `plus`, or `times` as natural-number operations is required.

I did not choose a simple term-size argument because some rules increase raw syntactic size. For example,

\[
\mathsf{times}(x,\mathsf{s}(y))
\to
\mathsf{plus}(\mathsf{times}(x,y),x)
\]

duplicates `x` syntactically: the left side has one occurrence of `x`, whereas the right side has two. Likewise,

\[
\mathsf{plus}(x,\mathsf{s}(y))
\to
\mathsf{s}(\mathsf{plus}(x,y))
\]

need not strictly reduce a naïve count of symbols. So ordinary size decrease is not an adequate proof.

LPO is suitable precisely because it is sensitive to both:

1. **root-symbol precedence** (`times` above `plus`, `plus` above `s`), and  
2. **structural descent in recursive arguments** (`s(y) > y`, `s(x) > x`),

rather than merely total term size.

Other standard methods could also potentially prove termination—for example, a suitable polynomial or matrix interpretation—but LPO is especially direct here because it follows the rewrite rules’ syntactic recursion pattern and needs no assumptions beyond the finite signature and the displayed rules.
