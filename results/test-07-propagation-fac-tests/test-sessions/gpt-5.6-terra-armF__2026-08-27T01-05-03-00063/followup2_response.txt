It uses only a **meta-level syntactic termination ordering** on the finite first-order terms of the given signature. It does not add, assume, or derive any object-level equations or rewrite rules such as associativity, commutativity, distributivity, identities for `0`, or intended natural-number semantics.

In particular, the proof does **not** assume that:

- `0` denotes a natural number;
- `s` is successor;
- `plus` is associative, commutative, or has `0` as an identity beyond the two displayed rewrite rules;
- `times` has any arithmetic interpretation;
- arguments are ground, constructor terms, normal forms, or numerals;
- rewriting follows a particular strategy.

The selected precedence

\[
\mathsf{times} > \mathsf{plus} > \mathsf{s} > \mathsf{0}
\]

and lexicographic statuses are not extra rules or object-level assumptions. They are parameters of the external proof ordering used to show that every displayed rewrite step decreases in a well-founded relation. They do not alter which terms rewrite, create new rewrites, restrict rule applicability, or impose guards.

The only background framework used is the ordinary definition of a first-order TRS over finite terms, plus the standard theorem:

> If every rule \(l \to r\) is strictly decreasing under a well-founded reduction ordering, then the TRS terminates.

LPO supplies such an ordering. Its use of facts such as

\[
\mathsf{s}(y) > y
\]

is not an object-level axiom saying that successor is numerically greater than its argument. It is merely the syntactic subterm property of the ordering: a compound term is greater than each of its proper syntactic subterms.

So, within the requested boundary, the proof is valid: termination follows from the syntactic form of the six rules and a standard external well-founded ordering, without strengthening the rewrite system or importing semantic assumptions.
