I chose the **Dependency Pair (DP) framework combined with a weakly monotonic algebra** because standard, simpler termination methods—such as syntactic path orderings or direct polynomial interpretations—fail on this specific system due to two major obstacles: **nested defined symbols** and **super-exponential growth**. 

Here is a breakdown of why alternative methods fail and why the DP framework was the perfect tool for the job.

### 1. Why Syntactic Orderings (LPO / MPO / KBO) Fail
To use Lexicographic Path Ordering (LPO) or Multiset Path Ordering (MPO), we would need to assign a strict precedence to the function symbols. 

Look at the `p` rule:
`p(s(s(x))) -> s(p(s(x)))`
For LPO to orient this rule, we must assign a precedence where the outer symbol on the left is greater than the outer symbol on the right: **`p > s`**.

Now look at the `fac` rule:
`fac(s(x)) -> times(fac(p(s(x))), s(x))`
To orient this rule, LPO requires that the argument of `fac` on the left is strictly greater than the argument of `fac` on the right: `s(x) >_lpo p(s(x))`.
Because `p` is at the root of the right side, LPO would require **`s > p`**. 

We now have a contradiction: `p > s` and `s > p`. Because `p` (a defined symbol) is nested inside the recursive call of `fac`, simple syntactic path orderings get stuck in a precedence loop and fail completely.

### 2. Why Direct Polynomial Interpretations Fail
If we tried to use a standard polynomial interpretation over the natural numbers without the Dependency Pair framework, we would have to prove that the left side is *strictly* greater than the right side ($L > R$) for every single rule.

Look at the `fac` rule again:
`fac(s(x)) -> times(fac(p(s(x))), s(x))`
If we map these to polynomials, `times` must be interpreted as actual multiplication (or something similar) to satisfy its own rules. Thus, the interpretation of `fac(x+1)` must be strictly greater than `fac(x) * (x+1)`.

This requires the mathematical function for `fac` to grow super-exponentially (like a true mathematical factorial). But polynomials cannot grow super-exponentially! A standard polynomial interpretation tool would search infinitely and fail because no polynomial can bound a factorial function.

### 3. Why the Dependency Pair (DP) Framework Succeeds
The DP framework is essentially a "divide and conquer" method that was invented to solve exactly these problems.

By extracting the Dependency Pairs, the framework allows us to split the termination proof into two distinct requirements:
1.  **Weak decrease ($L \ge R$)** for the original rules.
2.  **Strict decrease ($L > R$)** for the recursive loops (the Dependency Pairs).

This relaxation from strictly greater ($>$) to greater-than-or-equal-to ($\ge$) for the original rules is a superpower. It allowed me to assign the interpretation **`[fac](x) = 0`**. 
By doing this, I completely neutralized the super-exponential growth of the factorial rule. The requirement `fac(s(x)) >= times(fac(p(s(x))), s(x))` collapsed mathematically to `0 >= 0 * (x + 1)`, which is trivially true. 

Because I didn't have to evaluate the "result" of `fac`, I only had to prove that the *arguments* being passed into the recursive loop were getting strictly smaller. That is handled by the DP:
`FAC(s(x)) -> FAC(p(s(x)))`
By assigning the tuple symbol `[FAC](x) = x`, the DP requirement collapsed to `x + 1 > x`, which is a simple linear polynomial.

**Summary:** 
I chose this method because the nested `p` defeats structural orderings, and the factorial growth defeats direct polynomial interpretations. The DP framework elegantly bypasses both by allowing us to ignore the size of the output (by setting `fac=0`) and focus exclusively on the size of the inputs driving the recursion.
