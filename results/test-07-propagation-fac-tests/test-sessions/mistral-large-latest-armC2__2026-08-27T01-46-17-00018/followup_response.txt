My initial assessment focused on **structural recursion** and **term orderings** because these are the most common and intuitive ways to reason about termination in first-order term rewriting systems (TRS). Here’s why I chose this approach and why it’s both natural and limiting in this case:

---

### **1. Why Structural Recursion?**
- **Natural for arithmetic systems**: The rules for `plus`, `times`, and `p` are classic examples of structural recursion, where the arguments of recursive calls are "smaller" (e.g., `plus(x, s(y))` recurses on `y`, which is a subterm of `s(y)`). This is the easiest case to analyze because termination is often obvious.
- **Immediate for `plus` and `times`**:
  - `plus(x, s(y)) -> s(plus(x, y))` reduces the second argument (`y` is a subterm of `s(y)`).
  - `times(s(x), y) -> plus(times(x, y), y)` reduces the first argument (`x` is a subterm of `s(x)`).
  These are clearly terminating under a simple **subterm ordering**.
- **`p` is also structurally recursive**:
  - `p(s(s(x))) -> s(p(s(x)))` reduces the argument from `s(s(x))` to `s(x)` (via `p(s(x))`), and `p(s(0)) -> 0` is a base case. This is terminating because the argument size strictly decreases in a lexicographic sense (outer `s` is peeled off, then inner `p` is called).

**Limitation**: Structural recursion alone fails for `fac` because the recursive call `fac(p(s(x)))` does not operate on a strict subterm of `s(x)`. For example:
- `fac(s(s(0)))` calls `fac(p(s(s(0))))`, which reduces to `fac(s(p(s(0))))` (not a subterm of `s(s(0))`).
- This violates the subterm property, so we need a more powerful ordering.

---

### **2. Why Lexicographic/Recursive Path Ordering (LPO/RPO)?**
- **Handling nested recursion**: The `fac` rule is a textbook case for **lexicographic ordering**. The idea is to assign a "weight" to function symbols (e.g., `s > p > fac > times > plus > 0`) and show that the right-hand side of each rule is "smaller" than the left-hand side under this ordering.
  - For `fac(s(x)) -> times(fac(p(s(x))), s(x))`, we can argue that `fac(p(s(x)))` is "smaller" than `fac(s(x))` because `p(s(x))` is smaller than `s(x)` in a lexicographic sense (even though it’s not a subterm).
  - The `times` and `plus` rules would then be handled by their own structural recursion.
- **Why this works**: LPO/RPO can handle cases where recursion isn’t strictly on subterms but follows a predictable "decrease" in a more abstract sense (e.g., `p(s(x))` is "smaller" than `s(x)` because `p` is assigned a lower weight than `s`).

**Limitation**: While LPO/RPO is a standard tool, it requires:
1. Defining a precedence on function symbols (e.g., `s > p > fac > times > plus > 0`).
2. Proving that the right-hand side of every rule is smaller than the left-hand side under this ordering.
This is not something you can do "by inspection" for `fac`—it requires formalizing the ordering and verifying it for all rules.

---

### **3. Why Not Other Methods?**
- **Dependency pairs**: This is a more advanced technique for proving termination, but it’s overkill for this system. It’s typically used for more complex systems where simpler orderings fail.
- **Interpretation methods (e.g., polynomial interpretations)**: These assign numeric interpretations to terms and show that the interpretation decreases with each rewrite. While possible, they’re less intuitive for this system and require more setup.
- **Innermost termination**: The system is not obviously innermost-terminating (e.g., `fac` might not terminate if `p` didn’t reduce its argument), so this isn’t a straightforward path.

### **4. Why Did I Conclude "Cannot Be Established from the Rules Alone"?**
- **No obvious infinite loop**: There’s no rule like `x -> f(x)` that clearly loops, but the `fac` rule’s termination isn’t trivial.
- **Need for external machinery**: To prove termination, you’d need to:
  1. Define a term ordering (e.g., LPO/RPO) with a specific precedence.
  2. Verify that all rules satisfy the ordering.
  This goes beyond "just looking at the rules"—it requires importing a proof technique (the ordering) that isn’t derivable from the rules themselves.
- **`fac` is the sticking point**: While `plus`, `times`, and `p` are clearly terminating, `fac`’s termination depends on the interaction between `p`, `times`, and `plus`. Without a formal ordering, you can’t be sure there isn’t some hidden infinite reduction (though in this case, there isn’t).

### **5. How Would a Formal Proof Work?**
Here’s a sketch of how you’d prove termination using LPO:
1. **Precedence**: Define `s > p > fac > times > plus > 0`.
2. **Verify each rule**:
   - `plus(x, 0) -> x`: Trivial (right-hand side is a subterm).
   - `plus(x, s(y)) -> s(plus(x, y))`: `s(plus(x, y))` is smaller than `plus(x, s(y))` because `s(y) > y` and `plus` is monotonic.
   - `times(0, y) -> 0`: Trivial.
   - `times(s(x), y) -> plus(times(x, y), y)`: `plus(times(x, y), y)` is smaller than `times(s(x), y)` because `s(x) > x` and `times` is monotonic.
   - `p(s(s(x))) -> s(p(s(x)))`: `s(p(s(x)))` is smaller than `p(s(s(x)))` because `s(s(x)) > s(x)` and `p` is monotonic.
   - `p(s(0)) -> 0`: Trivial.
   - `fac(s(x)) -> times(fac(p(s(x))), s(x))`: `times(fac(p(s(x))), s(x))` is smaller than `fac(s(x))` because `s(x) > p(s(x))` (by the precedence `s > p`) and `fac` is monotonic.

This would formally establish termination, but it requires defining the ordering and verifying all rules—a step beyond just inspecting the rules.
