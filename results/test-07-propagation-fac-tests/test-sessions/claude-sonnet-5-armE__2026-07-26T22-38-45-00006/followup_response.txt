## Why LPO?

**1. The recursion structure is exactly what LPO is designed for.**

Looking at Rule 2:
```
F(x, y, S(n)) -> G(y, F(x, y, n))
```
the only thing that "shrinks" from left to right is the third argument of `F`, which goes from `S(n)` down to its immediate subterm `n`. Everything else (`x`, `y`) is passed through unchanged, and a *new* symbol `G` is introduced wrapping the recursive call. This is the classic pattern of **primitive recursion over a datatype** (`Z`/`S`), and LPO is precisely the tool built to certify termination of primitive-recursive-looking definitions: it lets you say "the head symbol got smaller/stayed controlled by precedence, AND/OR the arguments got smaller in an appropriate order," without needing to invent an explicit measure or a manual well-founded relation.

**2. No polynomial/size interpretation was needed — precedence did the work.**

An alternative would have been a polynomial interpretation (e.g., assign weights to `F`, `G`, `S`, `Z` and check the inequalities). But that requires *guessing numeric coefficients* satisfying nonlinear constraints, which is extra machinery and extra risk of failure or of needing to "tune" the interpretation — closer to inventing external structure. LPO instead only requires:
- a **precedence** on symbols (a simple, syntactic, easily justified choice: `F ≻ G` since `F`'s recursion calls `G`, and `G ≻ S ≻ Z` merely to totally order the signature), and
- **subterm comparisons**, which are totally free (built into any simplification ordering) and require no creativity.

This keeps the proof entirely inside the syntax of the rules — nothing is imported that isn't already implicit in the shape of the two equations.

**3. Both rules reduce to trivial subterm-property checks.**

- Rule 1 is an immediate instance of the subterm property (right side literally is a subterm of the left) — LPO handles this for free, no precedence work at all.
- Rule 2 reduces, after one precedence comparison (`F ≻ G`), to checking that each argument of the RHS's outer symbol is dominated by the LHS — and this bottoms out again in either the subterm property (for `y`) or a same-head-symbol lexicographic comparison that itself bottoms out in the subterm property (`S(n) ≻ n`).

So the entire termination proof is built from **only the subterm property**, chained together via LPO's recursive definition and one precedence fact. This is about as minimal and "argument-free" (in the sense of not needing external axioms) as a termination proof can get.

**4. LPO is a reduction order "for free."**

Because LPO is well-founded, monotonic (closed under contexts), and stable (closed under substitutions) *by general theorem*, once I show each rule's LHS ≻ RHS, termination of the *entire* rewrite relation follows automatically — I don't need a separate compatibility argument. This is why reduction orders like LPO (or RPO, KBO) are the standard first move for a simple TRS like this: they convert a global termination question into a finite number of local, syntactic inequality checks.

**In short:** I chose LPO because the system's only nontrivial rule is a textbook structural-recursion pattern (decrease in one argument position via a constructor, `S(n) → n`), and LPO with a precedence `F ≻ G` reduces the whole termination proof to two applications of the subterm property — no numeric interpretations, no auxiliary rules or side conditions, and no assumptions beyond the given syntax.
