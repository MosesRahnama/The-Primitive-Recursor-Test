Yes — the method complies, and it's worth spelling out precisely why, since the requirement is subtle enough to deserve scrutiny rather than a reflexive "yes."

## The concern being raised

The worry implicit in the question is: doesn't choosing a **precedence** (`F ≻ G ≻ S ≻ Z`) and a **status** (lexicographic, left-to-right) count as "importing" something not literally handed to me in the two rewrite rules? Isn't that a hidden assumption smuggled in from outside the system?

## Why it does *not* violate the constraints

**1. The precedence/status are parameters of a fixed, general-purpose method — not additions to the object system.**
LPO itself (its definition, well-foundedness, monotonicity, and substitution-stability) is a theorem that holds *for every* choice of precedence and status, proved once and for all in the general theory of term rewriting, independent of any particular TRS. Choosing a precedence is not asserting a new fact about `F`, `G`, `S`, `Z` as rewrite behavior — it is selecting *which instance* of an already-validated ordering family to test against the given rules. This is exactly the sense in which the prompt permits "any standard proof method": the method comes with its own internal machinery (precedence, status, weight functions, interpretations — depending on which method you pick), and supplying that machinery is not the same as adding a rewrite rule, guard, side condition, or unproven claim about the object system.

**2. Nothing was added to the rewrite system itself.**
The two rules
```
F(x, y, Z) -> x
F(x, y, S(n)) -> G(y, F(x, y, n))
```
are exactly as given — I did not introduce a third rule, restrict the domain with a guard (e.g. "only if `n` is a numeral"), or assume some extra equation (e.g. commutativity of `G`, or that `S`/`Z` obey Peano axioms beyond being free constructors). The precedence and status are *external bookkeeping used to certify* termination; they never appear as syntax being rewritten, and no rewrite step in the system depends on them existing.

**3. Every comparison actually performed was justified from the syntax alone.**
- Rule 1's orientation used only the **subterm property**: `x` is literally a subterm of `F(x,y,Z)` — this is a syntactic, structural fact about the given left-hand side, requiring no assumption at all.
- Rule 2's orientation used (a) the subterm property again (`y` inside `F(x,y,S(n))`; `n` inside `S(n)`), and (b) exactly one precedence fact, `F ≻ G`, which is *forced* by the rules themselves: `F`'s own defining equation is the only place `G` is introduced, and `G` never rewrites back into an `F`-headed term or vice versa in a cyclic way — so declaring `F` "above" `G` reflects the actual, observable call structure of the two rules, not an arbitrary external stipulation.

So the precedence was not an unjustified hidden assumption; it mirrors the dependency structure already present in the rules (`F`'s rule *calls* `G`; `G` has no rule of its own at all — it's uninterpreted here). Choosing `F ≻ G` is the *minimal, faithful* precedence choice, not an imported axiom about the symbols' meanings.

**4. The constants `S`, `Z` were treated as free/uninterpreted constructors — nothing more.**
I did not assume any additional object-level facts about `S` and `Z` (e.g. injectivity, no-confusion, induction principles à la Peano arithmetic). Their only role in the proof was as syntactic wrappers whose subterm relationship (`S(n)` has proper subterm `n`) is a bare parsing fact, true for *any* unary function symbol applied to a variable — not a borrowed arithmetic axiom.

## Conclusion

The LPO-based argument uses only:
- the literal left/right shapes of the two given rules,
- the generic, rule-independent theorem that LPO is a reduction order for *any* precedence/status choice, and
- one precedence choice (`F ≻ G`) that is directly read off the given rules' call structure, plus a tie-breaking total order among the remaining symbols that has no effect on the actual inequalities needed.

No rule was added, no side condition or guard was imposed, and no object-level axiom about `S`/`Z`/`G` beyond "these are just term-forming symbols" was used. So yes — this satisfies the stated requirement: termination is established from the presented rules alone, via a standard method (LPO), without strengthening or presupposing anything beyond the given syntax.
