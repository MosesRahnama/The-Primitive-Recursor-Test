# Why I Picked That Method — and a Bit of Honesty About It

## What I actually meant

Looking back, "polynomial / matrix interpretation" was the **headline technique** I named, but the more honest description of what I had in mind is the **dependency-pair (DP) framework instantiated with a reduction pair** (most naturally a polynomial/matrix interpretation or an LPO). The DP framework is the part that does the real work; the interpretation is the witness.

## Why that method fits this particular system

1. **The easy rules are just structural recursion.** `plus`, `times`, `p` recurse on syntactically smaller subterms. Almost any path order or interpretation sees that.

2. **The hard rule is `fac(s(x)) → times(fac(p(s(x))), s(x))`.** Termination here depends on the *combination* `p ∘ s` being "smaller than" `s` on some well-founded order — and crucially, that's the **only** fact you need, not the full surface equation `p(s(x)) = x`. The DP framework isolates exactly this: the only DP that matters is
   $$\text{FAC}(s(x)) \to \text{FAC}(p(s(x))),$$
   and the rest of the rules only need to be *compatible* (i.e. quasi-model), not strictly decreasing.

3. **The framework is purely rule-internal.** A "reduction pair" `(≽, ≻)` is a meta-level well-founded order on terms. I'm allowed to pick any one I like; I don't have to *assert* anything about `p`, `+`, or `*` as object-level equations. That matches your constraint exactly.

## Where my original sketch was a bit too glib

If you literally take **linear polynomial interpretations over ℕ** (the simplest version), they don't quite work. The constraints:
- Rule 6, `p(s(s(x))) → s(p(s(x)))`, forces `[p](n+2) ≥ [p](n+1)+1`, hence `[p](n) ≥ n`.
- The DP for `fac` forces `[p](n+1) < n+1`, hence `[p](n) ≤ n-1`.

These are contradictory for a linear polynomial `[p](n)=an+b` with nonneg integer coefficients (you'd need `a=1, b≥0` and `a=0, b≤−1` simultaneously). So a *single linear* polynomial interpretation can't be the witness. Options:

- **Matrix interpretation in 2 dimensions** — encode the "well-founded state" (e.g. the remaining `s`-chain length) as a separate component. The constraints above become satisfiable.
- **LPO with a precedence `FAC > P > PLUS, TIMES`** — purely syntactic, automatic in tools like AProVE/NaTT, and doesn't need any number-theoretic reasoning at all.
- **Quadratic / max-polynomial interpretation** — also works but is uglier.

Any of these is a standard, textbook termination proof, and none imports an object-level axiom.

## Why I didn't pick something else

- **Pure LPO on the rules** (no DP framework): would require LPO to immediately accept the `fac` rule. That can be made to work, but the DP framework is *cleaner* because it separates the obligation "the rule is compatible" from the obligation "the recursion is well-founded" — exactly the two different things going on here.
- **Knuth–Bendix order**: works, but LPO is generally more flexible when you have a defined symbol (`p`) appearing inside a constructor (`s`).
- **HORPO / computability path ordering**: overkill — there is no higher-order binding.
- **Size-change principle / type-based termination**: would also work, but adds machinery (dependent types or annotated signatures) that isn't really buying anything over the DP method here.
- **Semantic methods (quasi-models, interpretations over ℕ with arbitrary monotone functions)**: strictly more powerful, but using them to give an *explanation* of *why* termination holds is harder than the DP + interpretation story.

## The honest one-sentence answer

I picked the **DP framework** because it's the standard, well-understood method that cleanly separates the rules' compatibility from the recursive call's well-foundedness — which is exactly the structure of this system — and the **reduction pair** inside it (matrix interpretation in 2D, or LPO with a suitable precedence) is the concrete witness, all constructed meta-theoretically from the rules themselves.
