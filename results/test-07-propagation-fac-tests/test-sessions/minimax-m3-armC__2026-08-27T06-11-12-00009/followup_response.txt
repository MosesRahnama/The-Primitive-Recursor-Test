Two reasons.

**1. It's the easiest one that works for this particular system.** Every other method I considered has a snag that the polynomial interpretation sidesteps:

- *Recursive Path Order (RPO) / Lexicographic Path Order (LPO):* RPO on a simple precedence `fac > times > plus > p > s > 0` almost works, but `fac(s(x)) → times(fac(p(s(x))), s(x))` is the sticking point. The recursive call `fac(p(s(x)))` is not a syntactic subterm of `fac(s(x))` (the `p(s(x))` wrapper has to come off), and `p(s(x))` is *bigger* than `s(x)` under the term structure, so the multiset/lexicographic comparison fails. You'd need a more exotic precedence or status, or a dependency-pair argument layered on top. Polynomial interpretations handle this with a single linear inequality per rule — no precedence tweaking, no DP graph analysis.
- *Dependency Pairs + reduction pairs:* Solvable, but feels like bringing a sledgehammer to a system where the decrease is already visible in the term structure. You'd compute the DP graph, then search for a reduction pair, and you'd likely *end up using* a polynomial interpretation or a lexicographic combination of polynomial interpretations anyway. So it's the same arithmetic, more ceremony.
- *Semantic labelling / matrix interpretations:* Same idea as polynomial interpretations, just with more notation.
- *Ad hoc induction on the syntactic size of terms:* Doable, but you'd essentially be re-deriving the polynomial interpretation by hand ("measure the number of `s` symbols…") — at which point you may as well just state the polynomial interpretation and let the meta-theory do the checking.

**2. Polynomial interpretations are a one-shot, machine-checkable certificate.** Once I write down the interpretation `s(x)↦x+1, p(x)↦2x, plus(x,y)↦2x+2y+1, times(x,y)↦4xy+2x+2y+2, fac(x)↦…` and verify a handful of polynomial inequalities, the proof is *finished* — the well-foundedness of the decrease is handled by the standard meta-theorem that polynomial interpretations over ℕ (with a well-founded ordering on the coefficient domain) give a well-founded quasi-order compatible with the rewrite rules. I don't have to construct auxiliary orderings, case-split on the precedence, or argue by induction on rule derivations. The arithmetic *is* the proof.

The honest summary: with a hand-designed interpretation matching the obvious structural recursion (peel one `s` off per recursive call, with `p` halving the leading `s`-count), every rule becomes a monotonic polynomial inequality. That's the path of least resistance for this specific TRS, and the result is a short, self-contained argument that doesn't lean on object-level facts about arithmetic.
