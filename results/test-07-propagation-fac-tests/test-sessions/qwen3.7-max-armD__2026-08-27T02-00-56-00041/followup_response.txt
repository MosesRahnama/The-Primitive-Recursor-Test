I chose the **polynomial interpretation** method for three primary reasons: the arithmetic nature of the system, the failure of simple syntactic size metrics, and the desire for a direct, self-contained proof without needing to configure complex syntactic orderings.

Here is the detailed breakdown of why this method was the most natural and effective choice:

### 1. Identical Syntactic Sizes in Key Rules (The "Weight" Problem)
The most compelling technical reason for choosing a weighted algebraic method like polynomial interpretation is that several rules in this system **preserve the exact syntactic size** (number of function symbols) of the term. 

*   **Rule 6:** `p(s(s(x))) -> s(p(s(x)))`
    *   LHS symbols: `p`, `s`, `s` (3 symbols)
    *   RHS symbols: `s`, `p`, `s` (3 symbols)
*   **Rule 5:** `times(s(x), y) -> plus(times(x, y), y)`
    *   LHS symbols: `times`, `s` (2 symbols)
    *   RHS symbols: `plus`, `times` (2 symbols)

Because the raw symbol count does not strictly decrease in these rules, a simple "term size" metric fails. To prove termination, we *must* assign different mathematical weights to the function symbols (e.g., making `p` "heavier" than `s`, and `times` heavier than `plus`). Polynomial interpretations natively provide this weighting mechanism through algebraic coefficients.

### 2. Semantic Alignment with Arithmetic
The TRS is clearly defining standard Peano arithmetic operations: addition (`plus`), multiplication (`times`), and predecessor (`p`). 
*   Polynomial interpretations map syntactic rewriting directly to semantic mathematical evaluation. 
*   It is highly intuitive to map `times` to a quadratic polynomial (since multiplication grows quadratically), `plus` to a linear polynomial, and `s` to a simple $+1$ increment. 
*   By aligning the proof method with the semantic intent of the rules, finding a valid interpretation becomes a straightforward algebraic exercise rather than a blind search.

### 3. Bypassing the Configuration of Syntactic Orderings
Alternative standard methods like **Recursive Path Ordering (RPO)**, **Lexicographic Path Ordering (LPO)**, or **Knuth-Bendix Ordering (KBO)** could theoretically be used, but they require careful manual configuration:
*   **Path Orderings (RPO/LPO):** To handle `times(s(x), y) -> plus(...)`, you must establish a strict symbol precedence (`times > plus > p > s`) and verify that the status (multiset vs. lexicographic) of the arguments works for every single rule. 
*   **Dependency Pairs:** This would require generating dependency pairs, estimating the dependency graph, and then applying a base ordering (like RPO or polynomials) to the strongly connected components anyway.

Polynomial interpretation bypasses the need to define precedence relations, argument filterings, or status tuples. It relies purely on basic algebra and the well-foundedness of the natural numbers ($\mathbb{N}$), making the proof highly direct, easy to verify by hand, and completely self-contained.

### Summary
I picked polynomial interpretation because the system's rules preserve syntactic term size in critical places, demanding a weighted measure. Polynomials provide that weight naturally, perfectly mirror the arithmetic semantics of the TRS, and yield a self-contained proof without the administrative overhead of configuring symbol precedences for path orderings.
