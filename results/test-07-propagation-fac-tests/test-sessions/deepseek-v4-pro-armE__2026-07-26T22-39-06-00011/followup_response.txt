I picked polynomial interpretation because of its simplicity and direct fit to the structure of the rules.

The system is small, first‑order, and has no algebraic properties (no associativity, commutativity, etc.) that would break monotonic polynomial orderings. The second rule uses a helper symbol G whose only job is to re-wrap the result; a polynomial interpretation can simply project away G’s first argument and assign a “cost” that makes each recursive call strictly smaller. Concretely, I could see immediately that interpreting F(x,y,n) as x+size(n) with a constant offset, and G(y,v) as v, would yield a clean inequality for both rules.

Compared to other standard methods:
- Lexicographic path order (LPO) or the dependency pair framework would also work, but they require slightly more involved checks (e.g., subterm comparisons, argument filterings). Here the reduction in the third argument (S(n) → n) is obvious, but LPO would need to deal with G being uninterpreted and might need an extra precedence tweak.  
- A direct induction on the third argument of F could be attempted, but the rules themselves don’t express a structural decrease for x and y, so a weight function that ignores those arguments (or adds them into a sum) is more uniform.

Thus, a polynomial interpretation that maps F(x,y,n) to a sum involving x and the size of n, and G to a projection, gives a one‑line proof of termination without further adjustments. It’s the lightest tool that does the job cleanly and transparently.
