I chose the **Lexicographic Path Order (LPO)** for this specific system because it is the most elegant, direct, and structurally natural method for proving termination of systems built on **primitive recursion**. 

Here is the specific breakdown of why LPO was the best tool for the job compared to other methods:

### 1. It perfectly matches structural recursion
Look at the recursive rules in the system:
* `plus(x, s(y)) -> s(plus(x, y))`
* `times(s(x), y) -> plus(times(x, y), y)`
* `p(s(s(x))) -> s(p(s(x)))`

In every recursive step, a function is called on a strictly smaller piece of syntax (stripping away an `s`). LPO was explicitly designed to handle exactly this kind of structural descent. The lexicographic check (comparing arguments left-to-right) handles the inner decrease (e.g., `s(x)` strictly greater than `x`), while the precedence check handles the outer function symbols.

### 2. It handles variable duplication effortlessly
The rule `times(s(x), y) -> plus(times(x, y), y)` duplicates the variable `y`. 
If I had chosen a method that relies on counting symbols or weights—such as a **Knuth-Bendix Order (KBO)**—this duplication would cause a massive headache. In KBO, the total weight of the left side must be greater than or equal to the total weight of the right side. Because `y` appears once on the left but twice on the right, KBO would require assigning `y` a weight of zero, which triggers a cascade of strict admissibility conditions that complicate the proof. 
LPO completely bypasses this because it compares terms based on the *hierarchy of function symbols* (`times` > `plus`) rather than counting how many times a variable appears.

### 3. It avoids the trial-and-error of Polynomial Interpretations
Another standard way to prove termination is using **Polynomial Interpretations** (e.g., mapping `s(x)` to $x + 1$, `plus(x, y)` to $x + y$, etc.). While powerful, this method requires mapping syntax to external mathematical domains (the natural numbers). 
* It requires finding the "magic numbers" (coefficients) that make the math work out.
* It introduces arithmetic calculations into the proof.
* It borders on violating the strict constraint of your prompt: *"without importing... object-level assumptions beyond what is derivable from the given syntax."* 
LPO keeps the proof 100% within the domain of the given syntax.

### 4. It guarantees termination with a single precedence list
LPO is a "simplification order." By Kruskal's Tree Theorem, any simplification order on a finite signature guarantees that the rewriting system is well-founded (terminating). Therefore, all I had to do was provide one simple, defensible hierarchy (`times > plus > p > s > 0`) and mechanically verify the rules. It leaves no room for hidden assumptions, resulting in a rigorous and verifiable proof.
