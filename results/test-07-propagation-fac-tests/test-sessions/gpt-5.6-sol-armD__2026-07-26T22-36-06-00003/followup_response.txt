I chose a **polynomial interpretation** because it gives a compact, fully explicit termination certificate that fits all the rules uniformly.

In particular:

- It reduces the entire question to checking elementary inequalities over \(\mathbb N\).
- Strict monotonicity ensures that a decrease is preserved when a redex occurs inside any context, so root-step checks suffice for arbitrary rewrite steps.
- It handles the less syntactically obvious rules cleanly:
  - `times(s(x), y)` expands into a `plus` expression in which `y` appears again.
  - `p(s(s(x)))` rewrites to a term with an additional outer `s`.
  A simple term-size argument would not decrease on these rules.
- It does not rely on treating `0` and `s` as natural-number constructors at the object level. Mapping terms into \(\mathbb N\) is only a meta-level termination measure.
- Every rule can be verified directly, making it easy to confirm that no rule, guard, or structural assumption has been silently added.

The coefficients were selected to satisfy the inequalities forced by the rules. For example, `plus` needs to assign enough weight to its second argument for

\[
\operatorname{plus}(x,s(y)) > s(\operatorname{plus}(x,y)),
\]

and `times` needs enough weight on the left-hand side for

\[
\operatorname{times}(s(x),y)>
\operatorname{plus}(\operatorname{times}(x,y),y).
\]

Likewise, interpreting \(p(a)=2a\) makes the two sides of the first `p` rule differ by exactly \(1\).

This was not necessarily the only method—an appropriate simplification order may also prove termination—but the polynomial interpretation provides a particularly transparent numerical witness that addresses every rule directly.
