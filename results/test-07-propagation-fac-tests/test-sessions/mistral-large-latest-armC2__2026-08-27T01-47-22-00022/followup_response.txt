Great question! The choice of termination proof method depends on the structure of the rewrite system and the nature of the rules. Here’s why I leaned toward **recursive path orders (RPO/LPO)** and **polynomial interpretations** for this system, and why other methods might be less suitable:

---

### **1. Why Recursive Path Orders (RPO/LPO)?**
#### **Advantages for this system:**
- **Natural fit for structural recursion**: The rules for `plus`, `times`, `p`, and `fac` are all *structurally recursive* (i.e., they recurse on subterms of the left-hand side). RPO/LPO are designed to handle such cases by comparing the "size" or "complexity" of terms based on a precedence of function symbols.
  - Example: In `fac(s(x)) -> times(fac(p(s(x))), s(x))`, the recursive call `fac(p(s(x)))` is "smaller" than `fac(s(x))` because `p(s(x))` is a subterm of `s(x)` (after applying `p`), and `p` has lower precedence than `fac`.
- **No need for arithmetic**: Unlike polynomial interpretations, RPO/LPO don’t require assigning numeric weights to symbols, which can be tricky for systems with non-arithmetic operations (e.g., `p` or `fac`).
- **Handles lexicographic orders well**: The `times` rule `times(s(x), y) -> plus(times(x, y), y)` is lexicographic in nature (the first argument decreases, while the second stays the same). RPO/LPO can handle this by comparing arguments left-to-right.

#### **Precedence choice:**
A plausible precedence for this system is:
`fac > times > plus > s > p > 0`
- This ensures that:
  - `fac` is the "largest" symbol, so its recursive calls are smaller.
  - `times` is larger than `plus`, so `times(s(x), y)` is larger than `plus(times(x, y), y)`.
  - `s` is larger than `p`, so `p(s(x))` is smaller than `s(x)`.

#### **Why not other path orders?**
- **Simpler path orders (e.g., homeomorphic embedding)**: These are too weak for systems with nested recursion like `fac` or `times`.
- **Knuth-Bendix order (KBO)**: Requires a weight assignment, which is less intuitive for this system (e.g., how do you assign weights to `p` or `fac`?).

---

### **2. Why Polynomial Interpretations?**
#### **Advantages for this system:**
- **Intuitive for arithmetic-like rules**: The `plus` and `times` rules resemble arithmetic, so a polynomial interpretation (e.g., `plus(x, y) = x + y`, `times(x, y) = x * y`) is a natural fit.
- **Handles predecessor (`p`) well**: You can interpret `p(x)` as `max(x - 1, 0)`, which ensures `p(s(x))` is smaller than `s(x)`.
- **Factorial can be approximated**: While `fac(x)` is not a polynomial, you can use a polynomial upper bound (e.g., `fac(x) = x^2` or `x!` for small `x`) to show termination.

#### **Example interpretation:**
| Symbol | Interpretation          |
|--------|-------------------------|
| `0`    | `0`                     |
| `s(x)` | `x + 1`                 |
| `plus(x, y)` | `x + y`          |
| `times(x, y)` | `x * y`         |
| `p(x)` | `max(x - 1, 0)`         |
| `fac(x)` | `x^2` (or `x!` for small `x`) |

- For `fac(s(x)) -> times(fac(p(s(x))), s(x))`:
  - LHS: `fac(s(x)) = (x + 1)^2`
  - RHS: `times(fac(p(s(x))), s(x)) = fac(max(x, 0)) * (x + 1) = max(x, 0)^2 * (x + 1)`
  - For `x >= 0`, `(x + 1)^2 > x^2 * (x + 1)` holds (since `x + 1 > x^2` for `x >= 1`, and `x = 0` is a base case).

#### **Why not always use polynomial interpretations?**
- **Not all systems are arithmetic**: If the system had non-arithmetic rules (e.g., list operations), polynomial interpretations might not work.
- **Factorial is tricky**: `fac(x)` is not a polynomial, so you need a polynomial upper bound, which may not always exist or be easy to find.

---

### **3. Why Not Other Methods?**
#### **Dependency Pairs (DP):**
- **Pros**: Powerful for complex systems with mutual recursion.
- **Cons**: Overkill for this system, which has no mutual recursion. The rules are simple enough that RPO/LPO or polynomial interpretations suffice.
- **Example**: The `fac` rule would generate a dependency pair `FAC(s(x)) -> FAC(p(s(x)))`, but this is already handled by RPO/LPO.

#### **Semantic Methods (e.g., well-founded algebras):**
- **Pros**: Can handle very complex systems.
- **Cons**: Require finding a well-founded model, which is often non-trivial and less automatable than RPO/LPO or polynomial interpretations.

#### **Simplification Orders (e.g., path orders):**
- **Pros**: Simple and automatable.
- **Cons**: May not work for systems with non-structural recursion (e.g., if `fac` had a rule like `fac(x) -> fac(x)`).

---

### **4. Why Not Just "Inspection"?**
While it’s tempting to argue termination by inspection (e.g., "the arguments get smaller"), this is not rigorous. For example:
- The `fac` rule recurses on `p(s(x))`, which is smaller than `s(x)`, but this needs to be formalized (e.g., via RPO/LPO or polynomial interpretations).
- The `times` rule `times(s(x), y) -> plus(times(x, y), y)` reduces the first argument but keeps the second the same. This is lexicographic, not strictly decreasing, so inspection alone isn’t enough.

---

### **5. Final Choice: RPO/LPO**
For this system, **RPO/LPO is the most straightforward and automatable method** because:
1. The rules are structurally recursive.
2. A simple precedence (`fac > times > plus > s > p > 0`) suffices.
3. No arithmetic or polynomial bounds are needed.
4. It’s easy to verify manually (unlike dependency pairs or semantic methods).

Polynomial interpretations would also work but are slightly less elegant here because of the `fac` rule. However, they’re a good backup if RPO/LPO fails (which it doesn’t in this case).

---

### **Summary of Reasoning:**
| Method               | Why It Works Here                     | Why Not Other Methods?               |
|----------------------|---------------------------------------|--------------------------------------|
| **RPO/LPO**          | Structurally recursive rules, simple precedence. | Overkill for simpler methods.        |
| **Polynomial Interp.** | Arithmetic-like rules, handles `p` and `fac`. | `fac` is not a polynomial.           |
| **Dependency Pairs** | Not needed (no mutual recursion).     | Overkill for this system.            |
| **Inspection**       | Rules "look" terminating.             | Not rigorous; misses lexicographic cases. |

Thus, **RPO/LPO is the best choice** for this system.
