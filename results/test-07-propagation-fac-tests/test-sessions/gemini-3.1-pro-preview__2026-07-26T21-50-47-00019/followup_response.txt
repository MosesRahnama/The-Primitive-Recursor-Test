I chose the **Dependency Pair (DP) Framework** (specifically combined with the Subterm Criterion and Usable Rules) because it is uniquely suited to bypass two major roadblocks in this specific rewriting system that would cause older, traditional methods to fail or become overwhelmingly complicated.

Here is the step-by-step reasoning behind why this specific method was the right tool for the job:

### 1. The Obstacle of Non-Structural Recursion
In traditional term rewriting, the easiest way to prove termination is to show that a system is "structurally decreasing" using syntactic orderings like Lexicographic Path Ordering (LPO) or Knuth-Bendix Ordering (KBO). 

These older methods require the arguments of a recursive call to be a strict, syntactic subterm of the left-hand side. 
*   `plus` and `times` are structurally decreasing (e.g., `y` is a subterm of `s(y)`).
*   However, `fac` is not. In the rule `fac(s(x)) -> times(fac(p(s(x))), s(x))`, the recursive call is `fac(p(s(x)))`. 

Because `p(s(x))` is wrapped in another defined function symbol (`p`), it is *not* a syntactic subterm of `s(x)`. LPO and KBO instantly fail here because they cannot natively deduce that `p(s(x))` evaluates to something smaller than `s(x)`.

### 2. The Nightmare of Global Interpretations (The "Growth" Problem)
Before the DP framework was invented, the fallback for non-structural recursion was a **Global Polynomial Interpretation**. This means assigning a mathematical formula to every symbol in the system such that *every single rule* evaluates to $Left > Right$.

If we tried that here, we would face a massive mathematical headache:
*   `plus` represents addition (linear growth).
*   `times` represents multiplication (quadratic growth).
*   `fac` represents factorial (superexponential growth).

Because standard polynomials cannot bound exponential growth, we would be forced to use exotic exponential domains just to prove termination. Proving that an exponential interpretation strictly decreases across all 8 rules simultaneously is incredibly tedious and prone to arithmetic errors.

### 3. The Elegance of Dependency Pairs (Why I Picked It)
The Dependency Pair framework revolutionized termination proofs by shifting the focus from **data** (what the functions compute) to **control flow** (how the functions call each other). I chose it because of its incredible modularity:

*   **It isolates the loops:** Instead of solving the whole system at once, DP broke the system into isolated Strongly Connected Components (SCCs). I could treat `plus`, `times`, `p`, and `fac` as completely separate problems.
*   **It ignores the output (The Subterm Criterion):** For `plus`, `times`, and `p`, DP allowed me to use the "Subterm Criterion." This rule essentially says: *"If the arguments going into the next recursive call are structurally smaller, we don't care what the function returns."* This allowed me to instantly dispatch 3 of the 4 loops with zero math.
*   **It requires minimal math (Usable Rules):** For the problematic `fac` loop, DP says we only need to measure the *arguments* of the recursive calls, not the result of `fac`. Furthermore, the **Usable Rules** technique allowed me to completely throw away the rules for `times` and `plus`. I only had to evaluate the rules for `p` to prove that `p(s(x))` is smaller than `s(x)`. 

### Summary
I picked the DP framework because it is the most modern, surgical approach. It allowed me to bypass the exponential growth of the factorial function entirely, prove 75% of the system terminates by simply looking at the syntax, and isolate the only tricky part (the predecessor function) into a trivial piece of first-grade arithmetic ($x + 2 > x + 1$).
