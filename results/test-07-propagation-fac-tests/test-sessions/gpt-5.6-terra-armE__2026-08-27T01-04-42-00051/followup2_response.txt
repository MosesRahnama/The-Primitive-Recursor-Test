Yes. The LPO argument complies with those requirements.

The precedence choice

\[
F \succ G
\]

and the use of a lexicographic status for `F` are not additional rewrite rules, object-level axioms, guards, or assumptions about the intended meaning of the symbols. They are parameters of a standard meta-level termination ordering used to prove that the already-given rewrite relation is well-founded.

In particular, the proof does **not** assume any of the following:

- that `Z` denotes zero;
- that `S` denotes successor;
- that every term is of the form `Z` or `S(n)`;
- that `S` is injective, distinct from `Z`, or satisfies any constructor axioms;
- that `G` has any semantic behavior;
- any additional reduction rule for `G`, `S`, or `Z`;
- any restriction on substitutions beyond ordinary first-order substitution.

The only syntactic facts used are facts available for arbitrary first-order terms:

1. A term is greater than its proper subterms in LPO. Thus:
   \[
   F(x,y,Z) > x
   \quad\text{and}\quad
   S(n) > n.
   \]

2. Given lexicographic comparison for `F`,
   \[
   S(n) > n
   \implies
   F(x,y,S(n)) > F(x,y,n).
   \]

3. Given the freely selected ordering parameter \(F \succ G\), and the preceding comparisons,
   \[
   F(x,y,S(n)) > y
   \quad\text{and}\quad
   F(x,y,S(n)) > F(x,y,n),
   \]
   so LPO yields
   \[
   F(x,y,S(n)) > G(y,F(x,y,n)).
   \]

Choosing \(F \succ G\) does not alter the rewrite system; it merely defines a well-founded syntactic measure with which to check that both existing rules decrease. Therefore it is within the permitted use of a standard proof method and does not strengthen the system.
