I picked LPO because the rules have exactly the shape it handles well:

- The left side of the recursive rule has root symbol `F`.
- Its right side has root symbol `G`, containing a recursive `F` call on the syntactically smaller argument `n` rather than `S(n)`.
- The base rule returns a variable, which is immediately smaller than the whole left-hand side by the subterm property.

More concretely, the potentially concerning rule is

\[
F(x,y,S(n)) \to G(y,F(x,y,n)).
\]

A simple “term size decreases” argument does **not** work: depending on how size is counted, the right-hand side has an added `G`, so it can be larger than the left-hand side. Nor is it enough merely to observe that the third argument of the recursive `F` changes from `S(n)` to `n`, because rewriting may occur inside arbitrary contexts and the outer `G` must also be accounted for.

LPO directly captures both relevant facts:

1. The recursive occurrence decreases structurally:
   \[
   S(n) > n,
   \]
   hence
   \[
   F(x,y,S(n)) > F(x,y,n).
   \]

2. The outer function symbol changes from a higher-precedence symbol to a lower one:
   \[
   F \succ G.
   \]
   Since the whole left-hand side is also greater than both arguments of the `G` term, LPO concludes
   \[
   F(x,y,S(n)) > G(y,F(x,y,n)).
   \]

This is a purely syntactic termination proof: it requires no interpretation of `S` as successor or `Z` as zero, no assumption that only constructor-shaped arguments reach `F`, and no added conditions.

A polynomial interpretation or recursive path ordering could also be used, but LPO is especially short and transparent here because it handles the recursive descent and the `F`-to-`G` wrapper in one standard ordering argument.
