I picked a syntactic decreasing-call analysis—essentially the intuition behind dependency pairs plus a well-founded measure—because the question explicitly excludes relying on the intended arithmetic meaning of `0`, `s`, `plus`, `times`, `p`, or `fac`.

The key issue is the apparent recursive cycle:

```text
fac(s(x)) -> times(fac(p(s(x))), s(x))
```

Viewed naively, that does not syntactically replace `x` by a direct subterm: the recursive argument is `p(s(x))`. So a simple “all recursive calls use smaller subterms” argument would be insufficient.

Instead, I examined what must happen for that recursive `fac` occurrence to become reducible again. It can match `fac(s(...))` only if `p(s(x))` rewrites to a term headed by `s`. The only rule that can do that is:

```text
p(s(s(x))) -> s(p(s(x)))
```

and each use removes one `s` from the argument being traversed by `p`. Therefore, every genuine continuation of the `fac` recursion consumes successor depth. If the required successor structure is not present, the `p` term becomes irreducible and the `fac` call cannot continue.

I mentioned a structural/lexicographic or dependency-pair-style proof because those are proof techniques that express exactly this fact at the rewrite-system level:

- `plus` decreases successor depth in its second argument;
- `times` decreases successor depth in its first argument;
- `p` traverses inward through a finite successor spine;
- an active recursive `fac` continuation requires progress through that same finite spine.

That choice avoids assuming, for example, that `p` is a total predecessor function or that every term is a natural numeral. It treats non-numeral or malformed terms simply as terms that may get stuck in normal form, which is sufficient for termination.
