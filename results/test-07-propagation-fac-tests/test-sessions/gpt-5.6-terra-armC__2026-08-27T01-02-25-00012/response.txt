Yes. Termination can be established from the rewrite rules themselves; no Peano axioms, predecessor axioms, or intended arithmetic interpretation are required.

The relevant point is to reason over arbitrary finite first-order terms, not merely numerals:

- `plus` recursively removes a leading `s` from its second argument.
- `times` recursively removes a leading `s` from its first argument, with the resulting `plus` calls themselves terminating.
- `p(s(s(x))) -> s(p(s(x)))` moves the `p` inward across one successor layer. Eventually it either reaches `p(s(0)) -> 0` or becomes stuck on a nonmatching argument. There is no rule that can restart or reverse that traversal indefinitely.
- The only recursive `fac` call is `fac(p(s(x)))`. For that call to become another active `fac(s(...))` redex, the `p` computation must expose a successor; doing so consumes successor structure from the original argument. If no such structure is available, the term becomes stuck rather than looping.

Thus the absence of rules such as `fac(0) -> ...` or `p(0) -> ...` is not a problem for termination: those terms are simply normal forms. The system may be incomplete as an arithmetic specification on arbitrary terms, but incompleteness is not nontermination.

A formal proof could be phrased with a well-founded structural/lexicographic measure or a dependency-pair style argument tracking the decreasing successor spine traversed by `p`, together with the ordinary decreasing recursion of `plus` and `times`.
