I chose a high-level structural/semantic argument because the rewrite system is small and its rules have an obvious, well-founded measure: each left-hand side is “bigger” than its corresponding right-hand side in a way that is easily captured by an ordering on constructor nests (e.g., size of the term, or a polynomial interpretation mapping terms to natural numbers).  

The rules for `plus`, `times`, and `p` are all straightforwardly decreasing: `plus(x, s(y))` reduces the depth of the second argument; `times(s(x), y)` recurses on a structurally smaller first argument; `p` pushes a `s` inward and eventually eliminates it. The only rule that might look worrying is `fac`, but its recursive call receives `p(s(x))`, and any reduction of `p(s(x))` for a ground term will yield a term with fewer `s` constructors, so the argument of `fac` eventually becomes smaller.  

I didn’t need to invoke a more complex technique (like dependency pairs or a formal polynomial interpretation) because a simple measure-function interpretation (e.g., mapping terms to natural numbers assigning weight to `s`) already suffices and is easy to check mentally. The measure itself can be defined entirely within the language of the rewrite system; no external axioms about the symbols (such as algebraic properties of `plus` and `times` on naturals) are required. That’s why my method was essentially a direct, informal termination argument using a syntactic well-founded ordering—it’s the quickest way to see that the system is terminating without importing extra assumptions.
