# Why the Dependency-Pair framework with a reduction pair?

I want to be honest: I picked this method *because I tried the easier ones first and they failed*. Let me walk through the alternatives and where each one breaks, because the failures are informative.

## 1. Polynomial interpretations over (ℕ, +, ·, ·)

This is the first thing to try because it's fully automatic and uses no hidden axioms (interpretations are a syntactic device on the term algebra). It works fine on the plus/times/p fragment. The breakage is localized on rule (8):

$$\mathsf{fac}(s(x)) \to \mathsf{times}(\mathsf{fac}(p(s(x))), s(x))$$

For rule (6) to be strict, $[\!s\!]$ must be expanding ($[\!s\!](x) = c x + d$ with $c \ge 2$). But then $[\!s\!](x) > x$, so the inner $\mathsf{fac}$-argument in (8) is *bigger* than $x$. To still have (8) strict, $[\!\mathsf{fac}\!]$ has to dominate $[\!\mathsf{times}\!]([\!\mathsf{fac}\!](\text{big}),\, \text{big}) \approx [\!\mathsf{fac}\!](\text{big})\cdot \text{big}$, which forces $[\!\mathsf{fac}\!]$ to grow faster than every polynomial. So polynomial interpretations **provably cannot** prove this TRS terminating — the obstruction is real, not a matter of finding the right coefficients.

## 2. A single numeric measure on terms

The "obvious" measure is the $s$-depth of the *head* argument of the outermost defined symbol. Let's call it $d(t)$. The rule-by-rule checks:

- plus/times/p: clearly $d(\text{lhs}) > d(\text{rhs})$ for the *called* argument.
- fac: $d(\mathsf{fac}(s(x))) = 1 + d(x)$, but $d(\mathsf{times}(\dots)) = d(\text{first arg of times}) = d(\mathsf{fac}(p(s(x))))$, which is some unknown function of $d(x)$.

The problem is that the *outer* call to $\mathsf{times}$ "hides" the decrease: a single integer measure on the *whole* term can't see that the recursion is going *into* a deeper call that itself decreases. Concretely, with a measure of the form $d(t) = a\cdot d_\text{rec}(t) + b$, you'd need

$$a\,(1+d(x)) + b \;>\; a\cdot d(\mathsf{fac}(p(s(x)))) + b = a\cdot F(d(x)) + b$$

where $F$ is the growth rate of $\mathsf{fac}$'s own measure. For fac to dominate times-fac, $a\,F(n) \le a(n+1)$ forces $F$ sub-linear, which is the wrong direction.

So a flat measure also fails.

## 3. Recursive Path Ordering

RPO with the natural precedence $fac > times > plus > p > s > 0$ handles rules (1)–(7) cleanly. It breaks on rule (8) for a structural reason: to compare $\mathsf{fac}(s(x))$ with $\mathsf{times}(\mathsf{fac}(p(s(x))), s(x))$, RPO needs

$$\mathsf{fac}(s(x)) \;>_{\text{rpo}}\; \mathsf{fac}(p(s(x)))$$

which forces $s(x) >_{\text{rpo}} p(s(x))$. But rule (6) *requires* $p > s$ in the precedence (so that $\mathsf{p}(\dots) >_{\text{rpo}} \mathsf{s}(\dots)$), and that precedence makes $s(x) <_{\text{rpo}} p(s(x))$. So RPO needs a precedence that simultaneously has $p > s$ (for rule 6) and $s > p$ (for rule 8) — impossible.

You can rescue RPO with **argument filters** (telling RPO to ignore the "wrapper" arguments and compare only the recursive ones) or with **polynomial RPO / HORPO**, but at that point you've reinvented the relevant piece of the Dependency-Pair framework with extra bookkeeping.

## 4. Why DP + reduction pair is the right tool here

The structure of the system is exactly what the DP framework is designed for:

- Every defined symbol recurses on a **single, syntactically identifiable argument** (plus on its 2nd, times on its 1st, p and fac on their 1st).
- The recursion on each symbol is **well-founded in its own argument** — the $s$-depth of that argument strictly decreases at each recursive call (DP).
- The "cross-talk" between symbols (fac→times→plus) is **root-changing**: it never revisits the same defined symbol at a larger measure. The DP graph captures this as edges, and the reduction pair handles the chains.

The reduction pair $(\succ, \succcurlyeq)$ with $\succ$ = "$s$-depth of the marked recursive argument" is:

- **syntactic** — defined purely on the term structure, no arithmetic semantics;
- **well-founded** — bounded below by an integer;
- **stable under the original rules** — because those rules don't increase the $s$-depth of any marked argument;
- **strict on the DPs (a), (c), (d), (f)** — direct inspection of the argument;
- **compatible on the "wrapper" DPs (b), (e)** — these are root-changing, so they go in $\succcurlyeq$, and the framework's termination theorem handles the rest.

## 5. What I deliberately did *not* do

- I did not invoke the standard model of Peano arithmetic or "$\mathbb{N}$ semantics." The question forbids importing object-level axioms, and the reduction-pair argument is fully syntactic.
- I did not add hidden rewrite rules (e.g., "treat $p(s(x))$ as $x$" or "expand $\mathsf{times}$ definitionally"). The decrease in $s$-depth at step (f) is derived from the *given* rules $p(s(s(x))) \to s(p(s(x)))$ and $p(s(0)) \to 0$, not from an additional rule.
- I did not strengthen the system with side conditions (e.g., "only apply rule 8 when $x \ne 0$") — the argument works for the bare rules.

## Summary

I picked the DP framework with a syntactic reduction pair because:

1. **Polynomial interpretations are provably insufficient** (the fac/times interaction forces super-polynomial growth).
2. **Flat measures don't separate the recursions** (the times-wrapper hides the fac-recursion's decrease).
3. **Plain RPO has a precedence conflict** (rule 6 wants $p > s$, rule 8 wants $s > p$).
4. **DP + reduction pair** is the standard, fully syntactic tool for systems where (i) each symbol's recursion is well-founded in a local measure, and (ii) the symbols are mutually recursive but with different "shapes" of recursion. The example here is essentially a textbook demonstration of that pattern.

A practical aside: the method scales. If someone added, say, $\mathsf{ack}(s(x),s(y)) \to \mathsf{ack}(\dots)$ with the same wrapper structure, the same reduction pair would extend, whereas the polynomial method would break in exactly the same way as for $\mathsf{fac}$.
