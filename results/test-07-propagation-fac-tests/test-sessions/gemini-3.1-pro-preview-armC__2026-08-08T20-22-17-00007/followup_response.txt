I chose the **Dependency Pair (DP) framework paired with a polynomial interpretation** because it is the most elegant and standard way to overcome a specific hurdle in this rewrite system: the **rapid mathematical growth and nested structural evaluation** of the `fac` (factorial) rule.

Here is a breakdown of exactly why simpler methods fail here, and why the DP framework was the perfect fit:

### 1. Why simpler structural orderings (LPO / KBO) fail
Syntactic path orderings like Lexicographic Path Ordering (LPO) or Knuth-Bendix Ordering (KBO) prove termination by checking if the right-hand side of a rule is structurally smaller than the left. 
Look at the factorial rule:
`fac(s(x)) -> times(fac(p(s(x))), s(x))`
* The recursive call `fac(...)` is wrapped inside `times`. Structural orderings struggle when a recursive call is buried inside a new function symbol.
* The argument passed to the recursive call is `p(s(x))`. Structurally, `p(s(x))` is actually *larger* (more symbols) than the left-hand argument `s(x)`. It only becomes smaller *after* the `p` rules are applied. LPO and KBO cannot easily look ahead to see that `p(s(x))` will eventually evaluate to something smaller.

### 2. Why a direct Polynomial Interpretation fails
If we tried to map these terms to polynomials *without* Dependency Pairs, we would have to prove that the left side of every rule is **strictly greater** ($>$) than the right side. 
Because `fac` represents factorial growth, and `times` represents multiplication, finding standard polynomials where $P_{fac}(x + 1) > P_{times}(P_{fac}(x), x + 1)$ is mathematically complicated (or impossible with simple linear polynomials) because polynomials cannot bound exponential or factorial growth.

### 3. Why Dependency Pairs (DP) solve this perfectly
The Dependency Pair framework brilliantly separates the *rules of the system* from the *loops of the system*. 
Instead of proving that the entire right side of `fac(s(x)) -> times(fac(p(s(x))), s(x))` gets strictly smaller, the DP framework says:
*"We only care about the recursive loop. Just prove that the argument going into the next `FAC` is strictly smaller, and ensure the rest of the rules don't blow up (weakly decreasing)."*

This is why I picked it. By reducing the strict requirement ($>$) to a weak requirement ($\ge$) for the main rules, it gave me a massive mathematical loophole: **I could interpret the `fac` symbol as zero ($I(fac(x)) = 0$).**

By making $I(fac(x)) = 0$, I completely neutralized the explosive growth of the `times` function on the right side of the rule, turning a nightmare inequality into a trivial $0 \ge 0$. Then, the DP framework only required me to prove that the recursive step `FAC(s(x)) -> FAC(p(s(x)))` was strictly decreasing, which was easy to do by assigning a simple polynomial to evaluate `s` and `p`.

**In short:** I chose this method because it is the standard modern technique for proving termination of systems with nested evaluation (like `p(s(x))`) and rapidly growing mathematical functions, bypassing limitations that would make older syntactic or direct polynomial methods fail.
