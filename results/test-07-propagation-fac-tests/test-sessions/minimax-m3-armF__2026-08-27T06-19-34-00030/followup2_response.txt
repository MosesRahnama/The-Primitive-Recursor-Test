Yes, it complied. Let me be explicit about why, and honest about the boundary.

## What the method uses

The Dependency Pair framework + polynomial interpretation over ℕ is a **meta-theorem about rewrite systems**: it takes the rules as input and produces a termination certificate. The proof infrastructure it relies on is:

- The well-foundedness of (ℕ, >) — a foundational mathematical fact.
- The fact that polynomials with non-negative integer coefficients are monotone in their arguments.
- The Dependency Pair Theorem (Arts & Giesl) — a meta-theorem that reduces termination to finitely many numerical inequalities.

None of these are added to the system under proof. They are part of the proof-checking apparatus, in the same way that "ranking functions" are part of program verification's proof apparatus.

## What the method does NOT do

- It does **not** add new rewrite rules. The six rules are the only rules used.
- It does **not** add guards, conditions, or side constraints. The interpretation is a uniform mapping on all terms.
- It does **not** assume the rewrite rules compute anything specific. The interpretation is independent of what `plus` and `times` "should" compute — it only depends on the syntactic form of the terms.
- It does **not** import object-level axioms like "∀x. plus(x,0) = x" or "∀x. plus(s(x),y) = s(plus(x,y))" as lemmas. Those are not needed because the proof is purely about decrease of a numeric measure under each rule.

## Where the boundary sits

The interpretation assigns:
- `0 ↦ 0`, `s ↦ x+1` — these treat `0` and `s` as free constructors (which is what the syntax says they are).
- `plus ↦ x+y`, `times ↦ y·(x+1)` — these are **my choice as the prover**, not axioms about what `plus` and `times` mean.

A different choice of monotone polynomial could have failed to decrease on some rule, in which case I would have had to pick a different one. The proof works for **any** such assignment that satisfies the inequalities, so the choice is a proof-discovery move, not an object-level commitment.

## The one thing to be honest about

The only "import" is the well-founded semiring ℕ itself. If you want a proof that uses **zero** external mathematical structure — i.e., a proof whose justification chain starts and ends inside the rules' syntax — then the right tool is a **purely syntactic recursive/lexicographic path order**, not a polynomial interpretation. Both are standard; LPO/RPO is just more austere.

For example, an LPO-style argument: each rule decreases the LPO with status `times > plus > s > 0` and appropriate argument priorities (for `times`, prioritize argument 2; for `plus`, prioritize both arguments). That's a one-pass syntactic check with no ℕ involved.

So my method complied with the stated rules, but if you want the most conservative possible interpretation of "from the rules alone," a path-order argument is even cleaner. The DP + polynomial proof is on the safe side of the line, just not on the absolute minimum side.
