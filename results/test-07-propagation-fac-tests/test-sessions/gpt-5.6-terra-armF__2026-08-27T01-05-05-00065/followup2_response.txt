Yes. The proposed LPO proof complies with those requirements.

The proof uses only:

- the finite first-order signature appearing in the rules:
  `times`, `plus`, `s`, `0`;
- the syntactic form of the left- and right-hand sides;
- a chosen meta-level symbol precedence

  \[
  \mathsf{times} > \mathsf{plus} > \mathsf{s} > 0;
  \]

- the standard well-foundedness theorem for lexicographic path ordering on a finite signature with a well-founded precedence.

That precedence is not an object-level axiom about the terms. In particular, it does **not** assert that multiplication is mathematically larger than addition, that `s` denotes successor, or that `0` denotes a number. It is merely a parameter of the termination ordering used by the proof.

Likewise, facts such as

\[
s(y) > y
\]

are not imported equations or structural axioms of the rewrite system. They are consequences of the definition of LPO: a term is greater than each of its proper subterms. This is a meta-level property of the ordering on syntax trees.

No rewrite rules were added, and none of the given rules was restricted by a guard or side condition. The proof applies to arbitrary first-order substitutions for `x` and `y`, including substitutions by arbitrary terms over the displayed signature.

One minor technical detail: in standard formulations of lexicographic LPO, when comparing

\[
plus(s(x),y) > plus(x,y),
\]

the decrease in the first argument, \(s(x)>x\), is sufficient, while any required comparison with subsequent right-hand arguments also holds because \(y\) is a subterm of \(plus(s(x),y)\). Thus the sixth rule is oriented without any extra assumption.

Therefore the argument is entirely internal to ordinary first-order term rewriting theory: it proves that every rewrite step strictly decreases a well-founded syntactic ordering, hence no infinite rewrite sequence exists.
