I picked LPO because the rules have the characteristic shape that LPO handles directly:

- Recursive calls are structurally smaller in one argument:
  - `times(x, s(y))` calls `times(x, y)`;
  - `plus(x, s(y))` calls `plus(x, y)`;
  - `plus(s(x), y)` calls `plus(x, y)`.
- The outer defined symbol either stays the same on a lexicographically smaller argument tuple, or decreases in precedence:
  - `times` produces `plus`;
  - `plus` produces `s`;
  - the remaining rules reduce to a subterm.

Thus the precedence

\[
\mathsf{times} > \mathsf{plus} > \mathsf{s} > 0
\]

matches the visible dependency structure of the rules:

\[
\mathsf{times} \leadsto \mathsf{plus} \leadsto \mathsf{s}.
\]

Lexicographic status for `times` and `plus` then captures the argument decreases:

- for multiplication, the second argument changes from `s(y)` to `y`;
- for the fifth addition rule, the second argument changes from `s(y)` to `y`;
- for the sixth addition rule, the first argument changes from `s(x)` to `x`.

So LPO offers one uniform, syntax-only proof: it needs no interpretation of `0` or `s` as natural-number constructors, no semantic model, and no added assumptions such as associativity or commutativity.

Other standard approaches could also work—for example, a polynomial or matrix interpretation, or a dependency-pair argument—but LPO is especially direct here because every rule is visibly justified by either a subterm decrease, a precedence decrease, or a lexicographic structural decrease.
