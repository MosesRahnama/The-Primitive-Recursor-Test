I chose the **Lexicographic Path Ordering (LPO)** because this specific Term Rewriting System (TRS) is a textbook implementation of **primitive recursive arithmetic** (addition and multiplication over Peano naturals). LPO is essentially the syntactic embodiment of the structural induction used to define these functions, making it the most natural, direct, and elegant tool for the job.

Here is a detailed breakdown of why LPO was the optimal choice, along with why other standard methods were rejected.

### 1. Why LPO is a Perfect Fit
LPO relies on three core mechanisms, all of which map 1-to-1 with the structure of this TRS:

*   **The Subterm Property (Structural Descent):** In primitive recursion, recursive calls are made on strict subterms of the input. In Rule 2, `times(x, y)` is called on `y`, which is a strict subterm of `s(y)`. LPO natively recognizes that a term is strictly greater than its subterms, effortlessly satisfying the recursive descent without needing arithmetic math.
*   **Precedence (The Call Graph):** The system has a strict hierarchy of operations: multiplication calls addition, and addition calls the successor function. LPO allows us to simply declare `times > plus > s`, which perfectly mirrors the call graph and easily orients rules where the root symbol changes (like Rules 1, 2, 3, and 4).
*   **Lexicographic Status (Argument Reduction):** Rules 5 and 6 keep the root symbol the same (`plus` $\to$ `plus`) but peel off an `s` constructor from one of the arguments. LPO’s lexicographic comparison of argument lists `(x, s(y)) > (x, y)` and `(s(x), y) > (x, y)` handles this exactly as a human would reason about it: "the first argument stayed the same, but the second argument got strictly smaller."

### 2. Why Not Other Standard Methods?

While other methods *can* prove termination for this system, they introduce unnecessary friction, complexity, or "guesswork" compared to LPO.

#### Why not Knuth-Bendix Ordering (KBO)?
KBO is a close cousin to LPO but relies on a **weight function** in addition to precedence. KBO struggles (or at least becomes highly unintuitive) when variables are duplicated on the right-hand side. 
*   Look at **Rule 2**: `times(x, s(y)) -> plus(times(x, y), x)`. 
*   The variable `x` appears **once** on the LHS but **twice** on the RHS. 
*   In KBO, if a variable occurs more times on the RHS, the LHS *must* be strictly heavier than the RHS purely by weight (precedence cannot save you). This forces the inequality: $w(\text{times}) + w(x) + w(s) + w(y) > w(\text{plus}) + w(\text{times}) + w(y) + 2w(x)$, which simplifies to $w(s) > w(\text{plus}) + w(x)$. 
*   Because variable weights must be $\ge 1$ for KBO admissibility, you are forced to assign an artificially high weight to the simple constructor `s` (e.g., $w(s) \ge 2$) just to satisfy the duplication in Rule 2. LPO avoids this entirely because it doesn't use weights; it just uses the subterm property and precedence.

#### Why not Polynomial Interpretations?
Polynomial interpretations map terms to polynomials over $\mathbb{N}$ (e.g., interpreting `plus(x,y)` as $x+y$ and `times(x,y)` as $x \cdot y$). 
*   While this works conceptually, it requires **guessing the right polynomials** and then manually solving non-linear inequalities to prove that LHS $>$ RHS for every rule. 
*   For example, proving Rule 2 requires showing $x \cdot (y + 1) > (x \cdot y) + x$, which simplifies to $xy + x > xy + x$. Wait—that's not strictly greater! To make polynomial interpretations work, you have to add constants (e.g., interpreting `plus` as $x+y+1$), which requires trial-and-error tweaking. LPO requires zero arithmetic guessing; it is purely syntactic.

#### Why not Dependency Pairs?
Dependency Pairs (often combined with the Subterm Criterion or matrix interpretations) is the industrial standard for *automated* termination provers like AProVE or TTT2.
*   However, for a **manual proof**, it is massive overkill. It requires extracting dependency pairs, defining a reduction pair, analyzing the strongly connected components of the dependency graph, and proving that no infinite chains exist. 
*   For a simple, non-mutually-recursive system like this one, writing out a Dependency Pair proof would take pages of boilerplate compared to the half-page LPO proof.

### Summary
I picked LPO because it is the **path of least resistance** for primitive recursive systems. It requires no arithmetic guessing (like Polynomials), no awkward weight tuning to handle variable duplication (like KBO), and no heavy graph-analysis boilerplate (like Dependency Pairs). A simple, easily verifiable precedence (`times > plus > s`) is all that is needed.
