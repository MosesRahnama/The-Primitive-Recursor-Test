Great question! The choice of termination proof method depends on the structure of the rewrite system and the nature of its recursion. Here’s why I picked the **dependency pair (DP) method combined with reduction orders** (specifically, **recursive path order (RPO)** and **polynomial interpretations**) for this system:

---

### **1. Why Dependency Pairs (DP)?**
The **dependency pair framework** is a **modular and systematic** way to prove termination of term rewriting systems. It works by:
- Identifying **potential sources of non-termination** (recursive calls that could loop).
- Breaking the problem into smaller, manageable subproblems (dependency pairs).
- Allowing the use of **reduction orders** (like RPO or polynomial interpretations) to show that recursive calls decrease in a well-founded order.

#### **Why DP was a good fit here:**
- The system has **nested recursion** (e.g., `fac` calls `p`, which calls itself, and `times` calls `plus`).
- DP handles such cases by **isolating the recursive structure** and proving termination for each recursive call separately.
- It avoids the need for a single global order (which might be hard to find for complex systems).

---

### **2. Why Recursive Path Order (RPO)?**
RPO is a **syntactic order** that compares terms based on their structure and a **precedence** (priority) on function symbols. It’s particularly effective for:
- **Constructor-based systems** (like this one, where `0` and `s` are constructors).
- **Lexicographic or multiset recursion** (e.g., `plus` and `times` recurse on one argument while keeping others fixed).

#### **Why RPO worked well here:**
- The `plus` and `times` rules are **primitive recursive**, and RPO easily handles such cases by assigning `s` higher precedence than `plus`/`times`.
- The `p` (predecessor) rules are **structurally recursive**, and RPO can show `p(s(x)) < s(x)` by giving `p` higher precedence than `s`.
- The `fac` rule is the trickiest, but RPO can still handle it by ensuring `p(s(x)) < s(x)` (since `p` has higher precedence than `s`).

---

### **3. Why Not Other Methods?**
#### **(a) Simple Lexicographic Path Order (LPO)**
- LPO is similar to RPO but less flexible (it doesn’t allow multiset status for arguments).
- It would work for `plus` and `times` but might struggle with `fac` because `p(s(x))` is not a subterm of `s(x)`.

#### **(b) Polynomial Interpretations**
- Polynomial interpretations assign **semantic values** (e.g., natural numbers) to terms and show that rewrites decrease these values.
- They work well for **numeric recursion** (e.g., `plus` and `times`), but:
  - They can be **overkill** for simple systems like this.
  - They require **solving inequalities** (e.g., finding coefficients for `s`, `plus`, `times`), which is more work than RPO.
  - They might not easily handle the `fac` rule because `p(s(x))` is not obviously smaller than `s(x)` in a polynomial interpretation.

#### **(c) Semantic Methods (e.g., Well-Founded Models)**
- These are more general but **harder to automate** and often require human insight.
- They’re unnecessary here because the system is **syntactically well-behaved**.

#### **(d) Termination via Innermost Termination**
- Some systems terminate only for **innermost rewriting** (e.g., if outer rewrites could loop but inner ones don’t).
- This system is **fully terminating**, so innermost termination isn’t needed.

---

### **4. Why Not Just Subterm Order?**
- The **subterm order** (where `t > s` if `s` is a subterm of `t`) is **too weak** for this system:
  - It works for `p(s(s(x))) > s(p(s(x)))` (since `s(x)` is a subterm of `s(s(x))`).
  - But it fails for `fac(s(x)) > fac(p(s(x)))` because `p(s(x))` is **not** a subterm of `s(x)` (e.g., `p(s(0)) = 0`, and `0` is not a subterm of `s(0)`).
- Thus, we need a **stronger order** (like RPO) to handle `fac`.

---

### **5. Why DP + RPO Was the Best Choice**
1. **Modularity**: DP breaks the problem into smaller pieces (e.g., `fac`’s recursion is isolated).
2. **Flexibility**: RPO can handle both **lexicographic recursion** (`plus`, `times`) and **structural recursion** (`p`, `fac`).
3. **Automation-Friendly**: DP + RPO is widely supported by termination tools (e.g., AProVE, TTT2).
4. **No Extra Assumptions**: It works purely syntactically, without needing semantic interpretations or additional axioms.

---

### **6. Alternative Approach: Manual Proof by Structural Induction**
One could also prove termination **manually** by:
- Showing that `plus` and `times` terminate (easy, since they’re primitive recursive).
- Showing that `p` terminates (easy, since it reduces the number of `s` constructors).
- Showing that `fac` terminates by arguing that `p(s(x))` is "smaller" than `s(x)` in some sense.

However:
- This is **less rigorous** (harder to formalize for complex systems).
- It doesn’t scale well to larger systems.
- It’s **not machine-checkable** (unlike DP + RPO).

---

### **Final Answer: Why This Method?**
I chose **DP + RPO** because:
1. The system has **nested recursion** (`fac` → `p` → `fac`), and DP handles this cleanly.
2. The rules are **syntactically simple** (constructors `0`/`s`, no variables in non-recursive positions), making RPO a natural fit.
3. It’s a **standard, automatable method** that doesn’t require ad-hoc reasoning.
4. It **scales** to more complex systems (unlike manual proofs).
5. It **avoids overkill** (unlike polynomial interpretations, which would work but are more complex than needed).

The method is **just right** for this system: powerful enough to handle the recursion, but simple enough to apply without unnecessary machinery.
