I chose **Lexicographic Path Ordering (LPO)** for several specific reasons that make it the most elegant, robust, and direct tool for this particular system:

### 1. Purely Syntactic and Self-Contained
The prompt emphasized establishing termination *from the presented rules alone, without importing structural axioms or object-level assumptions*. 
* Had I used **Polynomial Interpretations** (another standard method), I would have had to map the terms to a mathematical domain like the natural numbers ($\mathbb{N}$) and prove inequalities like $a + b > c$ using the axioms of arithmetic. This introduces "external" mathematical theory.
* **LPO**, by contrast, is **purely syntactic**. It operates strictly on the structure of the terms and a simple ordering of the symbols (precedence). It requires no external mathematical domain, making it entirely self-contained.

### 2. Natural Fit for Structural Recursion
The rewrite rules in this system define standard primitive recursive functions (addition, multiplication, and a predecessor-like function $p$):
* **Rule 2 ($\text{plus}(x, s(y)) \to s(\text{plus}(x, y))$)** and **Rule 5 ($\text{times}(s(x), y) \to \text{plus}(\text{times}(x, y), y)$)** are classic structural recursions where a constructor ($s$) is peeled off from an argument in the recursive step.
* LPO is specifically designed to prove the termination of such structurally recursive definitions. It naturally formalizes the intuition that "the arguments to the recursive call are structurally smaller than the arguments to the original call."

### 3. Handling Symbol Dominance (Precedence)
In Rule 5, the root symbol changes from `times` on the left to `plus` on the right. 
* To prove this terminates, we need a way to say that `times` is "more complex" than `plus`, so replacing it is a step downward. 
* LPO does this directly via the precedence $\text{times} \succ \text{plus}$. Once this is established, LPO allows us to decompose the right-hand side and focus purely on showing that the arguments of the recursive `times` call have decreased ($s(x) \succ x$).

### 4. Effortless Handling of Nested Rules
Rule 6 ($p(s(s(x))) \to s(p(s(x)))$) can sometimes be tricky for basic weight-counting arguments because the symbol $s$ appears on both sides. However, LPO handles this effortlessly:
* By setting $p \succ s$, LPO allows the $s$ on the right-hand side to be ignored while it verifies that the inner term $p(s(x))$ is smaller than the left-hand side $p(s(s(x)))$. 
* It then compares the arguments of $p$: $s(s(x))$ vs $s(x)$, which trivially decreases.

By choosing LPO, the termination proof is reduced to a simple, finite, and completely checkable set of syntactic comparisons, leaving no room for ambiguity or reliance on external mathematical frameworks.
