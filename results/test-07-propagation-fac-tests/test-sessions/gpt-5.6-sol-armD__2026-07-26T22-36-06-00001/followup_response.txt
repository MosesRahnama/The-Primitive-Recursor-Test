I chose **lexicographic path ordering (LPO)** because the rules exhibit structural recursion, but not uniform decrease in ordinary term size.

In particular:

- `plus` recurses on its **second argument**:
  \[
  \mathsf{plus}(x,s(y))\to s(\mathsf{plus}(x,y)).
  \]
- `times` recurses on its **first argument**:
  \[
  \mathsf{times}(s(x),y)\to
  \mathsf{plus}(\mathsf{times}(x,y),y).
  \]
- `p` removes one `s` from its argument while adding an `s` outside:
  \[
  \mathsf p(s(s(x)))\to s(\mathsf p(s(x))).
  \]

A simple term-size measure is therefore unsuitable: some rules preserve size, and the multiplication rule can increase size by duplicating the occurrence of \(y\).

LPO is a natural fit because it combines:

1. **The subterm property**, handling rules whose right-hand side is a subterm or contains smaller recursive calls.
2. **Lexicographic argument comparison**, recognizing that  
   \(\mathsf{plus}(x,s(y))\) decreases to a call with second argument \(y\), while  
   \(\mathsf{times}(s(x),y)\) decreases to a call with first argument \(x\).
3. **Root-symbol precedence**, allowing a recursive symbol to dominate constructors introduced around or above the recursive call:
   \[
   \mathsf{times}\succ\mathsf{plus},\qquad
   \mathsf{plus}\succ\mathsf s,\qquad
   \mathsf p\succ\mathsf s.
   \]

Thus the single precedence

\[
\mathsf{times}\succ\mathsf{plus}\succ\mathsf p\succ\mathsf s\succ 0
\]

captures all three recursion patterns at once.

Most importantly for the stated boundary, LPO is **entirely syntactic**. It does not interpret `0`, `s`, `plus`, `times`, or `p` as natural-number operations, and it requires no induction axioms, constructor freeness assumptions, semantic equations, extra guards, or added rewrite rules. Once every given rule is strictly oriented, standard well-foundedness and closure under substitutions and contexts directly establish termination.
