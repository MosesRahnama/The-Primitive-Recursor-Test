**Verdict: yes.** Termination follows from a standard lexicographic path ordering (LPO) on finite first-order terms, without assuming that terms denote natural numbers or that the third argument has any particular shape.

Choose a precedence containing

\[
F \succ G
\]

with the other symbols ordered arbitrarily, and give \(F\) lexicographic status with argument order \(1,2,3\).

### Rule 1

\[
F(x,y,Z)\to x
\]

The right-hand side \(x\) is a proper subterm of the left-hand side. Hence

\[
F(x,y,Z) >_{\mathrm{LPO}} x.
\]

### Rule 2

\[
F(x,y,S(n))\to G\bigl(y,F(x,y,n)\bigr).
\]

Let \(L=F(x,y,S(n))\). Since \(F\succ G\), it suffices in LPO to show that \(L\) is greater than both arguments of the right-hand \(G\).

First,

\[
L>_{\mathrm{LPO}}y
\]

because \(y\) is a proper subterm of \(L\).

Second, compare \(L\) with \(F(x,y,n)\). Both have root \(F\), and their first two arguments are identical. At the third argument,

\[
S(n)>_{\mathrm{LPO}}n
\]

by the proper-subterm property. Therefore, by the lexicographic status of \(F\),

\[
F(x,y,S(n))>_{\mathrm{LPO}}F(x,y,n).
\]

Consequently,

\[
F(x,y,S(n))
>_{\mathrm{LPO}}
G\bigl(y,F(x,y,n)\bigr).
\]

LPO is well-founded on finite first-order terms and is closed under substitutions and contexts, so every rewrite step strictly decreases this ordering, including steps occurring inside larger terms. Thus no infinite rewrite sequence exists.

This argument uses only the syntactic subterm structure of the displayed terms; it does not require \(n\) to be a numeral, any induction axiom, freeness assumptions beyond ordinary finite first-order term syntax, or additional rewrite rules or guards.
