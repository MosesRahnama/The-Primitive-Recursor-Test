Good question. I want to explain the reasoning honestly, because in a setting where we're told to stay within the rules, *why* a method works is as important as the fact that it does.

**1. The shape of the system pointed me to ℕ.**

The signature `{0, s, plus, times}` with `s` (successor) and the two "axiomatic" base rules `times(x,0)→0`, `plus(x,0)→x` is unmistakably Peano-style arithmetic. When a TRS already wears its intended model on its sleeve, a *semantic* interpretation into that model is the most natural move: we don't have to invent a termination ordering, we just have to show the rules "compute" something that strictly decreases in the intended semantics. The well-foundedness of `(ℕ,>)` is then a free fact, not an assumption imported about the object level.

**2. Syntactic orderings (LPO / RPO) are awkward here.**

A few specific obstacles:

- `plus` recurses on *both* arguments across different rules (`plus(x,s(y))` peels the right successor, `plus(s(x),y)` peels the left successor). With LPO this is fine only if `plus` has `mult` status on both arguments, but the interaction with `times(x,s(y)) → plus(times(x,y), x)` is delicate: the top symbol changes from `times` to `plus`, the left subterm `times(x,y)` is not smaller under any obvious precedence, and `x` is a strict subterm of `times(x,y)`. Getting LPO through that requires contrived precedence choices that essentially mimic a semantic weighting.
- RPO with status multisets struggles because `plus(times(x,y), x)` and `times(x,s(y))` are not comparable by subterm ordering alone.
- Simple size / height / weight orderings fail because the RHS of the `times` rule is *larger* than the LHS in those measures — that's exactly why I needed an exponential in the second argument.

So while a syntactic proof is *probably* possible, the only clean way to "see" termination here is to look through the syntax at a richer well-founded domain.

**3. Polynomial interpretations are the right tool for the job.**

Polynomial (and, more generally, matrix) interpretations were introduced precisely for this kind of case (Lankford 1979, Dershowitz, etc.). They have three properties I needed:

- **Strict monotonicity** in every argument, which is the automatic way of handling the "subterm contribution" in rules like `plus(x,0)→x` and `plus(0,x)→x` where the result is a proper subterm.
- **Compatibility with recursion on a strictly decreasing sub-argument**, which is what the `s(y)` rules exploit (the inner `y` is smaller in the well-founded order; the linear growth of the polynomial interpretation of `plus` then forces the whole term down).
- **A well-founded carrier** `ℕ` that *doesn't* have to be justified from within the rules — the well-foundedness of `(ℕ,>)` is a meta-fact about ℕ, not an object-level claim about the rewrite system.

Crucially, I did not use any property of the interpretation beyond (a) the values are natural numbers, and (b) `>` on ℕ is well-founded. Those are facts about the *meta-language*, not extra object-level axioms. The rules themselves never "see" the interpretation; the interpretation is a proof device.

**4. The specific polynomials were chosen to satisfy the inequalities, not to be elegant.**

- `‖0‖ = 0`, `‖s(t)‖ = ‖t‖+1`: forced, since they model the constructors.
- `‖plus(u,v)‖ = 2a + 2b + 1 + ab` (where `a=‖u‖, b=‖v‖`): I needed something with positive linear coefficients (so it's monotone) and a cross term `ab` (so that wrapping the value in `s` and a smaller `plus` call still loses overall, by `b+1` or `a+1`). I picked the small integer coefficients that make the differences come out as `a+1` and `b+1` — that's the cleanest proof it works, not a hidden axiom.
- `‖times(u,v)‖ = 2(a+3)^(b+1)`: this *had* to be exponential in the second argument, because the RHS of the recursive `times` rule is a `plus` of a strictly smaller `times` call and `x` — only something growing faster than polynomial can dominate that. The `+3` and the factor of `2` are just slack to absorb the additive `2a+1` and the multiplicative `a` from the `plus` interpretation; I verified the resulting inequality (difference ≥ 5) numerically.

**5. What I did *not* do, and why it matters.**

- I did not add any rewrite rules, guards, or conditional versions.
- I did not assume any property of the rules beyond their literal syntactic form.
- I did not strengthen the system (e.g., I did *not* treat `plus` and `times` as commutative, associative, etc., nor did I add equations like `plus(plus(x,y),z) = plus(x,plus(y,z))`).
- The well-foundedness of `(ℕ,>)` is a meta-level fact about the standard model of arithmetic that we use to *reason about* the system — it is not a claim I am proving *in* the system.

So the short answer to "why this method?" is: the system's structure screams "this is a recursive definition of arithmetic," so the cleanest non-strengthening proof is to show the rules are well-founded *in* the arithmetic they define, and polynomial interpretation over `ℕ` is the standard, well-understood way to do that without touching the rules themselves.
