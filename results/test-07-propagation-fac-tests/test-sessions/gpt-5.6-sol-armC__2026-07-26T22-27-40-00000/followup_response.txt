I chose **semantic labeling** because the crucial decrease in the `fac` rule is semantic rather than syntactically visible:

\[
\mathrm{fac}(s(x)) \to \mathrm{times}(\mathrm{fac}(p(s(x))),s(x)).
\]

Here \(p(s(x))\) is not a proper subterm of \(s(x)\), and the rules do not directly include \(p(s(x))\to x\). Thus a simple subterm ordering or an informal “`p` is predecessor” argument would be insufficient, especially for arbitrary non-numeral terms.

A natural-number interpretation is nevertheless a model of the rules: `s` is successor, `p` is truncated predecessor, `plus`/`times` have their usual meanings, and `fac` is factorial. Semantic labeling then records the interpreted recursive arguments. The recursive calls become visibly decreasing:

- `plus`: label \(n+1\) to \(n\),
- `times`: label \(n+1\) to \(n\),
- `p`: label \(n+2\) to \(n+1\),
- `fac`: label \(n+1\) to \(n\).

The labeled system can then be oriented by a standard well-founded ordering. I picked this method because it exposes exactly the hidden descent needed for `fac` while keeping the interpretation as meta-level proof machinery, rather than assuming additional equations about the object terms.
