I picked a **polynomial interpretation into the natural numbers** because it is a direct, self-contained termination proof method that fits this TRS well.

The system has two features that make a simple syntactic measure less obvious:

- `times(s(x), y)` rewrites to `plus(times(x, y), y)`, so the right-hand side is structurally larger and introduces a surrounding `plus`.
- `p(s(s(x)))` rewrites to `s(p(s(x)))`, which moves the recursive `p` inward by only one successor while retaining an outer `s`.

Thus raw term size, symbol count, or depth do not uniformly decrease. For example, in

\[
times(s(x),y)\to plus(times(x,y),y),
\]

the right-hand side generally contains more function-symbol occurrences than the left.

A polynomial interpretation permits assigning a numerical “cost” to terms in a way that reflects the intended descent:

- the first argument of `times` is given enough weight that replacing `s(x)` by `x` outweighs the newly created `plus` and copied `y`;
- the second argument of `plus` is weighted sufficiently to make
  \[
  plus(x,s(y))\to s(plus(x,y))
  \]
  decrease;
- `p` is weighted so that
  \[
  p(s(s(x)))\to s(p(s(x)))
  \]
  decreases.

The particular coefficients were chosen to satisfy the resulting inequalities. For instance:

- Taking
  \[
  plus(m,n)=m+2n+1
  \]
  makes the second `plus` rule decrease by one:
  \[
  m+2(n+1)+1 > (m+2n+1)+1.
  \]

- Taking
  \[
  p(n)=2n
  \]
  makes the `p` recursive rule decrease by one:
  \[
  2(n+2)>2(n+1)+1.
  \]

- Taking
  \[
  times(m,n)=3mn+2m+n+1
  \]
  makes the loss of one successor in the first argument dominate the `plus(...,y)` introduced on the right:
  \[
  times(x+1,y)>plus(times(x,y),y).
  \]

So this method was chosen because it proves termination of all rules uniformly, handles rewriting at arbitrary subterm positions through monotonicity, and relies solely on the syntax and rewrite rules provided.
