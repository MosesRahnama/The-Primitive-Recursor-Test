I picked LPO because the rules have the exact shape it is designed to handle:

- The recursive-looking rule changes the root symbol from \(F\) to \(G\):
  \[
  F(x,y,S(n)) \to G(y,F(x,y,n)).
  \]
  Giving \(F\) higher precedence than \(G\) makes that outer change decreasing.

- The right side still contains an \(F\)-term, so root precedence alone is not enough. But that nested call has a structurally smaller third argument:
  \[
  S(n) \succ n.
  \]
  With lexicographic status for \(F\), this yields
  \[
  F(x,y,S(n)) \succ F(x,y,n).
  \]

- The other rule is immediate by the subterm property:
  \[
  F(x,y,Z) \to x.
  \]

Thus LPO proves each rule decreases using only the syntax of the terms and the two rewrite rules. It does not rely on interpreting \(S\) as a natural-number successor, \(Z\) as zero, or on assuming that terms have any intended arithmetic meaning.

A direct “count the number of \(S\)’s” argument would be less robust as a global termination proof, because the variables may be instantiated by arbitrary terms and rewriting may occur inside them. LPO automatically handles arbitrary substitutions and reductions in all contexts.
