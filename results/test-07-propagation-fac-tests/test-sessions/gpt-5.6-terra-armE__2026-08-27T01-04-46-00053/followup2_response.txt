Yes. The LPO argument complies with those requirements.

It introduces no new rewrite rules, equations, guards, conditions on substitutions, or assumptions about the intended meanings of \(Z\), \(S\), \(F\), or \(G\). In particular, it does **not** assume:

- that \(Z\) denotes zero,
- that \(S\) is a successor on natural numbers,
- that \(n\) is a numeral,
- that \(x,y,n\) are irreducible,
- that rewriting occurs only at the root,
- or any algebraic laws such as injectivity of \(S\), freeness of constructors, associativity, etc.

The choices

\[
F \succ G
\]

and “lexicographic status for \(F\)” are not object-level axioms or changes to the rewrite system. They are parameters of a meta-level termination ordering used to prove that the already-given rewrite relation is well-founded.

Likewise, the fact used in the proof,

\[
S(n) \succ n,
\]

is not an assumption that \(S\) decreases some externally supplied numerical value. It is the syntactic subterm property built into LPO: a finite term is greater in the ordering than each of its proper subterms. This is a standard metatheoretic fact about first-order term syntax.

The only general background used is what is inherent in applying a standard termination method to a first-order TRS: terms are ordinary finite first-order terms, and LPO is a well-founded, substitution-stable, context-stable ordering. Those are properties of the proof method, not added rules or object-level assumptions.

So, under the prompt’s explicit permission to use standard proof methods, the proof remains within the stated boundary. If “no structural axioms” were instead read to prohibit even meta-level properties of first-order syntax or of a termination ordering, then essentially no ordinary termination proof method—including dependency pairs, polynomial interpretations, recursive path orderings, or induction on term structure—would be available. But that would conflict with the prompt’s permission to use standard methods.
