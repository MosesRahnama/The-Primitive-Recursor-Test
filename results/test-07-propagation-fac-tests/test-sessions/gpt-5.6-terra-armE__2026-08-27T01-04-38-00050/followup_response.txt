I picked recursive path ordering because the rules have the characteristic shape it handles directly:

- The base rule removes an outer `F`:
  \[
  F(x,y,Z)\to x.
  \]
  Any simplification ordering with the subterm property immediately sees the left side as larger than one of its arguments.

- The recursive rule changes the outer symbol from `F` to `G`:
  \[
  F(x,y,S(n))\to G(y,F(x,y,n)),
  \]
  while the recursive `F` call is on the syntactically smaller argument \(n\) rather than \(S(n)\).

RPO captures both facts without assigning semantic meanings to symbols:

1. Declare \(F > G\), so replacing an outer `F` by an outer `G` is decreasing, provided the left-hand side dominates the arguments of the resulting `G`.

2. Give `F` lexicographic status. Then
   \[
   F(x,y,S(n)) > F(x,y,n)
   \]
   because the first two arguments are unchanged and \(S(n)>n\) by the purely syntactic subterm property.

That makes the recursive occurrence harmless even though it is placed underneath `G`.

I chose it rather than, for example, a direct “number of `S` constructors decreases” measure because such a measure alone does not obviously decrease globally: the second rule retains `S(n)` nowhere but introduces a nested `F`, and a naive symbol-count measure may increase or fail to compare the terms. A semantic natural-number interpretation could also prove termination, but it would require designing suitable interpretations and checking strict decrease. RPO is shorter and stays maximally close to the raw term syntax and rewrite structure.
