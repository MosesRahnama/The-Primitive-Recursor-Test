Use a recursive path ordering (RPO) with:

- precedence \(F > G\);
- any placement of \(S\) and \(Z\) in the precedence, since it is irrelevant here;
- lexicographic status for \(F\).

RPO is well-founded, stable under substitutions, and closed under contexts. It therefore suffices to orient each rule strictly from left to right.

1. **Rule**
   \[
   F(x,y,Z) \to x.
   \]

   The right-hand side \(x\) is a direct subterm/argument of the left-hand side \(F(x,y,Z)\). By the subterm property of RPO,
   \[
   F(x,y,Z) > x.
   \]

2. **Rule**
   \[
   F(x,y,S(n)) \to G(y,F(x,y,n)).
   \]

   Since \(F > G\), it is enough under RPO to show that
   \[
   F(x,y,S(n)) > y
   \quad\text{and}\quad
   F(x,y,S(n)) > F(x,y,n).
   \]

   The first comparison holds because \(y\) is a direct argument of the left-hand side:
   \[
   F(x,y,S(n)) > y.
   \]

   For the second, both terms have root symbol \(F\), and \(F\) has lexicographic status. Their first two arguments agree, while their third arguments satisfy
   \[
   S(n) > n
   \]
   by the subterm property. Hence
   \[
   F(x,y,S(n)) > F(x,y,n).
   \]

   Therefore, using \(F > G\),
   \[
   F(x,y,S(n)) > G(y,F(x,y,n)).
   \]

Thus every rewrite step strictly decreases a well-founded RPO. Consequently, there can be no infinite rewrite sequence.
