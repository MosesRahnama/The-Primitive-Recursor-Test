Yes. The method complies with those requirements.

The chosen precedence and status—e.g. \(F > G\) and lexicographic comparison for \(F\)—are not added rewrite rules, object-level axioms, guards, or assumptions about what the symbols mean. They are parameters of a standard meta-level termination ordering used to certify that the existing rules decrease.

The proof uses only syntactic facts inherent in first-order terms:

- A term is greater than its proper subterms in RPO, such as
  \[
  S(n) > n,\qquad F(x,y,Z)>x,\qquad F(x,y,S(n))>y.
  \]
- The two occurrences of `F` in the second rule can be compared lexicographically:
  \[
  F(x,y,S(n)) > F(x,y,n)
  \]
  because their first two arguments are identical and \(S(n)>n\).
- Choosing \(F>G\) then makes the outer-symbol change decreasing:
  \[
  F(x,y,S(n)) > G(y,F(x,y,n)).
  \]

None of that asserts, for example, that:

- `Z` is a natural-number zero;
- `S` is successor, injective, or distinct from `Z`;
- every term is either `Z` or `S(n)`;
- terms have a numerical size with some externally assumed behavior;
- additional reductions or equations are available.

The only relevant variable condition also holds syntactically: every variable on a right-hand side occurs on its corresponding left-hand side. That is already true of the displayed rules, not an added restriction:

\[
\operatorname{Var}(x)\subseteq\operatorname{Var}(F(x,y,Z)),
\]
and
\[
\operatorname{Var}(G(y,F(x,y,n)))
=
\{x,y,n\}
=
\operatorname{Var}(F(x,y,S(n))).
\]

So the ordering is a proof device, not a strengthening of the rewrite system. Under the prompt’s allowance of “any standard proof method,” RPO is within the stated boundary.
