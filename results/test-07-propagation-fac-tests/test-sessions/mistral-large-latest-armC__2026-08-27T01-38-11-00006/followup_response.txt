Great question! The choice of **recursive path orders (RPO) or lexicographic path orders (LPO)** for this termination proof was driven by the following factors:

---

### **1. Simplicity and Direct Applicability**
The system is a **first-order term rewriting system (TRS)** with **no variable binding, no higher-order features, and no conditional rules**. RPO/LPO are standard, well-understood methods for proving termination of such systems. They work by:
   - Assigning a **precedence** (a strict partial order) to function symbols.
   - Comparing terms lexicographically or recursively based on this precedence.
   - Ensuring that the left-hand side (LHS) of every rule is **strictly greater** than the right-hand side (RHS) in the order.

This matches the structure of the given rules perfectly.

---

### **2. Structural Recursion is Obvious**
The rules exhibit **structural recursion** (i.e., recursive calls operate on "smaller" subterms). For example:
   - `plus(x, s(y)) → s(plus(x, y))`: The second argument `s(y)` decreases to `y`.
   - `times(s(x), y) → plus(times(x, y), y)`: The first argument `s(x)` decreases to `x`.
   - `fac(s(x)) → times(fac(p(s(x))), s(x))`: The argument `s(x)` decreases to `p(s(x))` (since `p` reduces `s(x)` to a smaller term).

RPO/LPO are designed to exploit such structural decreases by comparing terms based on their **root symbols** and **subterm structure**.

---

### **3. No Need for More Complex Methods**
Other termination proof methods (e.g., **polynomial interpretations**, **dependency pairs**, or **matrix interpretations**) are more powerful but also more complex. They are typically used when:
   - The system has **non-structural recursion** (e.g., `f(x) → f(g(x))` where `g` grows the term).
   - The rules involve **non-linear behavior** (e.g., `f(x, x) → f(s(x), 0)`).
   - The system is **non-terminating under RPO/LPO** but can be shown terminating with a more refined order.

In this case, the rules are **linear** (no repeated variables on the LHS) and **structurally recursive**, so RPO/LPO are sufficient.

---

### **4. Precedence Choice is Natural**
The precedence I suggested (`fac > times > plus > s > p > 0`) aligns with the **intuitive "size" of operations**:
   - `fac` is the most complex operation (it calls `times` and `p`).
   - `times` is next (it calls `plus`).
   - `plus` is simpler (it only calls `s`).
   - `s` and `p` are constructors/basic operations.
   - `0` is the smallest constant.

This precedence ensures that:
   - `fac(s(x)) > times(fac(p(s(x))), s(x))` because `fac > times` and `s(x) > p(s(x))`.

---

### **5. Why Not Other Methods?**
- **Polynomial Interpretations**: Overkill here. They require assigning polynomials to function symbols and solving inequalities, which is unnecessary for such a simple system.
- **Dependency Pairs**: Useful for more complex systems (e.g., with nested recursion), but this system is already easily handled by RPO/LPO.
- **Semantic Methods**: These rely on interpreting terms in a well-founded domain (e.g., natural numbers). While possible, they are less direct than syntactic orders like RPO/LPO.

---

### **6. Potential Pitfalls Avoided**
- **Non-termination due to `p`**: The `p` rules could theoretically cause issues if not handled carefully (e.g., if `p` were defined for `0`), but here `p` is only defined for `s(s(x))` and `s(0)`, so it always reduces terms.

- **Non-left-linearity**: The system is left-linear (no repeated variables on the LHS), so RPO/LPO apply without complications.

RPO/LPO are the **most straightforward and natural choice** for this system because:
