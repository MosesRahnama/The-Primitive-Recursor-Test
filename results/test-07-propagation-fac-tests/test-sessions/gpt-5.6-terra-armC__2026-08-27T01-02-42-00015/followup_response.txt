I picked a syntactic structural/dependency analysis because the question expressly excludes importing the intended arithmetic meaning of the symbols. In particular, it would not be legitimate to say “`p` is predecessor” or “`fac` decreases its natural-number argument” unless that behavior were established from the rules.

The useful facts are instead visible in the rewrite shapes:

- recursive calls of `plus`, `times`, and `p` are controlled by exposed `s` structure;
- no rule rewrites a term into one with a new unbounded supply of such structure in the relevant recursive position;
- `fac` is the only nontrivial self-recursion, and its next potential `fac` redex can be exposed only through the finite `p`/`s` structure already present in its argument.

Dependency pairs are a natural formal framework for exactly this situation: they analyze recursive-call cycles syntactically, rather than assigning external meanings to symbols. A simple “term gets smaller by total symbol count” argument would not be appropriate, since some rules preserve or increase size, such as the `p` rule and the expansion of `times`.

More precisely, my earlier answer used “dependency pairs” as the kind of termination proof one would pursue, not as a fully displayed certificate. The key reason for choosing that route was to avoid relying on unstated arithmetic semantics while still handling the nested `fac(p(s(x)))` call.
