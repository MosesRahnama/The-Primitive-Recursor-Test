## Why I picked polynomial interpretations

**1. The system's shape practically demands it.**

Looking at the two rules:
```
F(x, y, Z)    -> x
F(x, y, S(n)) -> G(y, F(x, y, n))
```

The recursion is structurally simple — it's a primitive-recursion-style scheme peeling off `S` constructors one at a time down to `Z`. There's no exotic control flow, no mutual recursion with unpredictable argument permutation, and no need to track multiset comparisons or complex term orderings. This screams "just measure something that decreases," which is exactly what polynomial interpretations do.

**2. I ruled out heavier machinery as overkill.**

- **Recursive Path Ordering (RPO) / Lexicographic Path Ordering (LPO):** These work on precedence and syntactic subterm comparison. They *could* likely handle this too (since `F(x,y,S(n))`'s recursive call `F(x,y,n)` is on a proper subterm), but LPO/RPO proofs are more naturally suited to systems with deep structural nesting or symbol-precedence subtleties. Here, the decrease is essentially arithmetic (structural recursion on the `S`/`Z` argument), so an ordering based on counting/size is more direct and transparent than building a precedence argument.

- **Dependency pairs / semantic labeling:** These are typically reserved for systems where a single global argument doesn't monotonically decrease, or where rules interact in ways that create hidden mutual dependencies (e.g., non-orthogonal system, complex interleaving of defined symbols). Here there are only two rules, one defined symbol `F` (plus `G` which is never rewritten — it's a "constructor" from the rewriting perspective), and the recursion is directly on the third argument. Bringing in dependency pairs would be technically valid but disproportionate to the problem's complexity.

**3. Polynomial interpretations give a self-contained, checkable certificate.**

The prompt's constraint — *no imported axioms, no strengthened system, no hidden assumptions* — pushed me toward a method where the termination argument is a **finite, explicit, verifiable computation**: pick concrete polynomials, plug in, check that both inequalities hold identically over all naturals. This satisfies the "raw assessment" requirement cleanly: I don't need to reason abstractly about the *existence* of a reduction order; I exhibit one and verify it by direct algebra. There's no ambiguity or reliance on an external oracle (like an automated termination tool's internal proof search) — the check is fully transparent in the answer itself.

**4. The interpretation needed had to reflect exactly what the rules "consume."**

Since Rule 2 replaces `S(n)` with `n` and wraps the result in `G`, and since `G` doesn't appear anywhere else, I needed:
- An interpretation of `S` that grows with its argument (so `S(n) > n` numerically) — the natural choice `[S(n)] = n+1`.
- An interpretation of `F` that is sensitive to the third argument in a way that decreases when `S(n)` becomes `n` — hence the `y·n` term, which is what forces the strict inequality to work out for *both* rules simultaneously (the `y·(n+1)` vs `y·n + y` bookkeeping is exactly what makes rule 2's inequality tight but still strict by exactly 1).

That mutual constraint — satisfying both rules with a *single* monotone interpretation — is a classic polynomial-interpretation exercise, and once I saw the shape of rule 2, I could reverse-engineer the needed cross term `y·n` almost immediately, confirming this was the right tool for the specific algebraic obstacle in the problem.

**In short:** the system's small size, its clear single-recursion-parameter structure, and the requirement for an explicit, self-contained, no-extra-assumptions certificate together made polynomial interpretation the natural and most economical choice — powerful enough to work, and simple enough to present as a fully checkable proof.
