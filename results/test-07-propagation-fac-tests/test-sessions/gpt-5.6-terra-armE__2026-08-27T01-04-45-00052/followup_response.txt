I picked LPO because the rules have the characteristic shape that LPO handles directly:

1. **The first rule is a projection/subterm rule**
   \[
   F(x,y,Z)\to x.
   \]
   Its right-hand side is literally an argument of the left-hand side, so any simplification ordering with the subterm property orients it immediately.

2. **The second rule has recursive descent under a lower-precedence symbol**
   \[
   F(x,y,S(n))\to G(y,F(x,y,n)).
   \]
   The recursive \(F\)-call changes its third argument from \(S(n)\) to \(n\), a strict subterm decrease:
   \[
   S(n)>n.
   \]
   But that smaller recursive call is placed beneath an outer \(G\). LPO is designed for exactly this sort of nested recursive structure: assign
   \[
   F>G,
   \]
   then show the left side dominates both arguments of the \(G\)-term.

3. **It requires no semantic interpretation of \(Z\) or \(S\).**  
   I did not need to assume that \(Z\) is a natural-number zero, that \(S\) is successor, or that terms represent finite numerals. They are merely first-order function symbols. The proof uses only syntax: proper-subterm descent and the precedence \(F>G\).

4. **It covers all terms, including non-ground and unusual terms.**  
   A proof based informally on “the third argument gets smaller” can accidentally rely on an unstated natural-number interpretation. LPO instead proves termination uniformly for arbitrary terms substituted for \(x\), \(y\), and \(n\), such as terms containing \(F\), \(G\), \(S\), or other allowed syntax.

A direct syntactic argument could also be constructed, but it would effectively reproduce the same reasoning: recursive \(F\)-calls have a strictly smaller third argument, and the only rule that creates a \(G\) does so around such a smaller call. LPO packages that argument into a standard well-founded reduction ordering and verifies both rules without adding assumptions.
