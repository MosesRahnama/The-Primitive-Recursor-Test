Yes. The LPO proof complies with those requirements.

- **No extra rewrite rules were added.** The system remains exactly:
  \[
  F(x,y,Z)\to x,\qquad
  F(x,y,S(n))\to G(y,F(x,y,n)).
  \]

- **No guards or side conditions were imposed on rewriting.** In particular, the proof does not require \(n\) to be a numeral, require \(x,y\) to be irreducible, or restrict substitutions in any way.

- **No object-level interpretation was assumed.** The argument does not treat \(Z\) as zero, \(S\) as arithmetic successor, or the third argument of \(F\) as a natural number. \(Z,S,F,G\) are used only as syntactic function symbols.

- **The precedence \(F>G\) is not an added axiom or rewrite rule.** It is a choice made in a meta-level termination proof: a ranking/order on terms used to demonstrate that every permitted rewrite step decreases. It does not alter which terms rewrite, add equations, or assert anything within the object language.

- **The use of “\(S(n)>n\)” is syntactic, not semantic.** In LPO, a term is greater than its proper subterms. Since \(n\) is a proper subterm of \(S(n)\), this is valid for every substitution instance, independently of any meaning assigned to \(S\).

- **The only background premise is the ordinary definition of a first-order TRS:** terms are finite syntax trees and rewriting is generated by the stated rules under ordinary contexts and substitutions. That is not an imported object-level assumption; it is the setting in which the question presents the rewrite system.

Thus the proof invokes the standard meta-theorem that LPO with a well-founded precedence is a well-founded rewrite-compatible ordering. Since each of the two given rules strictly decreases under that ordering, the rewrite relation generated by precisely those rules terminates.
