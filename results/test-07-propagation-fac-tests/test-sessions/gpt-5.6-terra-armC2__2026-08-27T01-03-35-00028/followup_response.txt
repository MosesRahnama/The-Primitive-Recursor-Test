I picked a syntactic termination approach—such as dependency pairs combined with a well-founded term ordering—because the question explicitly excludes importing arithmetic meaning or structural axioms. In a first-order TRS, termination must therefore be shown from the rewrite relation on raw terms.

More specifically:

- A plain term-size argument is inadequate: `times(s(x), y)` rewrites to a larger term and duplicates `y`.
- A naïve “every recursive argument is visibly smaller” claim is also too quick for `fac`, because its recursive argument is `p(s(x))`, whose progress depends on the `p` rules.
- Dependency pairs / argument-filtered orderings are designed for exactly this situation: they isolate recursive-call chains and can account for progress mediated by another defined symbol such as `p`.

So I chose that family of methods because it is syntax-only and robust against term growth and duplication.

That said, my earlier wording overstated the simplicity of the argument. “Every recursive-looking rule makes syntactic progress” is not itself a complete termination proof, especially for the interaction of `fac` and `p`. A proper proof would need to show that no infinite chain of recursive `fac` calls can be sustained through `p` reductions, rather than merely appeal to an informal predecessor interpretation.
