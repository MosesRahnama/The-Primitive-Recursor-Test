I chose the Dependency Pair / SCC-decomposition method rather than trying a single reduction order (RPO, KBO, or one global polynomial/matrix interpretation) for a concrete structural reason specific to this system, not as a default choice.

**1. The system is *not* simply terminating.**
A direct simplification order requires every rule to satisfy `lhs ▷ rhs` under a monotonic, subterm-compatible order. Look at rule 8 together with 6–7:

```
p(s(s(x))) -> s(p(s(x)))
p(s(0))    -> 0
fac(s(x))  -> times(fac(p(s(x))), s(x))
```

In `fac(s(x)) -> times(fac(p(s(x))), s(x))`, the recursive argument `p(s(x))` is **syntactically bigger** than `x` (it has an extra `p` and `s` wrapped around it) — it only becomes "smaller" *after* several steps of rule 6 unfold and cancel the `p/s` nesting. Any order satisfying the subterm property (which all simplification orders must, by definition of the method) cannot certify that `fac(p(s(x)))` is smaller than `fac(s(x))`, because `p(s(x))` is not a subterm of `s(x)`. This is the textbook Arts–Giesl example demonstrating that simplification orders provably cannot handle this system — so picking RPO/KBO first would have been a proof attempt doomed by a known theoretical obstruction, not just a practical inconvenience.

**2. Why DP is the right escape route.**
The DP method exists precisely to handle "virtual" recursive calls like this. It replaces the requirement "orient every rule strictly" with two weaker, separable requirements:
- orient the *usable rules* only weakly (`≥`), and
- orient only the *dependency pairs lying on a cycle* strictly.

This matters here because rule 6 (`p(s(s(x))) -> s(p(s(x)))`) is exactly the kind of rule that a global order would need to orient strictly on its own, but inside the `FAC` cycle it only needs to be usable and weakly decreasing — its own strict decrease is discharged separately in the `P`-cycle. Splitting the problem this way is what makes the `fac/p` interaction tractable at all: I need one interpretation for the `P` cycle (`[p](n)=n`, ordinary successor arithmetic) and a *different* interpretation for the `FAC` cycle (`[p](n)=max(n-1,0)`, truncated predecessor) — these are two incompatible readings of `p` for the purposes of the argument, and no single global order could use both at once. DP’s SCC decomposition is precisely the tool that allows different (possibly incompatible) reduction pairs per component.

**3. Why SCC decomposition specifically, not just "DP pairs with one order."**
Even within DP, if one tries to find a single reduction pair orienting *all* dependency pairs at once, one runs into the same rigidity problem (the `PLUS`, `TIMES`, `P`, `FAC` pairs pull in different directions on the interpretation of `plus`, `times`, `p`). Decomposing into the dependency graph’s SCCs (`{DP1}`, `{DP3}`, `{DP4}`, `{DP6}`) and treating each with its own minimal interpretation is standard and sound (the DP framework guarantees soundness of this modular treatment), and it is the natural way to keep each sub-proof small and syntactically justified purely from the rules — no side conditions or external axioms are needed, only algebra over ℕ done four small times instead of one big (impossible) time.

**4. Staying within the stated boundary.**
Every ingredient used — dependency pairs derived mechanically from the rules, the dependency graph, usable rules, and polynomial interpretations over ℕ — is derivable from the rewrite rules themselves; nothing was added as an extra rule, guard, or assumption about `p` behaving like real predecessor (indeed I used two different, purely formal, monotone algebra structures for `p`, exactly as permitted by treating each SCC independently). This is why DP + SCC decomposition was the necessary, not merely convenient, method here.
