I picked **lexicographic path ordering (LPO)** because the rules have exactly the kind of recursive shape LPO is designed to handle:

- `times` calls itself on a structurally smaller second argument:
  \[
  \mathsf{times}(x,\mathsf{s}(y))
  \to
  \mathsf{plus}(\mathsf{times}(x,y),x).
  \]

- `plus` similarly calls itself on structurally smaller arguments:
  \[
  \mathsf{plus}(x,\mathsf{s}(y))
  \to
  \mathsf{s}(\mathsf{plus}(x,y)),
  \]
  and
  \[
  \mathsf{plus}(\mathsf{s}(x),y)
  \to
  \mathsf{s}(\mathsf{plus}(x,y)).
  \]

The apparent complication is that the recursive calls can appear under other symbols, especially `s`, and that multiplication rewrites to an expression headed by `plus`. A naïve “term size decreases” argument does not work uniformly: for example,

\[
\mathsf{plus}(x,\mathsf{s}(y))
\to
\mathsf{s}(\mathsf{plus}(x,y))
\]

does not necessarily reduce ordinary symbol count; both sides have the same basic amount of syntax.

LPO handles this directly by combining two syntactic facts:

1. Recursive arguments decrease, e.g. \(\mathsf{s}(y)>y\).
2. The defined symbols have a precedence, here
   \[
   \mathsf{times}>\mathsf{plus}>\mathsf{s}>0.
   \]

Thus it can recognize that a `plus` term decreases even when the recursive `plus` call is wrapped in `s`, and that a `times` term decreases when rewritten to a `plus` term containing a smaller `times` call.

I chose it over a semantic interpretation into natural numbers because LPO is purely syntactic and therefore especially well aligned with the restriction in the question: it does not rely on interpreting `0`, `s`, `plus`, or `times` as actual arithmetic operations, nor on any object-level arithmetic properties. It uses only the displayed term syntax, the rewrite rules, and a standard well-founded ordering on terms.
