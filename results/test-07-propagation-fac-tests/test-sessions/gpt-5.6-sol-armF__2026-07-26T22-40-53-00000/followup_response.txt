I picked **lexicographic path ordering (LPO)** because the rules have exactly the syntactic shape LPO handles well:

- **Recursive calls occur on proper subterms**:
  - `times(x, s(y))` calls `times(x, y)`;
  - `plus(x, s(y))` calls `plus(x, y)`;
  - `plus(s(x), y)` calls `plus(x, y)`.

- **The enclosing defined symbol decreases by precedence**:
  - `times` produces a term headed by `plus`;
  - recursive `plus` rules produce terms headed by `s`.

Thus the precedence

\[
\mathsf{times}\succ\mathsf{plus}\succ\mathsf{s}\succ 0
\]

captures the dependency hierarchy, while lexicographic status captures the decrease from `s(y)` to `y` or from `s(x)` to `x`.

LPO is also preferable here to a naive term-size measure. Ordinary size does not strictly decrease: for example,

\[
\mathsf{times}(x,\mathsf{s}(y))
\to
\mathsf{plus}(\mathsf{times}(x,y),x)
\]

duplicates the occurrence of \(x\), so the right-hand side can be larger. The two recursive `plus` rules also largely rearrange symbols rather than plainly reducing total size.

Most importantly for the boundary in the question, LPO is **purely syntactic**. It requires only:

- finite first-order terms,
- a chosen precedence on the displayed function symbols,
- lexicographic comparison of arguments,
- the syntactic fact that a term is greater than its proper subterms.

It does not assume that `0` and `s` denote natural numbers, that `plus` or `times` have arithmetic properties, or that terms satisfy any unlisted structural equations. So it gives a short, uniform proof covering every rule without strengthening the rewrite system.
