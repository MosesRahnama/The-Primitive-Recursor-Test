I picked a **polynomial interpretation approach (with dependency pairs as a fallback justification)** for a few concrete reasons tied to the specific structure of this system:

1. **The obstacle was clear and specific.** Most rules here (`plus`, `times`, `p`) are simple structural recursions where the recursive call is on an actual subterm — those are trivially handled by something like RPO or a simplification ordering with the subterm property. The one rule that breaks this pattern is
   ```
   fac(s(x)) -> times(fac(p(s(x))), s(x))
   ```
   Here `p(s(x))` is **not** a subterm of `s(x))`; it's a term that only *reduces* to something smaller. Since RPO/LPO/KBO rely on syntactic subterm comparisons (or precedence + syntactic structure), they cannot directly certify that `fac(p(s(x)))` is "smaller" than `fac(s(x))` — the required decrease is semantic, not syntactic. So I ruled out a pure simplification-order argument as insufficient on its own.

2. **Polynomial/semantic interpretations handle non-syntactic decrease.** The natural fix is to assign numerical meanings to terms such that rewriting strictly decreases the value, and then check that the recursive argument `p(s(x))` really does get a smaller value than `s(x)` under that interpretation — even though it's not a subterm. This is a standard, well-established technique precisely for cases like predecessor-based recursion, so it was the natural tool to reach for.

3. **It stays "self-contained."** The interpretation is built purely from the rules' shapes (assigning weights to symbols and checking monotonicity/decrease directly from the rewrite equations) — it doesn't presuppose external mathematical facts about ℕ, subtraction, or induction principles beyond what's needed to verify a well-founded numeric ordering, which is finitary and checkable rule-by-rule.

4. **Dependency pairs as a cross-check.** I mentioned DP/usable-rules as a secondary framing because it's the standard automation-friendly method (used by AProVE/TTT2) for exactly this kind of "recursive call hidden behind an auxiliary reduction" pattern — it decomposes the problem into simpler subproblems (usable rules for `p`, `plus`, `times`) and then only needs the decrease on the `fac` dependency pair, which lines up with the polynomial argument.

So the choice wasn't arbitrary — it followed directly from diagnosing *why* naive syntactic orderings fail on this system, and picking the minimal technique known to handle that specific failure mode (recursion through an auxiliary reducible term rather than direct subterm recursion).
