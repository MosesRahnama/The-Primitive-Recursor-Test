I chose the **Lexicographic Path Ordering (LPO)** because the specific structural features of this rewrite system immediately disqualify the other two most common termination proof methods: **Knuth-Bendix Ordering (KBO)** and standard **Polynomial Interpretations**. 

Here is the technical breakdown of why LPO was the necessary and most natural choice:

### 1. KBO is disqualified by Variable Duplication (Rule 5)
The Knuth-Bendix Ordering has a strict, fundamental requirement: **no variable may appear more times on the right-hand side (RHS) of a rule than on the left-hand side (LHS).** 

Look at Rule 5:
`times(s(x), y) -> plus(times(x, y), y)`

* On the LHS, the variable `y` appears **1** time.
* On the RHS, the variable `y` appears **2** times (once inside `times`, once as the second argument to `plus`).

Because `y` is duplicated, KBO is mathematically incapable of orienting this rule. Any method based on KBO fails immediately. LPO, however, does not have this variable-occurrence restriction. It handles variable duplication effortlessly as long as the root symbol of the LHS (`times`) is strictly greater in the precedence than the root symbol of the RHS (`plus`), which we established.

### 2. Polynomial Interpretations are disqualified by "Symbol Jumping" (Rules 2 and 6)
Standard polynomial interpretations over the natural numbers assign a polynomial to each function symbol and require the LHS to evaluate to a *strictly greater* integer than the RHS for all inputs. This method struggles heavily when a function symbol "jumps" from the inside of an expression to the outside.

Look at Rule 2:
`plus(x, s(y)) -> s(plus(x, y))`

If we use the standard, most natural polynomial interpretations for addition and successor:
* $s(y) = y + 1$
* $plus(x, y) = x + y$

Evaluating both sides yields:
* **LHS:** $plus(x, s(y)) = x + (y + 1) = x + y + 1$
* **RHS:** $s(plus(x, y)) = (x + y) + 1 = x + y + 1$

The LHS and RHS evaluate to the **exact same value**. Because polynomial interpretations require *strict* inequality ($LHS > RHS$), this standard interpretation fails. (You could try to invent non-standard, non-linear polynomials to force a strict decrease, but this quickly becomes algebraically complex and brittle).

The exact same "symbol jumping" problem occurs in Rule 6 (`p(s(s(x))) -> s(p(s(x)))`), where `s` moves outside of `p`. 

LPO does not rely on numerical evaluation. Instead, it relies on structural precedence. Because `plus > s` and `p > s` in our precedence, LPO easily recognizes that moving `s` to the outside is a valid terminating step without needing to calculate numerical values.

### 3. LPO handles the "Precedence + Lexicographic" combination perfectly
Because KBO and Polynomials fail, we must use a Path Ordering (like LPO or RPO). LPO was chosen over more complex variants (like RPO with multiset status) because the rules can be oriented using a simple **total precedence** and standard **left-to-right lexicographic comparison** of arguments.

* **Rule 5** (`times > plus`) handles the duplication of `y`.
* **Rules 2 and 6** (`plus > s` and `p > s`) handle the outward jumping of `s`.
* **Rule 6's nested structure** (`p(s(s(x))) -> s(p(s(x)))`) is perfectly resolved by LPO's recursive lexicographic comparison of the arguments of `p`, specifically comparing `s(s(x))` to `s(x)`.

### Summary
I picked LPO because it is the "textbook" solution for a system that combines **variable duplication** (which kills KBO) and **structural reorganization/symbol jumping** (which kills simple Polynomial Interpretations). It allowed for a clean, single-precedence proof without needing to import complex status tuples, matrix interpretations, or dependency pair transformations.
