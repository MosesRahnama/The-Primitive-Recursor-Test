I chose the **Multiset Path Ordering (MPO)** for several specific reasons that perfectly align with the strict constraints of your prompt, as well as the structural nature of the rewrite rules provided. 

Here is a breakdown of why MPO was the best tool for the job:

### 1. Strict Adherence to the "Purely Syntactic" Constraint
Your prompt emphasized using rules alone, with no external memory, no imported object-level axioms, and no hidden assumptions. 
* Other standard termination methods, such as **Polynomial Interpretations** or **Matrix Interpretations**, require mapping the function symbols to mathematical domains (like the natural numbers) and relying on the external arithmetic properties of those domains to prove termination. While mathematically sound, this borders on importing external semantics.
* **MPO is purely syntactic.** It does not compute values or map terms to numbers; it only looks at the tree structure of the terms (the syntax) and a simple ranking of the symbols (the precedence). The termination guarantee comes from Kruskal's Tree Theorem—a meta-theorem about syntactic trees, requiring zero additions to the object-level system.

### 2. Perfect Fit for Structural Recursion
The rules you provided for `plus`, `times`, and `p` are classic examples of primitive recursion. In primitive recursion, a function is defined by peeling away a constructor (in this case, `s`) from one of its arguments.
* `plus(x, s(y))` peels an `s` off the second argument.
* `times(s(x), y)` peels an `s` off the first argument.
* `p(s(s(x)))` peels an `s` off the inner term.

Path orderings (like MPO) were explicitly invented by Nachum Dershowitz in the late 1970s exactly to handle this kind of structural reduction. MPO natively understands the **subterm property** (e.g., that `s(x)` is strictly larger than `x`). 

### 3. Why Multiset instead of Lexicographic?
I specifically chose **Multiset** Path Ordering (MPO) over its sister method, **Lexicographic** Path Ordering (LPO), for maximum simplicity:
* In this TRS, `plus` recurses on its *second* argument, while `times` recurses on its *first* argument. 
* If I had used LPO, I would have had to explicitly define and justify a directional status (e.g., "evaluate left-to-right") for every function symbol to ensure the decreasing argument was caught properly.
* **Multisets ignore order.** By treating the arguments as an unordered bag (a multiset), MPO simply asks: *"Did at least one argument get smaller while the others stayed the same or got smaller?"* Comparing the multiset `{x, s(y)}` against `{x, y}` cleanly registers as a decrease without requiring me to specify any left-to-right evaluation rules. It kept the proof minimal and completely free of extra side conditions.

### Summary
I picked MPO because it provides a rigorous, textbook proof of termination using nothing but the literal shapes of the terms you provided, avoiding any mathematical domain mapping, and seamlessly handling recursion on different argument positions with minimal setup.
