I picked LPO because the rules have the characteristic shape that LPO handles directly:

- Recursive calls are made on a structurally smaller argument:
  - `plus(x, s(y)) -> ... plus(x, y)`: `s(y) > y`.
  - `times(s(x), y) -> ... times(x, y)`: `s(x) > x`.
  - `p(s(s(x))) -> ... p(s(x))`: `s(s(x)) > s(x)`.

- The potentially troublesome rules wrap those smaller recursive calls in another symbol:
  - `plus(...)` is placed under `s`.
  - `times(...)` is placed under `plus`.
  - `p(...)` is placed under `s`.

LPO is designed for exactly this situation: choose a precedence in which the caller symbol dominates the wrapper symbol:

\[
\mathit{times} \succ \mathit{plus} \succ p \succ s \succ 0.
\]

Then a term rooted by the larger symbol can be greater than a term rooted by the smaller wrapper symbol, provided it also dominates that wrapper’s arguments.

For example, in

\[
\mathit{times}(s(x),y)
\to
\mathit{plus}(\mathit{times}(x,y),y),
\]

a simple “term size decreases” argument does not work: the right-hand side is syntactically larger than the left-hand side. Likewise, an ordinary polynomial interpretation based only on natural-number sizes may require some care because `times` expands into both a recursive `times` call and an additional `y`.

LPO avoids counting symbols or appealing to the intended arithmetic semantics. It uses only:

1. the finite syntax of the terms;
2. the fact that a term is greater than its proper subterms;
3. the explicitly selected symbol precedence; and
4. lexicographic comparison of arguments at recursive calls.

Thus it is a natural fit for the requested boundary: it proves termination from the rewrite rules as pure syntax, without assuming that the symbols denote natural-number addition, multiplication, predecessor, or anything else.
