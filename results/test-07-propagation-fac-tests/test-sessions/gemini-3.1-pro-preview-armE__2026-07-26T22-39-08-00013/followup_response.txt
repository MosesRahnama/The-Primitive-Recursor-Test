I chose the **Lexicographic Path Order (LPO)** for this specific system because it is the most direct, elegant, and strictly compliant method given the severe constraints you placed in your prompt. 

Here is a breakdown of exactly why LPO was the best tool for the job:

**1. Strict Compliance with Your Constraints (Purely Syntactic)**
You explicitly forbade "imported object-level axioms," "hidden assumptions," and relying on external domains. 
* Many termination proofs use **Polynomial Interpretations** (mapping terms to natural numbers, e.g., $F(x,y,n) = x + y + n + 1$). While standard, this method requires importing the external domain of natural numbers ($\mathbb{N}$) and its arithmetic axioms (addition, multiplication, greater-than). 
* **LPO**, by contrast, operates entirely within the *free term algebra* provided by the syntax itself. It does not map terms to numbers; it relies solely on the topological structure of the trees (subterms) and a simple symbol ranking (precedence). It relies on zero external axioms.

**2. It Naturally Captures Primitive Recursion**
Rule 2 (`F(x, y, S(n)) -> G(y, F(x, y, n))`) is a classic primitive recursive schema. The recursion happens because `S(n)` is reduced to its immediate subterm `n`, while the other arguments (`x` and `y`) are passed along unchanged. LPO was historically designed specifically to handle this exact pattern. By comparing arguments left-to-right (or right-to-left), LPO immediately recognizes that the first two arguments are unchanged and the third has structurally shrunk.

**3. It Effortlessly Handles "Wrapped" Recursive Calls**
In Rule 2, the recursive call `F(x, y, n)` is not at the root of the right-hand side; it is nested inside the function `G(...)`. 
* Simple subterm reduction methods fail here because the RHS is not a direct subterm of the LHS. 
* LPO handles this gracefully through **precedence**. By simply declaring `F > G`, LPO allows us to "peel off" the `G` and compare the LHS directly with `G`'s internal arguments. This turns a potentially complex structural proof into a trivial one-step verification.

**4. It Avoids "Overkill" Methods**
I could have used the **Dependency Pair (DP)** framework, which is the modern standard for automated termination provers. However, the DP method requires generating a secondary TRS (the dependency graph), identifying cycles, and proving there are no infinite chains. For a system with only two rules and a clear structural decrease, using Dependency Pairs would be like using a sledgehammer to crack a nut. LPO provides a direct, one-shot proof.

In short, LPO was chosen because it perfectly aligns with the structural "shape" of your rules (primitive recursion wrapped in a secondary function) while remaining entirely within the airtight syntactic sandbox your prompt demanded.
