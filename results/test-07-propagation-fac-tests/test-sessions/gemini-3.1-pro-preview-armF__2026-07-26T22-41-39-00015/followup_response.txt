I chose the **Lexicographic Path Ordering (LPO)** because it is the most direct, elegant, and purely syntactic method for proving termination of primitive recursive systems, and it perfectly aligns with the strict constraints of your prompt. 

Here is a detailed breakdown of why LPO was the ideal tool for this specific system:

**1. It perfectly respects the "no external assumptions" constraint**
Your prompt explicitly forbade importing object-level assumptions, structural axioms, extra rules, or external memory. 
* Many other termination methods, like **Polynomial Interpretations** or **Matrix Interpretations**, require mapping the syntactic terms into an external mathematical domain (like the natural numbers) and relying on the well-foundedness of that external domain's arithmetic. 
* LPO, by contrast, operates *entirely on the syntax*. It does not care what `plus` or `times` actually "mean" mathematically. It only requires assigning a simple, internal precedence to the symbols (`times > plus > s > 0`) and relies on the fundamental structure of trees (terms).

**2. It handles "Symbol Demotion" effortlessly**
Look at the recursive rule for multiplication: 
`times(x, s(y)) -> plus(times(x, y), x)`
A complex symbol (`times`) is generating a new symbol (`plus`) at the root of the right-hand side. Because LPO allows us to declare `times > plus` in our precedence, it automatically permits a term rooted in `times` to rewrite to a term rooted in `plus`, provided the subterms of the `plus` don't get "larger" than the original `times` term. LPO was explicitly designed to handle this exact kind of structural shifting.

**3. It captures Primitive Recursion naturally**
The rules provided are a classic example of primitive recursion: they define operations by continually stripping away a constructor, `s`, from the arguments.
* `times(x, s(y)) -> ... times(x, y) ...`
* `plus(x, s(y)) -> ... plus(x, y) ...`
* `plus(s(x), y) -> ... plus(x, y) ...`
LPO inherently respects the **subterm property** (the rule that `s(y) > y`). When combined with lexicographic comparison, LPO recognizes immediately that pulling an `s` off an argument creates a strictly smaller term, regardless of how many other terms are being carried along.

**4. Why not other syntactic orderings?**
* **Knuth-Bendix Ordering (KBO):** KBO requires assigning both a precedence to symbols *and* a specific numerical weight to each symbol, while strictly tracking variable occurrences. While KBO could work here, finding and proving the correct weight distribution is extra algebraic bookkeeping. LPO is purely ordinal and requires no weight balancing.
* **Multiset Path Ordering (MPO):** MPO compares arguments as unordered bags (multisets) rather than ordered lists. MPO would actually also work for this system! However, because standard mathematical functions like `plus` and `times` are written with positional arguments (left-to-right), checking them lexicographically (left-to-right) is more intuitive to read and verify step-by-step than calculating multiset differences.

In short, LPO was chosen because it is the "path of least resistance" for this specific class of rewrite rules. It requires the least amount of setup (just a 4-symbol precedence hierarchy), involves no external math, and resolves every rule with a simple, mechanical syntactic check.
