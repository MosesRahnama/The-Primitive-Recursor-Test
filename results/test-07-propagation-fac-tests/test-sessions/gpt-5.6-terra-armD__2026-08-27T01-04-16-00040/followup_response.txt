I picked a polynomial interpretation because the rules have a clear recursive “size decrease” pattern, but that decrease is not captured by ordinary syntactic term size.

For example, in

\[
times(s(x),y) \to plus(times(x,y),y),
\]

the right-hand side is syntactically larger: it introduces both `plus` and a second occurrence of \(y\). A naïve node-count measure may increase rather than decrease. So a simple “each rule removes a constructor” argument would not work uniformly.

A polynomial interpretation lets us assign weighted numerical costs so that:

- the recursive first argument of `times` is expensive enough that replacing \(s(x)\) by \(x\) compensates for the added `plus` and duplicated \(y\);
- the second argument of `plus` receives sufficient weight to make
  \[
  plus(x,s(y)) \to s(plus(x,y))
  \]
  decrease;
- `p` is assigned enough weight that
  \[
  p(s(s(x))) \to s(p(s(x)))
  \]
  decreases despite moving the outer \(p\) inward.

The particular interpretation was chosen to make each difficult rule decrease by exactly \(1\):

\[
[s](n)=n+1,\qquad [plus](a,b)=a+2b+1,
\]

\[
[times](a,b)=2ab+2a+b+1,\qquad [p](a)=2a.
\]

The coefficients are not uniquely determined. They are a convenient solution to the inequalities imposed by the rules. In particular:

- `plus` needs its second argument weighted more heavily than the added outer `s`;
- `times` needs a mixed \(ab\) term because its recursive rule combines the decremented first argument with \(y\) and then adds another \(y\);
- `p` needs to count its argument with coefficient at least \(2\), because its rule effectively trades one `s` of argument depth for one outer `s` on the result.

Polynomial interpretations are also especially suitable here because they are entirely internal to the presented first-order syntax and rules: they introduce no rewrite rules, equations, induction principles, semantic assumptions about arithmetic operations represented by the symbols, or side conditions on terms.
