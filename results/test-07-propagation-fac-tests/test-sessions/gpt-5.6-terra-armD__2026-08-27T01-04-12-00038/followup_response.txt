I picked a **polynomial interpretation into the natural numbers** because it fits this rewrite system directly and proves termination without relying on the intended arithmetic meaning of `plus`, `times`, `s`, or `0`.

The key requirement is to find a well-founded measure that strictly decreases for every rewrite rule, including rewrites occurring inside larger terms. Natural numbers with \(>\) are well-founded, and polynomial interpretations give a standard way to assign each term such a number.

This system has two features making that method especially convenient:

1. **The right-hand sides can grow syntactically.**  
   For example,

   \[
   times(s(x),y)\to plus(times(x,y),y)
   \]

   replaces one `times` occurrence by a `plus` containing another `times` and an additional copy of \(y\). So simple measures such as total symbol count, term depth, or number of defined-function symbols do not obviously decrease—and some increase.

2. **The recursive argument structurally decreases, but other material is duplicated or rearranged.**  
   In the `times` rule, the first argument changes from \(s(x)\) to \(x\), which is a decrease. But the right-hand side also introduces `plus` and repeats \(y\). A weighted numeric interpretation can make the decrease contributed by removing the outer `s` outweigh that added structure.

The chosen interpretation was designed around precisely those inequalities:

- `plus(x,s(y)) -> s(plus(x,y))` requires `plus` to weight its second argument enough that reducing \(s(y)\) to \(y\) pays for the newly introduced outer `s`.
- `times(s(x),y) -> plus(times(x,y),y)` requires `times` to assign enough extra weight to its first argument that replacing \(s(x)\) by \(x\) pays for the enclosing `plus` and duplicated \(y\).
- `p(s(s(x))) -> s(p(s(x)))` requires `p` to grow sufficiently faster than `s`, so that one removed `s` produces a strict net decrease despite the new outer `s`.

A polynomial interpretation also has the useful compositional property that, because every function interpretation is strictly monotone, a decrease at the redex remains a decrease when that redex occurs inside any surrounding context. Thus it proves termination of the full first-order rewrite relation, not merely root rewriting.

Other methods could likely be used—for example, recursive path ordering with a suitable precedence and status choices, or dependency-pair analysis—but the polynomial interpretation gives a short, explicit, rule-by-rule proof entirely within the stated syntax and rules.
