I picked a dependency-pair argument with numeric interpretations because the system has a nested recursive-call pattern that makes a direct “every rule decreases one obvious size measure” proof awkward.

In particular, the difficult rule is:

```text
fac(s(x)) -> times(fac(p(s(x))), s(x))
```

Syntactically, its right-hand side is larger: it introduces `times`, retains `s(x)`, and contains the recursive `fac` call. A simple term-size measure therefore fails immediately.

Also, `fac` does not recur directly on the literal subterm `x`; it recurs on:

```text
p(s(x))
```

To prove that this is smaller from the rules alone, one cannot appeal informally to arithmetic facts such as “predecessor of successor is the original number” unless those facts are represented by the rewrite behavior. The interpretation handles this explicitly:

```text
[p(t)] = max([t] - 1, 0)
[s(t)] = [t] + 1.
```

Hence:

```text
[p(s(x))] = [x].
```

That is not an imported object-level arithmetic axiom; it is a chosen interpretation used to orient the displayed rewrite rules.

Dependency pairs are useful because they separate two distinct obligations:

1. **Ordinary rewriting may build surrounding structure.**  
   For example, the `fac` rule builds a `times(...)` term. The interpretation shows that, globally, this rewrite still decreases.

2. **Recursive calls must not form an infinite call cycle.**  
   The dependency pairs isolate exactly the possible calls:
   - `plus` calls `plus` with a shorter successor argument;
   - `times` calls `times` with a shorter first successor argument, and can call `plus`;
   - `p` calls itself with one fewer `s`;
   - `fac` calls itself on `p(s(x))`, and can call `times`.

The marked-symbol interpretation then gives a strict decrease along every such possible recursive-call step.

A more elementary alternative would be a carefully layered induction based on the shapes reachable by `p`, `plus`, `times`, and `fac`. But that proof would still effectively need to establish the same facts: that `p(s(t))` reduces in a terminating way and behaves as a predecessor on successor-shaped inputs, that `times` terminates by decreasing its first argument, and that `fac` invokes itself on a smaller argument. Dependency pairs package those interdependent recursive-call arguments in a standard, syntax-only termination method.
