**Why I picked this method:**

I chose a combination of **lexicographic path order (LPO)** for the bulk of the proof, supplemented by the **dependency pair method** for the `fac`/`p` interaction, for several specific reasons:

1. **Syntactic, not semantic**: The system has no variable binding, no conditional, and no imported algebra. That rules out semantic/interpretation methods (polynomial interpretations over ℕ, matrix interpretations, etc.) as the *primary* tool — they would require choosing a concrete interpretation, which implicitly smuggles in Peano arithmetic. LPO and dependency pairs work purely on the term structure of the rules themselves.

2. **The system is "almost obviously" terminating**: The rules are textbook mutually recursive definitions. A powerful, fully automatic method like LPO will dispatch `plus`, `times`, and the non-recursive cases of `p` immediately with a reasonable precedence. There's no need to invent a complex measure.

3. **The only real subtlety is `fac` vs. `p`**: The rule `fac(s(x)) → times(fac(p(s(x))), s(x))` is the one place where the recursive call isn't on a structural subterm of the left-hand side. A naïve structural ordering fails here. I needed a principled way to say "yes, `p(s(x))` is smaller than `s(x)`."

4. **Why the dependency pair method specifically for that case**:
   - It isolates the *one* non-trivial pair: `fac(s(x)) → fac(p(s(x)))`.
   - It makes explicit that the recursion via `fac` is only problematic because of the `p` call.
   - The proof obligation reduces to showing `p(s(x))` is "smaller" than `s(x)` in some well-founded order — and that fact is discharged by the `p` rules themselves, which are decreasing in the LPO/subterm order.
   - This keeps the argument local: I don't need a global measure that simultaneously handles all functions; I just need a chain of decreasing arguments along the dependency pairs.

5. **Alternatives I considered and rejected for this system**:
   - **Polynomial interpretation**: Works easily, but requires fixing `0 ↦ 0`, `s ↦ n+1`, etc. — that fixes a model, which is a stronger commitment than the question allows. The question asks whether termination is derivable *from the rules alone*, not whether some natural interpretation works.
   - **Semantic labelling / quasi-models**: Overkill for a system this simple.
   - **Recursive path order (RPO) alone**: Would work, but I find LPO slightly cleaner to state and the dependency pair framing makes the `fac`/`p` point crisper.

6. **Conservative framing**: I deliberately phrased the `p` argument as "derivable from the rules themselves" (via an induction on the structure of derivations, not on natural numbers). This is the key point that makes the answer a *yes* — termination is provable without importing Peano arithmetic or any external semantics.

**In short:** I picked LPO + dependency pairs because they (a) are purely syntactic, (b) are the standard methods for exactly this shape of mutually recursive definition, and (c) let me separate the trivial 90% of the system from the one place (`fac`/`p`) where the real work is, and discharge that one place using the `p` rules alone.
