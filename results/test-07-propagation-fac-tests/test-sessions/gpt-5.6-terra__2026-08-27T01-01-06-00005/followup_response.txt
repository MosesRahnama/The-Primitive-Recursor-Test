I picked a **modular termination argument** because the rules naturally separate into:

1. a base computational subsystem for `plus`, `times`, and `p`, and  
2. one recursive rule for `fac`.

That division makes the proof transparent and stays within the presented TRS.

For the base subsystem, an ordering such as LPO/RPO is appropriate because its recursion is structurally decreasing:

- `plus` recurses on the second argument beneath `s`;
- `times(s(x), y)` recurses on the strictly smaller first argument `x`;
- `p(s(s(x)))` recurses on the smaller successor chain `s(x)`.

A recursive path ordering can orient all of those rules simultaneously using a precedence such as

```text
times > plus > p > s > 0.
```

That is a standard purely syntactic termination proof: it does not need to interpret `0`, `s`, `plus`, etc. as natural-number operations.

The `fac` rule is different:

```text
fac(s(x)) -> times(fac(p(s(x))), s(x)).
```

A single simple RPO precedence is not the most natural way to show this rule decreases, because the recursive call is not literally on a syntactic subterm of `s(x)`: it is on `p(s(x))`. The decrease comes from the behavior of the displayed `p` rules. Specifically, reducing `p(s(x))` removes one outer successor from the successor-prefix structure of `s(x)`.

So I first prove the `p` behavior from its own rules, then use the resulting strictly decreasing “leading `s` count” as the recursion measure for `fac`.

I chose this approach rather than, for example, assigning an arithmetic interpretation to all symbols because:

- it directly reflects the dependency structure of the rules;
- it avoids relying on semantic assumptions about intended natural-number meaning;
- it handles the non-obvious interaction between `fac` and `p`;
- it gives a clear explanation for why the `fac` recursion cannot continue indefinitely.

In short: RPO/LPO is convenient for the plainly structural base rules, while the `fac` rule requires a relative argument based on the syntactically derivable descent of `p(s(x))`.
