I selected the **Lexicographic Path Ordering (LPO)** for several specific reasons that make it the most appropriate and robust tool for this system under the constraints you provided:

### 1. Strict Adherence to the "No External Assumptions" Constraint
Other common termination proof methods, such as **Polynomial Interpretations**, map terms to algebraic structures (like the natural numbers $\mathbb{N}$). To prove termination that way, one must appeal to external properties of arithmetic (e.g., proving that $x \cdot (y + 1) > x \cdot y + x$ for all $x, y \in \mathbb{N}$). 
Because your prompt strictly forbade importing structural axioms or object-level assumptions beyond the syntax, LPO was the ideal choice:
* LPO is a **purely syntactic ordering**. 
* It operates strictly on the tree-structure of the terms and a finite, user-defined precedence over the symbols ($\text{times} > \text{plus} > \text{s} > 0$).
* It requires no external mathematical theories, arithmetic axioms, or domain-specific assumptions.

### 2. Perfect Fit for Primitive Recursive Definitions
The term rewriting system presented is a classic definition of primitive recursive arithmetic (addition and multiplication). LPO is historically and theoretically designed precisely to handle such hierarchical, structurally decreasing systems:
* **Hierarchical Dependency:** Multiplication (`times`) is defined in terms of addition (`plus`), which is defined in terms of successor (`s`). The precedence hierarchy $\text{times} > \text{plus} > \text{s}$ perfectly mirrors this dependency.
* **Structural Descent:** In the recursive rules, the arguments strictly decrease structurally (from $\text{s}(y)$ to $y$, or from $\text{s}(x)$ to $x$). LPO’s subterm property and lexicographic status are mathematically tailored to detect this exact type of structural descent.

### 3. Handling of Nested Right-Hand Sides
Look at Rule 2:
$$\text{times}(x, \text{s}(y)) \rightarrow \text{plus}(\text{times}(x, y), x)$$
Here, the right-hand side introduces a *new* function symbol (`plus`) at the root, but buries the recursive call (`times(x, y)`) inside it. 
* A simple dummy reduction or size-change analysis can struggle when a rule wraps a recursive call inside another function symbol.
* LPO handles this effortlessly. Because $\text{times} > \text{plus}$, LPO allows us to "peel off" the $\text{plus}$ symbol and compare the main term $\text{times}(x, \text{s}(y))$ directly against the nested recursive term $\text{times}(x, y)$.

### 4. Maximizing Decidability and Readability
LPO is a well-known, decidable, and fully automated ordering. By presenting the proof via LPO, the termination argument is:
* **Constructive:** It provides a clear, step-by-step recipe that anyone (or any automated theorem prover) can verify in a finite number of syntactic steps.
* **Unambiguous:** There are no "clever" coefficients to guess (as in polynomial interpretations) or complex dependency graphs to trace—just a direct comparison of left-hand and right-hand sides.
