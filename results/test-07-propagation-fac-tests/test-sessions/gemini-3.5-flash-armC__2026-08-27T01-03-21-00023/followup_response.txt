I chose the **Dependency Pair (DP) Framework** combined with **Polynomial Interpretations** for three primary reasons:

### 1. Modularity (Dealing with Multiple Operations)
The system is not flat; it has a clear hierarchy of operations: `plus` and `times` are helper functions, `p` is a predecessor helper, and `fac` is the main function utilizing them all. 
* **Why DP?** The Dependency Pair framework allows us to decompose the TRS into its **Dependency Graph**. This separates the termination of `plus` and `times` from the termination of `fac` and `p`. Instead of finding one single, complex ordering that fits all rules simultaneously, we can solve the termination of each subsystem independently.

### 2. Syntactic Orderings Fail on Defined Functions in Arguments
Standard syntactic orderings like **LPO (Lexicographic Path Ordering)** or **MPO (Multiset Path Ordering)** struggle with rules like:
$$\text{fac}(s(x)) \rightarrow \text{times}(\text{fac}(p(s(x))), s(x))$$
* **The Problem:** In LPO, to show that the left-hand side is greater than the right-hand side, we must prove that the arguments of the recursive call are smaller. We would need to prove that $s(x) \succ_L p(s(x))$. However, because $p$ is a *defined function symbol* (not a constructor) and has its own rewrite rules, syntactic orderings cannot easily "know" that $p(s(x))$ evaluates to something smaller than $s(x)$ without complex precedence hierarchies that often fail.
* **Why Polynomial Interpretations?** Polynomials provide a **semantic** approach. By mapping terms to natural numbers, we can assign a concrete mathematical meaning to $p$ and $s$ (e.g., $[s](x) = x + 2$ and $[p](x) = x - 1$). This algebraically guarantees that $[p(s(x))] = x + 1 < x + 2 = [s(x)]$, bypassing the limitations of purely syntactic comparisons.

### 3. Intuitiveness and Ease of Verification
Polynomial interpretations translate abstract term-rewriting relations into high-school algebra. Showing that:
$$x + 1 < x + 2$$
is universally true for all $x \ge 0$ is incredibly easy to explain, understand, and verify, making the proof both rigorous and highly accessible.
