I picked LPO because it was the simplest standard technique that could do the job without smuggling in any extra machinery.

A few concrete reasons:

**1. It handles structural recursion natively.**
Most of the rules (R1, R2, R3, R4, R7) are obviously terminating because the RHS is literally a subterm of the LHS. LPO's subterm clause covers these for free, so I only had to think hard about R5, R6, and R8.

**2. It handles "same-topsymbol with shrinking multisets" cleanly.**
R2 and R5 are the standard recursive-on-an-argument patterns. LPO's multiset rule captures both: the recursive argument shrinks ($s(y) \succ y$ via subterm, $s(x) \succ x$ via subterm), so the other argument just needs to be preserved.

**3. The precedence was forced, not chosen.**
I didn't "pick" the precedence strategically — each rule essentially dictates it:
- R8 forces $\text{fac} > \text{times}$ (the LHS top must beat the RHS top).
- R5 forces $\text{times} > \text{plus}$.
- R2 forces $\text{plus} > s$.
- R6 forces $s > p$.

So really the order is *forced by the rules*, and I just verified that no cycle was created. That's the honest story — I checked whether the dependency structure of the rules admits a well-founded precedence, and it does. R7 ($p(s(0)) \to 0$) was free since $0$ is a subterm regardless of where $0$ sits in the precedence.

**4. It needed no strengthening.**
Other termination methods I considered would have required importing structure not derivable from the rules:
- A polynomial/ranking-function argument would have needed arithmetic on the natural-number interpretation of `s` and `0`, which is an external semantic assumption.
- A recursive-path-order or matrix-interpretation argument would have similarly required semantic content.
- A simple "measure decreases" argument would have needed me to postulate that `s`-chains have a length, etc.

LPO is *purely syntactic* — it's just a well-founded order on terms built from a precedence on symbols and the subterm relation. That keeps me squarely inside the constraint that I can use the rules' structure and nothing else.

**5. Honest caveat.**
I should flag: I checked the argument rule by rule, but I didn't run a prover (e.g., AProVE, μ-termination tool) to mechanically verify. So if you want a machine-checked certificate, this human proof would need to be fed into a tool for confirmation. That said, the LPO orientation is standard and the case analysis is small.
