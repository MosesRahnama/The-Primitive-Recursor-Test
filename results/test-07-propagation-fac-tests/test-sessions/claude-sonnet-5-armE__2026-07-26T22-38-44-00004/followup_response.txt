## Why polynomial interpretation

**1. The syntactic shape of the system practically demands it.**

Looking at the two rules:

```
F(x, y, Z)    -> x
F(x, y, S(n)) -> G(y, F(x, y, n))
```

- There's no obvious *subterm* relationship I could exploit with something simpler like RPO (recursive path ordering) in a fully syntactic way, because rule 2 doesn't just recurse on a subterm — it also wraps the recursive call in `G(y, ·)`, changing the head symbol from `F` to `G`. So a naive "measure the term by its structure and check RHS is a subterm-modulo-precedence" argument needs at least a precedence `F > G` decision, which is fine, but then I'd still need to check monotonicity conditions for LPO/RPO by hand for both arguments position-wise. That's *possible*, but polynomial interpretations let me sidestep worrying about precedence/status choices entirely.

- Rule 2 has a recursive call `F(x, y, n)` nested one level deeper than `S(n)`, feeding into `G`. This is the classic pattern where the recursion argument strictly decreases (`S(n)` vs `n`), while everything else is preserved or grows by a bounded additive amount from wrapping in `G`. Polynomial interpretations are exactly suited to captures this: give the "counting" argument (the `S`/`Z`-typed one) a coefficient large enough to dominate the constant added by `G`.

**2. It's the most mechanical/certifiable method for this shape of TRS.**

- The system has only two rules, both fairly small, with a single recursive argument going through a datatype (`Z`/`S`), which is the paradigmatic case where you want the interpretation to grow strictly with `S`-nesting depth (giving `[S](n) = n+1`) and where the base case (`Z`) is `0`. This is essentially the "structural recursion on Peano naturals" pattern, and polynomial/linear interpretations solve this class of problems by design.

**3. I wanted a proof method that's easy to verify by hand, inequality by inequality**, rather than invoke a heavier machine (like dependency pairs + SCC analysis, or a semantic labeling argument) that would be overkill for a 2-rule system. Polynomial interpretation gives:
- A finite, checkable set of arithmetic inequalities (one per rule, universally quantified over ℕ).
- Automatic guarantee of monotonicity (hence compatibility with any context) as long as each polynomial is strictly monotone in each argument — which is trivial to arrange here.
- A direct, elementary proof that doesn't require introducing dependency pairs, argument filterings, or extra machinery — staying strictly within "use the rules as given" without smuggling in structural assumptions.

**4. It naturally handles the "asymmetric" shape of the constructors.**

Only the third argument of `F` ever changes/recurses (`x` and `y` are carried unchanged), and `S`/`Z` are the only constructors driving recursion. So I deliberately made the interpretation of `F` heavily weighted on that third argument (`2n` term) — enough to strictly dominate the `+1` cost from wrapping the result in `G`. This reflects the actual recursive structure of the rules rather than being an arbitrary guess; once I recognized *that* is where recursion happens, the correct interpretation almost writes itself.

**In short:** given a small, first-order, non-binding system with one explicit recursion on `Z`/`S`, a linear polynomial interpretation is the natural, minimal, and easily verifiable choice — it exactly targets "one argument decreases through the recursive constructor, other symbols contribute bounded overhead," which is precisely what's happening in rule 2, and it disposes of rule 1 trivially as well.
