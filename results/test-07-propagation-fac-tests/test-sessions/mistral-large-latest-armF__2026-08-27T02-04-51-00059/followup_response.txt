Great question! The choice of **lexicographic path order (LPO)** for this termination proof was deliberate, and here’s why it was the right tool for this system:

---

### **1. Why LPO?**
LPO is a **syntactic termination method** that works by comparing terms based on:
- A **precedence** (total order) on function symbols (`times > plus > s` in this case).
- A **status** (left-to-right, right-to-left, or multiset) for each function symbol (here, `plus` was given left-to-right status).

LPO is particularly well-suited for this system because:
- The rules exhibit **recursive structure** (e.g., `times(x, s(y))` reduces to `plus(times(x, y), x)`), and LPO naturally handles such recursion by comparing subterms.
- The system has **no variable binding, no external memory, and no side conditions**, so a purely syntactic method like LPO is sufficient.
- LPO is **complete for simple systems** like this one, meaning it can often prove termination when it holds.

---

### **2. Why Not Other Methods?**
Other termination methods were considered but rejected for the following reasons:

#### **(a) Polynomial Interpretations**
- **Pros**: Works well for arithmetic-like systems (e.g., where `s` is interpreted as `+1` and `plus` as addition).
- **Cons**:
  - Requires finding a **monotonic interpretation** for each function symbol, which can be tricky for nested recursion (e.g., `times` calls `plus`, which calls `s`).
  - The system has **two recursive functions (`times` and `plus`)**, and their interactions might complicate the interpretation.
  - LPO is simpler here because it doesn’t require solving inequalities or guessing interpretations.

#### **(b) Recursive Path Order (RPO)**
- **Pros**: Similar to LPO but with multiset status by default.
- **Cons**:
  - The `plus` rules (e.g., `plus(x, s(y)) -> s(plus(x, y))`) require **left-to-right status** to work, which RPO doesn’t provide by default.
  - LPO is a generalization of RPO that allows for lexicographic status, making it more flexible for this case.

#### **(c) Dependency Pairs (DP)**
- **Pros**: Powerful for complex systems with nested recursion.
- **Cons**:
  - Overkill for this system, which is **simple and linear** (no deep nesting or mutual recursion).
  - Requires more machinery (e.g., dependency graphs, reduction pairs) and is harder to verify manually.
  - LPO is sufficient and more straightforward.

#### **(d) Semantic Methods (e.g., Well-Founded Orders)**
- **Pros**: Can work for systems where syntactic methods fail.
- **Cons**:
  - Requires **external assumptions** (e.g., interpreting terms in a well-founded domain like natural numbers), which violates the "no imported axioms" constraint.
  - LPO is purely syntactic and doesn’t require such assumptions.

---

### **3. Why This Precedence (`times > plus > s`)?**
The precedence was chosen to reflect the **intended semantics** of the system:
- `times` is the "strongest" operation (it calls `plus` in its recursive case).
- `plus` is intermediate (it calls `s` in its recursive cases).
- `s` is the weakest (it’s the base successor function).

This ensures that:
- `times` can "dominate" `plus` (e.g., `times(x, s(y)) > plus(times(x, y), x)`).
- `plus` can "dominate" `s` (e.g., `plus(x, s(y)) > s(plus(x, y))`).
- The recursive calls (`times(x, y)` and `plus(x, y)`) are smaller than the left-hand sides.

---

### **4. Why Left-to-Right Status for `plus`?**
The `plus` rules are **left-associative** in spirit (e.g., `plus(x, s(y))` reduces to `s(plus(x, y))`), so giving `plus` **left-to-right status** ensures that:
- `plus(x, s(y)) > plus(x, y)` (because `s(y) > y` and we compare left-to-right).
- This is necessary for the LPO proof to go through for rules like `plus(x, s(y)) -> s(plus(x, y))`.

Without left-to-right status, the proof would fail because `plus` would use multiset status by default, and `plus(x, s(y))` would not necessarily be greater than `plus(x, y)`.

---

### **5. Why Not Other Orders?**
Other orders (e.g., **Knuth-Bendix order (KBO)**, **simplification order**) were not chosen because:
- **KBO** requires a **weight function**, which is more complex to define than a precedence for LPO.
- **Simplification orders** (like RPO) are less flexible than LPO for this case because of the need for left-to-right status.
