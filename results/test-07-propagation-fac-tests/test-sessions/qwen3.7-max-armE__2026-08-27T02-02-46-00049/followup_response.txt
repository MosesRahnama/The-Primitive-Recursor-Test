I chose the **polynomial interpretation** method because it directly and elegantly resolves the two competing structural features of this specific rewrite system: **syntactic size growth** and a **Peano recursion counter**. 

Here is the detailed breakdown of why this method was the most natural and robust choice for these specific rules:

### 1. Handling Syntactic Size Growth (Variable Duplication)
If you look closely at **Rule 2** (`F(x, y, S(n)) -> G(y, F(x, y, n))`), the variable `y` appears **once** on the left-hand side, but **twice** on the right-hand side (once as an argument to `G`, and once inside the recursive call to `F`). 

Because of this duplication, the raw syntactic size (number of nodes) and the depth of the term actually *increase* during this rewrite step if `y` is a large term. Therefore, naive syntactic termination metrics (like term size or depth) immediately fail. A polynomial interpretation bypasses this by assigning a semantic *weight* to the terms, allowing the decreasing "fuel" (the counter `n`) to mathematically pay for the structural growth (the extra `G` and `y`).

### 2. The "Fuel" / Counter Pattern
The third argument of `F` is explicitly constructed using Peano natural numbers (`Z` and `S(n)`). In Rule 1, it hits the base case `Z`. In Rule 2, it strictly decreases from `S(n)` to `n`. 

This is a classic primitive recursion pattern where the third argument acts as a "fuel" counter or recursion depth. Mapping `Z` to `0` and `S(n)` to `n + 1` via a polynomial interpretation is the most direct translation of the syntax into a well-founded mathematical domain ($\mathbb{N}$). It turns the structural descent on `S(n)` into standard mathematical induction.

### 3. Constructing a Tight Ranking Function
Polynomial interpretations allow us to construct a single, concrete numeric score (a ranking function) for every term. By setting the interpretation of `F` to `x + y·(n + 1) + n + 1`, we achieve a perfectly tight bound:
* The `y·(n + 1)` component ensures that when `n` decreases by 1, the interpretation drops by exactly `y + 1`.
* This drop perfectly absorbs the `+ y` added by the `G(y, ...)` wrapper on the right-hand side of Rule 2, leaving a strict, constant decrease of exactly `1` for every rewrite step. 

### Why not other standard methods?
While other standard methods *could* work, polynomial interpretation is often preferred for this specific shape of rules for practical reasons:
* **Recursive Path Ordering (RPO):** RPO would work here if we set the precedence $F > G$. However, RPO requires defining precedence relations and argument statuses (lexicographic vs. multiset), which introduces meta-level machinery. 
* **Knuth-Bendix Ordering (KBO):** KBO would also work by assigning a heavy weight to `S` and a light weight to `G`, but finding the exact weight inequalities can sometimes be more tedious than simply solving the algebraic coefficients for a polynomial.

Ultimately, the polynomial interpretation was chosen because it reduces the termination proof to **basic, self-contained algebra** that transparently proves every step decreases a concrete numeric value, without needing to invoke the structural embedding rules of path orderings.
