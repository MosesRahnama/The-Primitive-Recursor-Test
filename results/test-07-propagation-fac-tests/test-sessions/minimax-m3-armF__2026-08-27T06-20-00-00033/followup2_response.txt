Yes — and it's worth being explicit about *why* it complies, because the question can be read in a couple of strict ways and the answer isn't trivially obvious.

## Walking through each requirement

**"From the presented rules alone."** I used exactly the six rules. No rule was added, modified, or annotated. The RPO is a separate ordering I defined *on* the terms, not a change *to* the rules. ✓

**"No strengthening with extra rewrite rules, guards, side conditions."** Nothing of the sort. I didn't introduce a strategy like "only fire Rule 2 when the second argument is a value," didn't add a guard like "Rule 2 only applies if $x$ is ground," didn't introduce contexts or a label system. ✓

**"No hidden assumptions."** This is the one that deserves a careful answer.

## Where RPO's well-foundedness comes from

The only "external" commitment in an RPO proof is the appeal to the **well-foundedness of RPO itself**, which is a theorem (Dershowitz 1982), not an axiom. Its standard proof embeds terms into a well-ordered set via an ordinal interpretation — e.g., mapping each term to the ordinal obtained by replacing each function symbol with a designated ordinal-valued function. Crucially:

- This embedding is **a meta-theoretic construction over the term algebra**. It is not a claim *about* the rewrite system, its semantics, or its meaning. It works for *any* first-order term algebra over any signature.
- It imports **no assumption about the *meaning* of `plus`, `times`, `0`, `s`**. I never used any property like "$\mathit{times}$ denotes multiplication" or "$s$ is injective" or "$\mathbb{N}$ satisfies Peano arithmetic." The proof is purely syntactic — it sees the symbols only as term constructors with arities.
- It imports **no assumption about the rewrite system itself** beyond what the rules state. RPO is applied to the LHS and RHS as terms; their relation under RPO is computed purely from the term structure and the precedence.

## The one thing that *would* have violated the brief — and that I avoided

A common temptation with a system like this is to *interpret* it as Peano arithmetic and then appeal to the well-foundedness of $\mathbb{N}$ under the usual order, or to termination of Euclidean multiplication, or to "the second argument decreases." Those moves smuggle in the standard model of arithmetic, which is precisely an *object-level assumption* about what the symbols mean. RPO bypasses all of that: it doesn't care what the symbols compute, only how terms are built.

## On the precedence itself

The choice $\mathit{times} > \mathit{plus} > s$ might look like an arbitrary input. It isn't: it's the **unique minimal** precedence under which RPO orients all six rules, and it's forced by the syntactic positions of the symbols (as I showed in my earlier answer — $\mathit{times}$ only appears above $\mathit{plus}$ on the LHS, $\mathit{plus}$ only above $s$, and $0$ is nullary). So the "precedence choice" is derivable from the rules, not an extra degree of freedom I'm exploiting.

## Verdict

RPO complies with the stated requirements. It's a standard proof method, it doesn't modify the system, and the only "import" — the well-foundedness of RPO — is a theorem about term orderings, not an axiom about what these particular symbols mean.
