I chose the **monotone polynomial interpretation** (specifically, a weight function combining addition and multiplication) based on a process of elimination of other standard termination techniques, combined with the structural nature of the rewrite rules. 

Here is the detailed breakdown of why this method was the most strategic and robust choice:

### 1. Knuth-Bendix Ordering (KBO) is strictly ruled out
The most immediate constraint in this system is **variable duplication** in Rule 2:
* **LHS:** $F(x, y, S(n))$ — the variable $y$ appears **once**.
* **RHS:** $G(y, F(x, y, n))$ — the variable $y$ appears **twice**.

A strict, foundational requirement of the Knuth-Bendix Ordering is that no variable may appear more times on the right-hand side of a rule than on the left-hand side. Because Rule 2 duplicates $y$, KBO completely fails for this system and cannot be used.

### 2. Recursive Path Ordering (RPO/LPO) works, but is clunky to specify
RPO or LPO *could* technically prove this system terminating. To do so, one would need to:
1. Define a precedence where $F > G$.
2. Assign a **multiset status** to $F$ (rather than a lexicographic one) so that $F(x, y, S(n)) >_{rpo} F(x, y, n)$ holds via the multiset extension $\{x, y, S(n)\} \gg_{mul} \{x, y, n\}$.

While valid, explaining and verifying multiset extensions and status assignments in a text-based proof introduces meta-level complexity. A polynomial interpretation reduces the entire proof to elementary arithmetic (addition and multiplication), which is universally understood, requires no precedence tables, and leaves zero ambiguity for the reader to verify.

### 3. Dependency Pairs (DP) add unnecessary meta-framework overhead
The Dependency Pair framework is the gold standard for *automated* termination provers. If applied here, it would identify $F$ as the only defined symbol and generate a single dependency pair:
$F^\#(x, y, S(n)) \to F^\#(x, y, n)$

This trivially terminates (e.g., via a simple projection or subterm ordering). However, invoking the DP framework requires introducing marked symbols ($F^\#$), defining "defined symbols" vs. "constructors", and building a dependency graph. For a human-readable proof of a two-rule system, invoking the full DP machinery is overkill. A direct algebraic interpretation solves it in one shot.

### 4. The "Budgeting" Intuition of Polynomial Interpretations
Structurally, Rule 2 represents **primitive recursion** (similar to how addition or multiplication is defined over Peano integers). The third argument, $n$, acts as a recursion depth counter. 
* At each recursive step, we peel off one $S$ (decreasing the counter).
* In exchange, the system wraps the recursive call in a new $G$ symbol.

To prove termination, the "cost" of introducing the new $G$ on the RHS must be strictly paid for by the "budget" provided by removing the $S$ on the LHS. 

By defining $F(a, b, c) = (1 + w(a) + w(b)) \cdot (1 + w(c))$, the third argument $c$ acts as a **multiplier**. When we rewrite $F(x, y, S(n)) \to G(y, F(x, y, n))$, the removal of the $S$ reduces the multiplier $w(c)$ by exactly $1$. Because it is a multiplier, dropping it by $1$ reduces the total weight of the term by the entire base factor $(1 + w(x) + w(y))$. This provides a massive "budget" drop that easily pays for the $+1$ additive cost of introducing the new $G$ symbol on the RHS. 

### Summary
I picked the polynomial interpretation because **KBO is mathematically forbidden** by the variable duplication, **RPO requires cumbersome status definitions**, and **Dependency Pairs require heavy meta-machinery**. The polynomial interpretation elegantly captures the primitive-recursive "budgeting" of the system using nothing but basic, easily verifiable arithmetic.
