Yes. The LPO proof complies with those requirements.

The chosen precedence

\[
F \succ G \succ S \succ Z
\]

is a **meta-level termination-proof device**, not an added object-level axiom. It does not:

- add rewrite rules,
- restrict substitutions,
- require the third argument to be a numeral,
- assume constructor disjointness or injectivity,
- add guards or side conditions, or
- assign semantic meanings to \(F,G,S,Z\).

It simply defines a well-founded ordering on the existing finite first-order terms. The standard properties of LPO—well-foundedness, substitution stability, context closure, and the subterm property—belong to the proof method explicitly permitted by the prompt.

For completeness, under definitions of LPO that require the left side to dominate all arguments during a same-root lexicographic comparison, that condition also holds:

\[
F(x,y,S(n)) \succ x,\quad
F(x,y,S(n)) \succ y,\quad
F(x,y,S(n)) \succ n
\]

by the subterm property, with \(n\) occurring inside \(S(n)\). Since \(S(n)\succ n\), this yields

\[
F(x,y,S(n))\succ F(x,y,n).
\]

Then \(F\succ G\), together with domination of both \(G\)-arguments,

\[
F(x,y,S(n))\succ y
\quad\text{and}\quad
F(x,y,S(n))\succ F(x,y,n),
\]

orients the second rule. The first rule is oriented directly by the subterm property.

Thus the proof uses only the syntactic form of the presented rules plus a standard reduction ordering. The only background convention is the ordinary one inherent in “first-order term rewriting”: terms are finite first-order terms. No extra object-level structure is imported.
