**Verdict: Yes.** Termination follows from the rules alone, for arbitrary first-order terms—not merely terms where the third argument is a numeral.

Use the **lexicographic path ordering (LPO)** with lexicographic status for all symbols and, for example, the precedence

\[
F \succ G \succ S \succ Z.
\]

LPO is a well-founded simplification ordering: it is closed under substitutions and contexts, so orienting every rule strictly proves termination.

### Rule 1

\[
F(x,y,Z)\to x
\]

The left-hand side is strictly greater than \(x\) by the subterm property, since \(x\) is an immediate argument of \(F(x,y,Z)\).

### Rule 2

\[
F(x,y,S(n))\to G(y,F(x,y,n)).
\]

Let \(l=F(x,y,S(n))\). Since \(F\succ G\), it suffices under LPO to show that \(l\) is greater than each argument of the right-hand \(G\):

1. \(l\succ y\), by the subterm property.
2. \(l\succ F(x,y,n)\). Both terms have root \(F\); lexicographically, their first two arguments are identical, while
   \[
   S(n)\succ n
   \]
   by the subterm property. Hence
   \[
   F(x,y,S(n))\succ F(x,y,n).
   \]

Therefore,

\[
F(x,y,S(n))\succ G(y,F(x,y,n)).
\]

Both rules strictly decrease in a well-founded ordering. Consequently, the rewrite system is terminating. This argument treats \(x,y,n\) as arbitrary terms and imports no assumptions that \(Z,S\) encode natural numbers or that \(G\) has any additional properties.
