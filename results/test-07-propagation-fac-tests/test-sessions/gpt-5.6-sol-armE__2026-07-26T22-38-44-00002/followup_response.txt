I chose **lexicographic path ordering (LPO)** because the second rule has two features that make simpler measures awkward:

- It replaces \(S(n)\) by its proper subterm \(n\), suggesting a structural descent.
- It also introduces a new \(G\)-node and duplicates \(y\), so ordinary term size or symbol-count measures need not decrease.

LPO captures exactly the relevant syntactic structure:

1. Its **subterm property** gives \(S(n)\succ n\) and \(F(x,y,Z)\succ x\).
2. Lexicographic comparison therefore gives
   \[
   F(x,y,S(n))\succ F(x,y,n).
   \]
3. Choosing \(F\succ G\) lets the whole left side dominate the newly introduced outer \(G\), provided it dominates both \(G\)-arguments—which it does.

Most importantly, LPO is already a well-founded ordering closed under substitutions and contexts. Thus it proves termination for arbitrary terms and for reductions occurring anywhere inside a term, without assuming that \(S\) and \(Z\) denote natural numbers or that the third argument has any special semantic form.

The precedence is only a proof-device ordering on the existing symbols, not an added rewrite rule or object-level axiom. Other methods could work, but LPO gives a short, entirely syntactic proof and naturally handles the growth and duplication in the second rule.
