I picked these specific methods—**Dependency Pairs (DP)** and **Semantic Interpretation**—because the system exhibits a classic "hidden decrease" pattern that causes standard, purely syntactic termination techniques to fail. 

Here is the step-by-step reasoning for why these methods were necessary:

### 1. The Bottleneck: A "Hidden" Structural Decrease
The primary obstacle to termination is the factorial rule:
`fac(s(x)) -> times(fac(p(s(x))), s(x))`

The recursive call passes `p(s(x))` to `fac`. Semantically, `p` is the predecessor function, so `p(s(x))` evaluates to `x`, which is strictly smaller than `s(x)`. However, **syntactically**, `p(s(x))` is not a subterm of `s(x)`. 

### 2. Why Standard Syntactic Methods Fail
Because of this syntactic mismatch, the standard workhorses of automated termination analysis fail immediately:
*   **Simplification Orderings (RPO, LPO, KBO):** These rely on the subterm property or lexicographic comparisons of function symbols. Since `p(s(x))` is not a subterm of `s(x)`, and `p` is not greater than `s` in any sensible precedence, these orderings cannot prove that `s(x) > p(s(x))`.
*   **Standard Polynomial Interpretations:** To use polynomials over the natural numbers, we must satisfy the `p` rules. The rule `p(s(s(x))) -> s(p(s(x)))` translates to the inequality $P(x+2) \ge P(x+1) + 1$. 
    If $P(x) = ax + b$, this requires $a(x+2)+b \ge a(x+1)+b+1$, which simplifies to $a \ge 1$. 
    Because polynomial coefficients over $\mathbb{N}$ must be non-negative integers, $P(x)$ must grow at least as fast as $x$ (i.e., $P(x) \ge x$). 
    Consequently, for the `fac` dependency pair, we would need $FAC(x+1) > FAC(P(x+1))$. But since $P(x+1) \ge x+1$, this requires $FAC(x+1) > FAC(\ge x+1)$, which is impossible to satisfy strictly. Thus, standard polynomial interpretations cannot orient this rule.

### 3. Why I Picked Dependency Pairs (DP)
Since we cannot easily orient the entire system with a single reduction ordering, the Dependency Pair framework is the natural escalation. 
*   **Isolation:** DPs allow us to isolate the recursive calls from the rest of the system. We only need to analyze the cycle `FAC(s(x)) -> FAC(p(s(x)))`.
*   **Contextual Analysis:** By isolating the cycle, we can analyze the *reduction behavior* of `p(s(x))` specifically in the context of usable rules, rather than forcing a global syntactic ordering. This allows us to observe that `p` eventually strips away the `s` constructors, breaking the infinite chain.

### 4. Why I Picked Semantic Interpretation
When syntactic interpretations (like polynomials) fail due to integer-coefficient constraints, the most robust fallback is to use the **intended semantic model** of the rules.
*   **Bypassing Syntactic Limits:** The rules are literally the recursive definitions of Peano arithmetic. By mapping the terms to their actual mathematical values in $\mathbb{N}$ (where $p$ is interpreted as the true predecessor function $P(x) = \max(0, x-1)$), the problematic inequality $P(x+1) \ge x+1$ vanishes. Instead, we get $P(x+1) = x < x+1$.
*   **Derivable from the Rules:** The prompt specified "without importing structural axioms... beyond what is derivable from the given rules." The standard model of natural numbers is the unique minimal model derived directly from these specific rewrite rules on ground terms. Using the semantic values of the terms as a well-founded measure is a standard, valid termination technique that relies solely on the operational semantics defined by the TRS itself.

**Summary:** I chose these methods because the `fac` rule hides a structural decrease behind a `p` function call. This breaks purely syntactic orderings and standard polynomials, making it mandatory to use Dependency Pairs to isolate the recursion and Semantic Interpretation to capture the actual mathematical decrease.
