I chose **dependency pairs combined with a weak semantic interpretation** because the system has two distinct features:

1. **Most rules compute without obviously shrinking the whole term.**  
   For example,
   \[
   times(s(x),y)\to plus(times(x,y),y)
   \]
   and
   \[
   fac(s(x))\to times(fac(p(s(x))),s(x))
   \]
   make the right-hand side syntactically larger. A simple term-size argument therefore fails.

2. **The actual recursive calls do decrease specific arguments.**  
   Dependency pairs expose exactly those calls:
   - `plus` recurses from \(s(y)\) to \(y\);
   - `times` recurses from \(s(x)\) to \(x\);
   - `p` recurses from \(s(s(x))\) to \(s(x)\);
   - `fac` recurses from \(s(x)\) to \(p(s(x))\).

The natural-number interpretation serves a different role: it shows that the ordinary rewrite steps between recursive calls cannot increase the selected semantic measure. In particular, interpreting

\[
p(a)=\max(a-1,0)
\]

makes the factorial call decrease:

\[
[s(x)]=x+1>[p(s(x))]=x.
\]

This avoids assuming that \(x\) is syntactically a numeral. The inequality holds for every valuation of \(x\) in \(\mathbb N\), so the proof applies to arbitrary terms.

A single globally strict interpretation would be less natural here because several rules are best interpreted as equalities—for example the arithmetic rules and the factorial equation:

\[
(x+1)!=(x+1)x!.
\]

Dependency pairs let those rules be merely weakly decreasing while requiring strict decrease only at the recursive cycles. Thus the method matches the structure of the TRS: **semantic preservation for computation, strict descent for recursion**.
