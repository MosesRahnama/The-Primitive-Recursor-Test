I chose semantic labeling because the difficult recursive call is

\[
\mathrm{fac}(s(x)) \to \cdots \mathrm{fac}(p(s(x)))\cdots
\]

Here \(p(s(x))\) is **not syntactically a subterm** of \(s(x)\), and it is actually larger by ordinary symbol-counting. Thus simple structural recursion, term-size measures, or a straightforward simplification order do not directly show descent. The rule

\[
p(s(s(x))) \to s(p(s(x)))
\]

also does not decrease ordinary term size.

The rules nevertheless describe a clear semantic descent: under the natural-number interpretation, \(p(s(x))\) has the value of \(x\), so the recursive `fac` call decreases from \(x+1\) to \(x\). Semantic labeling makes that hidden numeric decrease explicit, while also handling the ordinary decreases in `plus`, `times`, and `p`.

I picked it because it distinguishes two issues cleanly:

1. The system is not obviously terminating by raw syntax alone.
2. Its rules admit a well-founded model that exposes all recursive decreases.

Using naturals here is a metatheoretic termination interpretation whose compatibility is checked against every displayed rule; it does not add predecessor or arithmetic equations as new object-level rewrite axioms.
