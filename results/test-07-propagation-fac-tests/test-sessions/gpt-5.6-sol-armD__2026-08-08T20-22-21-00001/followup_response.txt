I chose a **polynomial interpretation over \(\mathbb N\)** because the rules combine two different recursive patterns that a single obvious syntactic size measure does not handle cleanly:

- `plus` recurses on its second argument.
- `times` recurses on its first argument but introduces both a `plus` and another copy of `y`.
- `p(s(s(x))) -> s(p(s(x)))` preserves much of the syntax and merely shifts one `s` from below `p` to above it.

For example, ordinary symbol count does **not** orient the multiplication rule:

\[
times(s(x),y)\to plus(times(x,y),y),
\]

because the right-hand side duplicates \(y\) and can be larger than the left-hand side.

A polynomial interpretation is well suited here because its coefficients can assign different significance to argument positions and symbols:

- Giving the second argument of `plus` coefficient \(2\) makes
  \[
  plus(x,s(y))\to s(plus(x,y))
  \]
  decrease.
- The chosen mixed polynomial for `times`,
  \[
  2xy+2x+y+1,
  \]
  accounts for the duplicated \(y\) in the multiplication rule and makes that rule decrease by exactly \(1\).
- Interpreting \(p(a)=2a\) makes removing an `s` below \(p\) outweigh adding an `s` above it.

I also chose this method because it gives a **single, direct global certificate**:

1. every interpretation is strictly monotone, so decreases are preserved inside all contexts;
2. every rule can be checked by elementary arithmetic;
3. the target order \(>\) on \(\mathbb N\) is well-founded;
4. no assumptions about terms denoting natural numbers, constructor freeness, induction equations, or intended arithmetic semantics are required.

So the method was selected mainly for robustness and transparency: one explicit interpretation handles every rule, including the two potentially awkward ones for `times` and `p`, while staying entirely within standard syntactic termination theory.
