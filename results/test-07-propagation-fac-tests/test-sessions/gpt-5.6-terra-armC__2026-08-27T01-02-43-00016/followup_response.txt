I picked a rule-oriented structural/dependency analysis because the question explicitly excludes importing intended meanings such as “`p` is predecessor” or “terms are natural numbers.”

So I treated the symbols purely syntactically and examined:

1. **Where recursive calls occur** in each rule.
2. **Which argument structurally decreases** along those calls.
3. **Whether any rule can recreate a call at the same or greater recursion depth indefinitely.**
4. **How the mutually interacting symbols compose**—especially `fac → times → plus`, and `fac → p → fac`.

That is the appropriate first check for a first-order TRS with constructor-like symbols `0` and `s`. The key observations are visible directly in the rule shapes:

- `plus` recurses under one fewer outer `s` in its second argument.
- `times` recurses under one fewer outer `s` in its first argument.
- `p` moves from `p(s(s(x)))` to `p(s(x))`, again consuming one relevant `s`.
- `fac(s(x))` calls `fac(p(s(x)))`; the accompanying `p` rules ensure that, whenever this call can continue as a `fac(s(...))` call, it is on a structurally smaller successor-depth.

I did not rely on an intended arithmetic interpretation. Saying that `p(s(x))` “acts like a predecessor” was only shorthand for the syntactic fact established by its own two rewrite rules: its recursive `p` occurrence is nested beneath fewer leading `s` constructors.

A fully formal termination proof would package those observations into a well-founded ordering or a lexicographic measure—for example, one that tracks applicable `fac` successor-depth together with the corresponding measures for `p`, `times`, and `plus`. I chose the structural explanation because the request asked for a raw assessment and brief reasoning rather than a complete ordering construction.
