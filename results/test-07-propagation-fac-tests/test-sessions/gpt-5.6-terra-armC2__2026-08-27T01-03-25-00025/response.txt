Yes. Termination can be established purely as a syntactic property of this TRS; it does not require assuming that terms denote natural numbers or importing equations about `p`, `plus`, `times`, etc.

The potentially delicate rule is:

```text
fac(s(x)) -> times(fac(p(s(x))), s(x))
```

but it does not create a rewrite cycle. For `fac` to recurse again, `p(s(x))` must reduce to a term headed by `s`. The only way that happens is through

```text
p(s(s(x))) -> s(p(s(x)))
```

which moves the pending `p` inward through an already-present successor layer. With finitely represented terms, this cannot happen indefinitely: once no applicable outer `s` layer remains, the relevant `p(...)` is stuck and the inner `fac` no longer matches `fac(s(_))`.

Likewise:

- `plus` recursively removes one outer `s` from its second argument.
- `times` recursively removes one outer `s` from its first argument.
- `p` moves its recursive occurrence beneath a successor layer and cannot loop by itself.
- Duplication in the `times` rule can increase term size and derivation length, but does not provide a way to regenerate an unbounded recursive descent.

Thus a formal syntactic termination proof—for example via dependency-pair analysis with an appropriate well-founded measure/order—is possible from these rules alone. What would *not* be justified is a proof that merely assumes, without analysis, that `p(s(x))` is the arithmetic predecessor of `s(x)` for every arbitrary term `x`; that semantic assumption is unnecessary.
