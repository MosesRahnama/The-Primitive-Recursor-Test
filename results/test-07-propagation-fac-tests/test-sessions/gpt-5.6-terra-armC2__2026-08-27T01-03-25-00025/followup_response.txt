I chose a syntactic recursive-call/dependency-pair style analysis because the question explicitly rules out relying on the intended arithmetic meaning of the symbols.

A naïve semantic argument such as “`p` is predecessor, so `fac` decreases” would not be valid here unless that behavior were derived from the rules themselves. Likewise, a simple term-size argument is unsuitable: for example,

```text
times(s(x), y) -> plus(times(x, y), y)
```

can make the term larger and duplicates `y`.

Instead, the relevant question is whether recursive calls can be re-enabled indefinitely by the rewrite rules:

- `plus` consumes successor layers from its second argument.
- `times` consumes successor layers from its first argument.
- The recursive `p` rule moves `p` inward:

  ```text
  p(s(s(x))) -> s(p(s(x)))
  ```

  It can expose an outer `s`, but only by consuming one already-existing successor layer beneath the `p`.

- `fac` creates `fac(p(s(x)))`. For that inner `fac` to become reducible again, `p(s(x))` must rewrite to something headed by `s`. That requires `x` itself to have another outer `s`; each such re-enabling step exhausts one finite successor layer in the original term.

That is exactly the sort of interaction dependency pairs, possibly combined with a decreasing auxiliary measure for the `p`-successor structure, are intended to formalize. I mentioned dependency pairs as a suitable proof framework, not because the informal discussion itself was a complete dependency-pair certificate.

So the method was selected because it is rule-internal, handles non-size-decreasing rules and duplication, and does not assume that the terms represent natural numbers.
