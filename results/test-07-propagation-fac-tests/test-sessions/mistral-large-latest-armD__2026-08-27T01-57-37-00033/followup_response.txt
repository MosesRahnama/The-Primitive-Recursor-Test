Great question! The choice of **Recursive Path Order (RPO)** (or more generally, **simplification orders**) for proving termination of this rewrite system was deliberate, and here’s why:

---

### **1. Why RPO?**
RPO is a **syntactic termination method** that works directly on the structure of the rewrite rules without requiring additional semantic interpretations (e.g., interpreting terms as numbers or using external orderings like polynomial interpretations). This makes it ideal for systems like this one, where:
- There are no **variables with binding** (e.g., no λ-abstractions or quantifiers).
- There is no **external memory** or **state** (e.g., no mutable references or I/O).
- The rules are **purely syntactic** (no side conditions, guards, or axioms beyond what’s given).

RPO is also **complete for finite rewrite systems**: if a system is terminating, RPO can (in principle) prove it, provided you choose the right precedence and status. This makes it a "go-to" method for such problems.

---

### **2. Why Not Other Methods?**
Here’s why other common termination methods were less suitable for this system:

#### **(a) Polynomial Interpretations**
- **Pros**: Works well for arithmetic-like systems (e.g., `plus` and `times`).
- **Cons**:
  - Requires finding a **semantic interpretation** (e.g., mapping terms to polynomials or natural numbers), which can be tricky for non-arithmetic symbols like `p` (predecessor).
  - The `p` rules (`p(s(s(x))) → s(p(s(x)))` and `p(s(0)) → 0`) are not naturally captured by standard polynomial interpretations (e.g., `p` would need to be interpreted as a function that "undoes" `s`, which is not polynomial).
  - Polynomial interpretations often require **trial and error** to find the right coefficients, whereas RPO is more mechanical.

#### **(b) Dependency Pairs (DP) Method**
- **Pros**: Powerful for complex systems with nested recursion.
- **Cons**:
  - Overkill for this system, which is **simple and non-nested** (no mutual recursion or deeply nested calls).
  - DP requires decomposing rules into dependency pairs and then proving termination of the resulting DP problem, which is more work than RPO for this case.
  - RPO can handle this system directly without extra machinery.

#### **(c) Lexicographic Path Order (LPO)**
- **Pros**: Simpler than RPO (no status to worry about).
- **Cons**:
  - **Weaker than RPO**: LPO cannot handle cases where a function symbol needs **multiset or lexicographic status** (e.g., `plus` and `times` in this system benefit from lexicographic status).
  - The `p` rules would be harder to orient with LPO because `p(s(s(x)))` and `s(p(s(x)))` are not easily comparable without status.

#### **(d) Semantic Methods (e.g., Well-Founded Orders)**
- **Pros**: Can work if you interpret terms as natural numbers (e.g., `s(x)` as `x + 1`, `p(x)` as `x - 1`).
- **Cons**:
  - Requires **external assumptions** (e.g., interpreting `s` as successor and `p` as predecessor), which violates the constraint of "no object-level axioms beyond the rules shown."
  - The system is **syntactic**, and RPO can prove termination without such interpretations.

---

### **3. Why This Precedence?**
The precedence `times > plus > s > p > 0` was chosen because:
1. **`times > plus`**: The `times` rule reduces to `plus`, so `times` must dominate `plus`.
2. **`plus > s`**: The `plus` rule reduces to `s`, so `plus` must dominate `s`.
3. **`s > p`**: The `p` rules "undo" `s`, so `s` must dominate `p` (otherwise, `p(s(s(x)))` would not be greater than `s(p(s(x)))` in RPO).
4. **`p > 0`**: The rule `p(s(0)) → 0` requires `p` to dominate `0`.

The **lexicographic status** for `plus` and `times` was chosen because:
- Their recursive calls are on the **first argument** (e.g., `plus(x, s(y))` recurses on `x` and `y`, but the critical recursion is on `x` in `plus(x, y)`).
- This ensures that `plus(x, s(y)) > plus(x, y)` (since `s(y) > y`).

Other precedences fail to orient all rules. For example:

- If `plus > times`:

---

### **5. Summary: Why RPO Worked Best**
1. **Syntactic**: RPO works directly on the rules without needing external interpretations.
2. **Mechanical**: The precedence and status can be derived systematically (no guesswork like in polynomial interpretations).
3. **Powerful Enough**: RPO can handle all rules in this system, including the tricky `p` rules.
4. **No Extra Assumptions**: Unlike semantic methods, RPO doesn’t require interpreting terms as numbers or other objects.

For this system, RPO was the **simplest, most direct, and assumption-free** method to prove termination.
