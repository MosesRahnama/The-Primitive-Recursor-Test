I chose the **Polynomial Interpretation** method for this specific system because of three key reasons:

### 1. Simple Size-Counting Fails (Why we need a weighted approach)
A simple syntactic count of symbols (the size of the term) does not work here. 
Let’s look at Rule 2:
$$\text{LHS: } F(x, y, S(n)) \implies \text{RHS: } G(y, F(x, y, n))$$
* The LHS has 4 symbols (excluding variables): $F$, $S$, and the parentheses/commas structure.
* The RHS has 5 symbols: $G$ and $F$, plus their structure.

Because the right-hand side has *more* function symbols than the left-hand side, the system is not size-decreasing. We needed a method that could weight the arguments of $F$ so that the decrement of $S(n)$ to $n$ carries more "weight" than the introduction of the new outer symbol $G$. Polynomials do this naturally by multiplication.

### 2. Complete Self-Containment (No external framework)
The prompt specifically asked for an assessment using "only the presented rules... without importing structural axioms or object-level assumptions."

While syntactic path orderings (like **LPO**—Lexicographic Path Ordering) could also prove termination here (using the precedence $F \succ G$), LPO requires importing a heavy mathematical framework: defining the recursive path ordering, proving its well-foundedness, and establishing the preservation of the termination property under substitution. 

By contrast, the **Polynomial Interpretation** method is completely self-contained. It translates the symbolic rules into high-school algebra over positive integers. Once the polynomials are defined:
* Strict monotonicity is proven by simple derivatives ($\ge 1 > 0$).
* Strict decrease is proven by basic subtraction (e.g., $(2 > 0)$).

It requires absolutely no external machinery other than the fact that standard integers cannot decrease infinitely (the well-foundedness of $>$ on $\mathbb{N}_{>0}$).

### 3. Natural Mapping of the "Counter" Behavior
The system defines a classic primitive recursive loop:
* $F$ peels a layer of $S$ off its third argument.
* It wraps the result in $G$ and passes $y$ along.

The third argument of $F$ acts as a **counter**. The polynomial $[F](x,y,z) = z(y+2) + x$ captures this perfectly: the "potential energy" of the term is directly proportional to the counter $z$, scaled by the multiplier $(y+2)$ to offset the cost of duplicating/introducing $y$ and $G$ in the recursion step.
