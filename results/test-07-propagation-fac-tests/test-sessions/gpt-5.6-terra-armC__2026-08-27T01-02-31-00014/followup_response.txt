I picked a syntactic descent / dependency-chain analysis because the question was specifically whether termination follows from the rules themselves, without giving the symbols external arithmetic meaning.

The relevant feature is not that `plus`, `times`, `p`, and `fac` denote familiar arithmetic operations. It is that their recursive calls are constrained by the literal term shapes in the left- and right-hand sides:

- `plus` and `times` recurse with one fewer exposed `s` in the relevant argument.
- `p(s(s(x))) -> s(p(s(x)))` preserves overall size but shortens the consecutive `s`-prefix still under the recursive `p`.
- The only recursive `fac` call is `fac(p(s(x)))`. For that to become another reducible `fac(s(...))`, the `p` term must expose an `s`; doing so consumes a finite amount of successor-prefix structure from `x`.

So I focused on whether there is a syntactic recursive cycle that can replenish the structure needed for another recursive call. There is no rule that creates an unbounded new supply of leading `s` constructors in the argument being processed by `p`/`fac`.

I chose that approach rather than, for example, interpreting `s` as numeric successor and `fac` as factorial, because such an interpretation would import precisely the object-level assumptions the question excludes. A fully formal presentation could package the same idea as a dependency-pair argument with a well-founded measure on the relevant successor-prefix/recursive-call structure; my prior response gave the underlying reasoning rather than such a certificate.
