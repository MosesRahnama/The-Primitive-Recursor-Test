Great question! The choice of method was guided by the **structure of the rewrite system** and the **limitations of simpler techniques**. Here’s why I picked the **Dependency Pair (DP) method with polynomial interpretations** over other approaches:

---

### **1. Why Not Lexicographic/Recursive Path Order (LPO/RPO)?**
- **LPO/RPO is simple and automatable**, but it **fails for this system** because of the `p` (predecessor) rules.
  - Rule 6: `p(s(s(x))) → s(p(s(x)))` requires `p > s` in the precedence, but other rules (e.g., `fac(s(x)) → times(fac(p(s(x))), s(x))`) require `s > p` to orient the `s(x)` argument.
  - This **contradiction** means LPO/RPO cannot orient all rules simultaneously.
- **Conclusion:** LPO/RPO is too rigid for this system.

---

### **2. Why Not Simpler Methods (e.g., Innermost Termination, Match-Bounds)?**
- **Innermost termination** (a weaker property) might work, but it doesn’t guarantee full termination (e.g., outermost reductions could still loop).
- **Match-bounds** (a technique for string rewriting) is not directly applicable here because:
  - The system has **non-linear rules** (e.g., `times(x, 0) → 0`).
  - The `p` and `fac` rules involve **non-monotonic behavior** (e.g., `p` decreases arguments, while `s` increases them).
- **Conclusion:** These methods are either too weak or not well-suited.

---

### **3. Why Dependency Pairs (DP)?**
The DP method is **designed for systems where simpler orders fail**. It works by:
1. **Focusing on recursive calls** (dependency pairs) rather than the entire rewrite relation.
2. **Allowing flexible interpretations** (e.g., polynomial, matrix, or semantic) to orient DPs.
3. **Handling non-monotonic rules** (like `p(s(s(x))) → s(p(s(x)))`) by treating them as separate "recursive components."

#### **Why DP Works Here:**
- The `p` and `fac` rules **do not form infinite chains** when analyzed in isolation (via DPs).
- The **polynomial interpretation** can handle:
  - `plus` and `times` (monotonic, additive/multiplicative).
  - `p` (non-monotonic, but `max(x - 1, 0)` ensures termination).
  - `fac` (exponential growth, which dominates the `p` decreases).
- **No extra assumptions** are needed—just the rules and standard DP machinery.

---

### **4. Why Polynomial Interpretations?**
- **Polynomials are expressive enough** for this system:
  - Linear interpretations work for `plus`, `times`, and `p`.
  - Exponential interpretations (e.g., `2^x`) handle `fac`.
- **They are automatable** (many termination tools use them).
- **They avoid overcomplicating the proof** (e.g., no need for semantic interpretations or external theories).

---

### **5. Alternative Methods Considered (and Rejected)**
| Method               | Why Not?                                                                 |
|----------------------|--------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| **LPO/RPO**          | Fails due to `p` vs. `s` precedence conflict.                            |
| **Innermost Term.**  | Doesn’t guarantee full termination.                                      |
| **Match-Bounds**     | Not applicable to non-linear or non-monotonic rules.                     |
| **Semantic Methods** | Overkill; polynomials suffice.                                           |
| **Matrix Interpret.**| More complex than needed; polynomials work.                              |

---

### **6. Summary: Why DP + Polynomials?**
1. **LPO/RPO fails** → Need a more flexible method.
2. **DP handles recursive structure** → Isolates problematic rules (e.g., `p` and `fac`).
3. **Polynomials are sufficient** → No need for advanced techniques.
4. **No extra assumptions** → Proof stays within the given rules.

This combination is **standard for such systems** and is used in tools like AProVE, TTT2, and NaTT. It’s a "sweet spot" between simplicity and power.
