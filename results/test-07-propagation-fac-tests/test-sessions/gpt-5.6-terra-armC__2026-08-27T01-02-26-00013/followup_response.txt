I picked a structural/dependency-pair style argument because the system is constructor-oriented: the potentially recursive symbols are `plus`, `times`, `p`, and `fac`, and their recursive calls are governed by the visible `s`/`0` term structure. That is the appropriate starting point when no object-level arithmetic meaning may be assumed.

More specifically:

- `plus` and `times` have plainly decreasing recursive argument positions.
- `p` has its own terminating recursion along an `s`-prefix.
- The only nontrivial interaction is `fac → fac` through `p`; thus a proper proof must analyze the `fac` and `p` dependency behavior together, rather than merely assert that the RHS is syntactically smaller.

I mentioned dependency pairs because they are a standard purely syntactic method for exactly that situation: they isolate recursive-call chains and show that no infinite chain can pass through `fac` and `p`. No assumption such as “`p` is predecessor on natural numbers” is needed.

That said, my prior explanation was compressed. Referring to “leading `s`-chains” should be understood as a fact about matching and rewriting of the displayed terms, not as an appeal to the intended arithmetic interpretation. In particular, if `p(s(x))` cannot rewrite into a term headed by `s`, then the resulting `fac(p(s(x)))` is not another `fac` redex; it is stuck at the root.
