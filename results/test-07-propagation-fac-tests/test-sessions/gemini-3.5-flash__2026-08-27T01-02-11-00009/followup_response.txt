I chose the **Dependency Pair (DP) Framework** combined with **Polynomial Interpretations** for three primary reasons: **modularity**, the **non-trivial recursion** in the `fac` rule, and the **limitations of purely syntactic orders**. 

Here is a detailed breakdown of why this was the most effective and robust method for this specific system:

---

### 1. Modularity (Decomposition into SCCs)
A direct termination proof (finding a single reduction order for the entire system at once) is notoriously difficult for systems with hierarchical dependencies like this one, where:
$$\text{fac} \rightarrow \text{times} \rightarrow \text{plus}$$
By using the DP Framework, we can decompose the system into **Strongly Connected Components (SCCs)**. This allowed us to:
* Isolate each recursive function (`plus`, `times`, `p`, and `fac`).
* Prove termination for each function independently using simple, localized termination arguments.
* Avoid the mathematical complexity of finding a single, global ordering that satisfies all rules simultaneously.

### 2. The Syntactic Defeats of standard LPO/MPO
Standard syntactic path orderings like **Lexicographic Path Order (LPO)** or **Multiset Path Order (MPO)** struggle or fail outright on the `fac` rule:
$$\text{fac}(s(x)) \rightarrow \text{times}(\text{fac}(p(s(x))), s(x))$$

For LPO to prove this rule terminates, the left-hand side must be strictly greater than the right-hand side ($\text{fac}(s(x)) \succ_{lpo} \text{times}(\text{fac}(p(s(x))), s(x))$). 
* This requires $\text{fac}$ to have a higher precedence than $\text{times}$ ($\text{fac} \succ_{lpo} \text{times}$).
* It also requires the arguments of the recursive call to decrease: $s(x) \succ_{lpo} p(s(x))$.
* However, because $p$ is a defined function symbol and not a constructor, $s(x) \succ_{lpo} p(s(x))$ **does not hold** under standard LPO without defining a complex quasi-precedence where $s \succ_{lpo} p$, which in turn conflicts with the rule $p(s(s(x))) \rightarrow s(p(s(x)))$.

By stripping away the outer contexts using Dependency Pairs, we only had to compare the arguments of the recursive call: $s(x)$ vs. $p(s(x))$.

### 3. Polynomial Interpretations Handle "Semantic" Decreases
The decrease from $s(x)$ to $p(s(x))$ is semantic: we know that the predecessor of a successor of $x$ is smaller than the successor of $x$. 

Polynomial interpretations over the natural numbers ($\mathbb{N}_0$) are perfect for capturing this arithmetic intuition:
* By mapping $s(x)$ to $x + 2$ and $p(x)$ to $x - 1$, we easily show that $[p(s(x))] = x + 1$, which is strictly less than $[s(x)] = x + 2$.
* Attempting a global polynomial interpretation for the entire system without DPs would fail because `fac` grows factorially, while standard polynomial interpretations can only bound functions with polynomial growth. Isolating `fac` into a DP allowed us to use a linear polynomial interpretation ($[\text{FAC}](x) = x$) that only measures the recursion depth, ignoring the factorial growth of the actual term values.
