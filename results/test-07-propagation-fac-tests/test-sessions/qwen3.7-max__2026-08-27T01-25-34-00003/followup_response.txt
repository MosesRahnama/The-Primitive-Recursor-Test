I chose this specific two-stage method (Polynomial Interpretation for rules 1–7, and Semantic/Relative Termination for rule 8) because of the specific obstacles presented by the `fac` and `p` rules, which cause standard automated termination techniques to fail. 

Here is the detailed breakdown of why alternative methods fail and why this method was necessary:

### 1. Why standard syntactic orders (RPO, LPO, KBO) fail
Standard path orders (like Recursive Path Order or Knuth-Bendix Order) rely on syntactic subterm relations or precedence weights. 
* Look at the `fac` rule: `fac(s(x)) -> times(fac(p(s(x))), s(x))`. 
* The recursive call is `fac(p(s(x)))`. Syntactically, `p(s(x))` is **not** a subterm of `s(x)`, nor is it structurally smaller. 
* To a purely syntactic order, `p` and `s` are just uninterpreted function symbols. It cannot "know" that `p` means predecessor and `s` means successor, so it cannot deduce that `p(s(x))` is smaller than `s(x)`. Syntactic orders will immediately fail on this rule.

### 2. why a pure Polynomial Interpretation fails
Polynomial interpretations are the gold standard for arithmetic systems, but they hit a wall with the factorial function.
* To orient the `fac` rule using a single polynomial interpretation, we would need to assign a polynomial $P_{fac}(x)$ such that $P_{fac}(x+1) > P_{times}(P_{fac}(x), x+1)$. 
* Because the factorial function grows faster than any polynomial (it grows roughly as $n^n$), no standard polynomial interpretation over $\mathbb{N}$ can successfully bound the growth of `fac` while strictly orienting the rule. 

### 3. Why a pure Semantic Interpretation fails
Since syntactic and polynomial methods fail on `fac`, we are forced to use a **semantic interpretation**—mapping terms to their actual mathematical values in $\mathbb{N}$. This perfectly captures the decrease in `fac`, because mathematically, the predecessor of a successor is strictly smaller: $val(p(s(x))) = x < x+1 = val(s(x))$.

However, a pure semantic interpretation cannot prove termination of the *whole* system on its own. 
* Look at rule 1: `plus(x, 0) -> x`. Semantically, $val(plus(x, 0)) = x + 0 = x$, and $val(x) = x$. 
* The semantic value is **preserved**, not strictly decreased. A measure that only weakly decreases ($\ge$) cannot prove termination, because it cannot rule out infinite sequences of value-preserving steps.

### 4. The Solution: Relative Termination (The Two-Stage Approach)
Because we need a strict decrease for rules 1–7, but a semantic decrease for rule 8, the system must be split using **relative termination**:

* **Stage 1 (Strict Measure for Rules 1–7):** We use a Polynomial Interpretation to prove that the arithmetic rules terminate on their own. This is highly effective for `plus` and `times`. It also elegantly solves the unusual predecessor rule `p(s(s(x))) -> s(p(s(x)))`. By choosing $p(x) = 2x$, the LHS evaluates to $2x+4$ and the RHS to $2x+3$, yielding a strict decrease of 1.
* **Stage 2 (Semantic Measure for Rule 8 relative to 1–7):** We use the semantic interpretation for `fac`. We observe that rules 1–7 are *semantic identities* (they preserve the mathematical value of the term). Therefore, no matter how many times rules 1–7 fire, the semantic value of the argument to `fac` never increases. When rule 8 fires, it strictly decreases the semantic value. 

### Summary
I picked this method because the `fac` rule requires **semantic knowledge** (that predecessor-of-successor decreases) which breaks syntactic orders, and its **super-polynomial growth** breaks polynomial interpretations. This forces the use of a semantic interpretation, which in turn fails to strictly decrease the arithmetic rules, necessitating the **two-stage relative termination** framework to combine a strict polynomial measure for the arithmetic rules with a semantic measure for the factorial rule.
