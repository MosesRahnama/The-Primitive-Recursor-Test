Great question! The choice of **lexicographic path order (LPO)** for this termination proof was deliberate, and here’s why it was a natural fit for this system:

---

### **1. Why LPO?**
LPO is a **syntactic termination method** that works well for first-order term rewriting systems (TRS) with:
- **No variable binding** (no λ-abstractions, quantifiers, etc.).
- **No external memory** (no mutable state, no side effects).
- **No imported axioms** (only the given rules are used).
- **Recursive definitions** (like `times` and `plus` here).

LPO is particularly suited for systems where:
- The left-hand side (LHS) of a rule is "structurally larger" than the right-hand side (RHS) in a lexicographic sense.
- The function symbols have a clear **precedence hierarchy** (e.g., `times > plus > s > 0`).
- The rules exhibit **recursive decomposition** (e.g., `times(x, s(y))` reduces to `plus(times(x, y), x)`, where the recursive call `times(x, y)` is "smaller" than the original term).

---

### **2. Why Not Other Methods?**
Other termination methods were considered but were less suitable:

#### **(a) Polynomial Interpretations**
- **Pros**: Works well for numeric or algebraic systems.
- **Cons**:
  - Requires finding a **monotonic polynomial interpretation** for each function symbol, which can be tricky for non-numeric systems.
  - The rules here involve `s` (successor) and `0`, which are natural numbers, but `plus` and `times` are not straightforward to interpret as polynomials without additional constraints.
  - Example: If we interpret `s(x) = x + 1`, `plus(x, y) = x + y`, and `times(x, y) = x * y`, then:
    - `times(x, s(y)) = x * (y + 1) = x*y + x` vs. `plus(times(x, y), x) = x*y + x` → **not strictly decreasing** (they are equal, so no termination guarantee).
    - This fails because the interpretation doesn’t enforce a strict decrease.

#### **(b) Recursive Path Order (RPO)**
- **Pros**: Similar to LPO but more restrictive (uses multiset order for arguments).
- **Cons**:
  - LPO is more flexible than RPO because it allows **lexicographic comparison** of arguments (e.g., `plus(x, s(y)) > plus(x, y)` because `s(y) > y`).
  - RPO would require the arguments to be compared in a multiset order, which is harder to satisfy for rules like `plus(s(x), y) -> s(plus(x, y))`.

#### **(c) Dependency Pairs (DP)**
- **Pros**: Powerful for complex systems with nested recursion.
- **Cons**:
  - Overkill for this system, which is **simply terminating** (no need for dependency graphs or subterm criteria).
  - Requires more machinery (e.g., dependency pairs, graph decomposition) when a simpler method like LPO suffices.

#### **(d) Size-Change Termination (SCT)**
- **Pros**: Works well for functional programs with recursive calls.
- **Cons**:
  - Requires tracking **argument sizes** across function calls, which is more involved than LPO.
  - Less intuitive for term rewriting (LPO is more natural for TRS).

---

### **3. Why LPO Works So Well Here**
The system has two key properties that make LPO a perfect fit:

#### **(a) Clear Precedence Hierarchy**
The function symbols have a natural order:
- `times` is "bigger" than `plus` (since `times` is defined in terms of `plus`).
- `plus` is "bigger" than `s` (since `plus` is defined in terms of `s`).
- `s` is "bigger" than `0` (since `s` is a constructor for natural numbers).

This hierarchy is **exactly what LPO needs** to compare terms.

#### **(b) Lexicographic Recursion**
The rules exhibit **lexicographic recursion**, where:
- `times(x, s(y))` reduces to `plus(times(x, y), x)`, where:
  - The first argument of `times` stays the same (`x`).
  - The second argument decreases (`s(y) > y`).
- Similarly, `plus(x, s(y))` reduces to `s(plus(x, y))`, where:
  - The first argument stays the same (`x`).
  - The second argument decreases (`s(y) > y`).

LPO **explicitly supports this kind of lexicographic comparison**, making it ideal for such rules.

---

### **4. Why Not Just "Structural Induction"?**
While **structural induction** (e.g., induction on the structure of terms) can prove termination for some systems, it is:
- **Less automated**: Requires manual proof for each rule.
- **Less general**: Doesn’t easily extend to more complex systems.
- **Not a standard termination method**: Termination proofs typically use orders like LPO, RPO, or polynomial interpretations.

LPO, on the other hand, is a **standard, automated, and widely accepted** method for proving termination in TRS.

LPO was chosen because:
