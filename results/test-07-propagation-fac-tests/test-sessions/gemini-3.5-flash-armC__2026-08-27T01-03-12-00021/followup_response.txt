I chose the **Dependency Pair (DP) Framework** combined with a **Polynomial Interpretation** for several specific reasons, as simpler standard techniques (like Lexicographic Path Order) fail or become extremely complicated on this specific system. 

Here is the breakdown of why this method was chosen:

### 1. Simple Syntactic Orders (like LPO or RPO) Fail Here
Standard path orderings like LPO (Lexicographic Path Order) or RPO (Recursive Path Order) are the first things one might try, but they cannot handle the `fac` rule:
$$\text{fac}(s(x)) \to \text{times}(\text{fac}(\mathbf{p}(s(x))), s(x))$$

For LPO to orient this rule, the argument of the recursive call, $\mathbf{p}(s(x))$, must be strictly smaller than the incoming argument, $s(x)$. 
However, $\text{p}$ is a *defined function symbol*, not a constructor. Under standard LPO, a term starting with a defined symbol $\text{p}$ cannot be easily proven smaller than $s(x)$ because the rules for $\text{p}$ define its behavior, which LPO's purely syntactic precedence cannot capture. This is a classic bottleneck in termination proofs.

### 2. The DP Framework Deconstructs the Problem (Modularity)
The Dependency Pair framework is the modern standard for termination because it modularizes the proof. 
* Instead of proving that the entire right-hand side of a rule is smaller than the left-hand side, it isolates the **recursive calls** (the "dependency pairs").
* This allowed us to ignore the outer `times(...)` constructor in the `fac` rule and focus solely on the transition:
  $$\text{FAC}(s(x)) \to \text{FAC}(p(s(x)))$$
* It also allows us to ignore "unrelated" rules. For the `FAC` dependency pair, we only need to consider the "usable rules" (the rules for `p`), completely ignoring the rules for `times` and `plus`, which vastly simplifies the proof.

### 3. Polynomial Interpretations Handle "Semantic" Decreases
The predecessor function `p` decreases its argument by one. Syntactic orders struggle with this, but **Polynomial Interpretations** excel at it because they map symbolic terms to actual numbers.
By defining $[p](X) = X - 1$ and $[s](X) = X + 2$, we easily map the symbol $p$ to a well-behaved mathematical subtraction. This makes proving that the argument to $\text{FAC}$ decreases (from $x+2$ to $x+1$) trivial.

### Summary
The method was chosen because **it is the most direct tool capable of handling defined functions inside recursive arguments**. It bypasses the limitations of purely syntactic path orderings by combining semantic interpretation (polynomials) with structural modularity (Dependency Pairs).
