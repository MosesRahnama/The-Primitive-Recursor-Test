I chose the **Dependency Pair (DP) framework combined with a monotone algebra (polynomial interpretation)** because standard, simpler termination techniques fundamentally fail on this specific rewrite system. 

Here is the breakdown of why alternative methods were rejected and why Dependency Pairs were necessary:

### 1. Knuth-Bendix Ordering (KBO) fails due to the variable condition
KBO requires that for every rule $l \to r$, the number of occurrences of any variable in $r$ must be less than or equal to its occurrences in $l$. 
* Look at the factorial rule: `fac(s(x)) -> times(fac(p(s(x))), s(x))`
* The variable `x` appears **once** on the left-hand side, but **twice** on the right-hand side (once inside `p(s(x))` and once in `s(x)`). 
* Because of this, KBO is immediately ruled out.

### 2. Lexicographic Path Ordering (LPO/RPO) fails due to the subterm condition
Simplification orderings like LPO and RPO rely on the property that a term is strictly greater than its proper subterms. To orient a recursive rule like $f(\dots) \to \dots f(t) \dots$, LPO requires the recursive argument $t$ to be a strict subterm of the left-hand side arguments.
* In the factorial rule, the recursive call is `fac(p(s(x)))`. 
* The argument `p(s(x))` is **not** a syntactic subterm of `s(x)`. 
* Therefore, LPO/RPO cannot orient this rule directly.

### 3. Direct Polynomial Interpretations fail due to the multiplicative context
If we try to prove termination by applying a polynomial interpretation directly to the original rules (without Dependency Pairs), we must find an interpretation where the left-hand side is strictly greater than the right-hand side for all rules.
* For the factorial rule, we would need: $[\text{fac}](x+1) > [\text{times}]( [\text{fac}](x), x+1 )$.
* Because `times` represents multiplication, its interpretation must grow multiplicatively (e.g., $[\text{times}](u, v) = u \cdot v$). 
* This forces the inequality $F(x+1) > F(x) \cdot (x+1)$. No polynomial $F(x)$ can satisfy this for arbitrarily large $x$, because the right side grows asymptotically faster than the left side. Direct polynomial interpretation is mathematically impossible here.

### Why Dependency Pairs succeed
The Dependency Pair framework solves all of the above problems by **stripping away the non-recursive context**. 

Instead of orienting the full rule, DP isolates the recursive call into a dependency pair:
`FAC#(s(x)) -> FAC#(p(s(x)))`

1. **The `times` context is gone:** We no longer have to mathematically beat the multiplicative growth of `times`. We only need to prove that the argument to the recursive call is strictly decreasing.
2. **Trivial orientation:** With the context removed, a very simple polynomial interpretation easily orients the pair strictly:
   * $[s(x)] = x + 1$
   * $[p(x)] = x$ (or $\max(x-1, 0)$)
   * $[\text{FAC}\#(x)] = x$
   * This yields $x + 1 > x$, which is trivially true.
3. **Usable rules:** We only need to *weakly* orient ($\ge$) the usable rules for `p`, which is easily satisfied by the same interpretation.

**Summary:** 
I picked Dependency Pairs because it is the standard, most robust technique for systems where recursive calls are embedded inside multiplying contexts (`times`) and where the recursive argument is modified by a helper function (`p`) rather than being a direct syntactic subterm. Any state-of-the-art automated termination tool (like AProVE or TTT2) would immediately bypass KBO/LPO and jump to the DP framework for this exact reason.
