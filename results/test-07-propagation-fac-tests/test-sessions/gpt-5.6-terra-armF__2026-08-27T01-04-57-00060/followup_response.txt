I picked LPO because the system has a very characteristic recursive shape:

- `times` calls itself on a structurally smaller second argument, but produces a surrounding `plus`.
- `plus` calls itself on structurally smaller arguments, but produces a surrounding `s`.
- The “administrative” rules reduce directly to subterms.

A simple size measure is awkward here. For example,

```text
times(x, s(y)) -> plus(times(x, y), x)
```

does decrease the recursive `times` argument from `s(y)` to `y`, but it also duplicates `x` syntactically: one copy occurs inside `times(x,y)` and one occurs as the second argument of `plus`. Thus ordinary symbol-count or term-size measures need not decrease.

LPO handles exactly this situation without assigning arithmetic meanings to the symbols. Its two relevant mechanisms are:

1. **Precedence between defined symbols**

   ```text
   times > plus > s > 0
   ```

   This reflects that a `times` redex may unfold into a `plus`, and a `plus` redex may unfold into an `s`.

2. **Lexicographic decrease in recursive arguments**

   ```text
   times(x, s(y)) > times(x, y)
   plus(x, s(y))  > plus(x, y)
   plus(s(x), y)  > plus(x, y)
   ```

   because `s(y) > y` and `s(x) > x` by the subterm property.

That combination proves decrease even where a naïve numeric size measure may fail.

LPO was also appropriate for the stated boundary because it is purely syntactic: it uses only the term constructors, the variables, the six rules, and a chosen symbol precedence/status. It does not assume that `0`, `s`, `plus`, or `times` denote natural-number operations, and it does not rely on associativity, commutativity, distributivity, or any unlisted equation.

A polynomial interpretation or a more specialized multiset/lexicographic measure could also be attempted, but LPO is the direct standard method for this sort of constructor recursion with nested calls under lower-precedence symbols.
