Yes. The proposed LPO proof complies with those requirements.

The precedence

\[
\texttt{times}\succ\texttt{plus}\succ\texttt{s}\succ\texttt{0}
\]

and lexicographic status are **meta-level components of a termination ordering**, not added object-level axioms or rewrite rules. They do not assert that `times`, `plus`, or `s` have any arithmetic properties, nor do they alter which rewrites are possible.

The proof relies only on:

- the syntactic tree structure of ordinary finite first-order terms;
- the proper-subterm relation;
- the root symbols and argument positions visible in the rules;
- the standard theorem that LPO is well-founded and closed under contexts and substitutions.

It does **not** assume:

- associativity or commutativity of `plus` or `times`;
- arithmetic interpretations of `0` and `s`;
- constructor freeness, injectivity, or disjointness;
- typing or restrictions on substitutions;
- extra guards, side conditions, equations, or rewrite rules.

Choosing a precedence is legitimate under the permission to use a standard proof method. It is analogous to choosing coefficients in a polynomial interpretation: the chosen data certify that existing rewrite steps decrease, but do not strengthen the TRS.

The only background convention is that “first-order term rewriting system” means rewriting over finite first-order terms. That is part of the stated syntactic framework, not an imported object-level assumption. Under that standard meaning, the proof is compliant.
