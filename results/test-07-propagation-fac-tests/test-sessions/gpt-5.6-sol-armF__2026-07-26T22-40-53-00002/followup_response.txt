I picked **lexicographic path ordering (LPO)** because the rules have a clear syntactic recursive structure that LPO captures directly:

- Recursive calls keep the same root symbol while replacing `s(y)` by `y` or `s(x)` by `x`.
- Other rules replace a term rooted by `times` with one rooted by `plus`, or a term rooted by `plus` with one rooted by `s`.
- Some right-hand sides are larger by ordinary symbol count, so a simple term-size argument would not work. For example:
  \[
  \texttt{times}(x,\texttt{s}(y))
  \to \texttt{plus}(\texttt{times}(x,y),x).
  \]

The precedence

\[
\texttt{times}\succ\texttt{plus}\succ\texttt{s}\succ\texttt{0}
\]

matches those root-symbol transitions, while lexicographic comparison recognizes the recursive decreases:

\[
\texttt{s}(y)\succ y,\qquad \texttt{s}(x)\succ x.
\]

LPO was also a good fit for the constraints because it is a standard **syntactic reduction order**:

- it is well-founded on finite first-order terms;
- it is closed under substitutions and contexts, so orienting the rules proves termination of arbitrary rewrite sequences, not merely root rewriting;
- it needs no interpretation of the symbols as arithmetic operations;
- it does not assume induction principles, constructor freeness, associativity, commutativity, or extra equations.

A polynomial interpretation or a custom multicomponent measure might also prove termination, but would require more parameter selection or bookkeeping. LPO gives a short, uniform orientation of every rule using exactly the recursive shape already visible in the rules.
