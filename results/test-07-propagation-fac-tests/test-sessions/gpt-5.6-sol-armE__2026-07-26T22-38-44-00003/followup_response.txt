I picked **lexicographic path ordering (LPO)** because the second rule has two features that LPO handles directly:

\[
F(x,y,S(n)) \to G(y,F(x,y,n)).
\]

1. **The recursive call decreases structurally.**  
   The inner call changes from \(F(x,y,S(n))\) to \(F(x,y,n)\). LPO’s subterm property gives \(S(n)>n\), and lexicographic status for \(F\) lifts that to
   \[
   F(x,y,S(n))>F(x,y,n).
   \]

2. **The right-hand side adds a surrounding symbol \(G\).**  
   A simple measure such as term size does not work: the right-hand side is larger than the left. LPO can nevertheless orient the rule by choosing \(F\succ G\), provided the left-hand side also dominates \(G\)’s arguments—which it does.

The first rule is then immediate from the subterm property:

\[
F(x,y,Z)>x.
\]

So LPO gives a short, standard, purely syntactic proof covering arbitrary terms, substitutions, and rewrite positions. It does not rely on interpreting \(S\) as a natural-number successor or \(Z\) as zero.

It was not the only possible method. For example, a suitable polynomial interpretation could also work, but it would require constructing and checking numerical interpretations for every symbol. LPO matches the visible recursive structure more naturally and with fewer auxiliary choices.
