I chose the **Lexicographic Path Ordering (LPO)** for three main reasons: it perfectly matches the structural hierarchy of your rules, it is purely syntactic (requiring zero external assumptions), and it is mathematically robust. 

Here is a detailed breakdown of why LPO was the most suitable tool for this specific system:

### 1. The Rules Have a Clear Symbol Hierarchy (Precedence)
In term rewriting, if one operation is defined in terms of another, there is a natural hierarchy. Looking at your rules, we can observe a clear "dependency" chain:
* `times` is defined using `plus` (Rule 2: $\text{times}(x, s(y)) \to \text{plus}(\dots)$).
* `plus` is defined using `s` (Rules 5 & 6: $\text{plus} \to s(\text{plus}(\dots))$).
* `s` wraps around variables and eventually reduces to the constant `0`.

LPO is designed specifically to exploit this kind of hierarchical structure. By setting the operator precedence to reflect this hierarchy ($\text{times} \succ \text{plus} \succ \text{s} \succ 0$), the proof practically writes itself because the "bigger" operations are naturally allowed to decompose into "smaller" ones.

### 2. Strict Compliance with the "No External Assumptions" Constraint
Other common termination proof methods, such as **Polynomial Interpretations**, require mapping terms to a semantic domain (usually the natural numbers $\mathbb{N}$) and proving that every rewrite step strictly decreases the mapped value. 

While highly effective, polynomial interpretations require:
* Importing the algebraic axioms of $\mathbb{N}$ (addition, multiplication, and the well-foundedness of the standard $>$ relation on $\mathbb{N}$).
* Ensuring the interpretation functions are monotonic and well-defined.

Because your prompt explicitly demanded a proof **without importing structural axioms or object-level assumptions**, LPO was the safest choice. LPO is a **purely syntactic** relation defined directly on the structure of the terms themselves. It relies only on the induction of the term structure, making it 100% self-contained within the syntax of your provided system.

### 3. Lexicographic Status Handles Argument Shifts Naturally
In rules like:
* $\text{plus}(\text{s}(x), y) \to \text{s}(\text{plus}(x, y))$
* $\text{times}(x, \text{s}(y)) \to \text{plus}(\text{times}(x, y), x)$

The recursive calls are evaluated by decreasing one of the arguments while keeping the other the same, or shifting information. 
* By assigning a **left-to-right lexicographic status** to the operators, LPO can compare arguments one-by-one. 
* For $\text{plus}(\text{s}(x), y)$, the first argument strictly decreases ($\text{s}(x) \succ x$), which immediately licenses the rewrite step regardless of what happens to the second argument.

### Summary
LPO is the most elegant and minimal tool for this TRS because it **turns the structural definition of your functions into the proof of their termination**, requiring no math beyond the syntax of the terms themselves.
