Great question! The choice of method for assessing termination here was guided by the **structure of the rewrite system** and the **types of rules** it contains. Here’s why I picked the approach I did:

---

### **1. Why Not More Formal Methods (e.g., Dependency Pairs, Matrix Interpretations)?**
While powerful, formal termination-proving methods like **dependency pairs (DP)**, **matrix interpretations**, or **polynomial orderings** are often overkill for small, constructor-based systems like this one. They require:
   - More setup (e.g., defining a precedence or matrix weights).
   - More manual effort (e.g., checking compatibility with all rules).
   - More machinery (e.g., DP graphs, usable rules).

Since this system is **small and syntactically simple**, a lightweight approach suffices.

---

### **2. Why Recursive Path Ordering (RPO) or Lexicographic Path Ordering (LPO)?**
The system’s rules resemble **primitive recursive definitions** (like Peano arithmetic), which are known to terminate under **RPO/LPO** with a natural precedence. Specifically:
   - The rules for `plus`, `times`, and `p` are **structurally recursive** (recursive calls are on "smaller" subterms).
   - The `fac` rule is **nested recursive** (it calls `p` on its argument before recursing), but `p` is already known to terminate and reduce the argument.

**RPO/LPO works well here because:**
   - The system is **constructor-based** (no non-constructor symbols on the right-hand side that aren’t on the left).
   - The recursive calls are **strictly decreasing** in a lexicographic or subterm order.
   - The precedence `fac > times > plus > s > p > 0` (or similar) can be used to orient all rules.

---

### **3. Why Not Just "Term Size" or "Subterm Order"?**
While **term size** (number of symbols) or **subterm order** (a term is bigger than its subterms) can prove termination for `plus` and `times`, they fail for `fac`:
   - In `fac(s(x)) → times(fac(p(s(x))), s(x))`, the right-hand side is **larger** than the left-hand side in term size (because `times` and `s(x)` add symbols).
   - However, the **recursive call to `fac` is on a smaller term** (`p(s(x))` is smaller than `s(x)`), so a **lexicographic order** (where we compare the first argument of `fac` first) works.

Thus, a **combination of subterm and lexicographic order** (i.e., RPO/LPO) is needed.

---

### **4. Why Not Knuth-Bendix Ordering (KBO)?**
KBO is another strong method, but it requires:
   - Assigning weights to symbols (e.g., `s` and `p` must have weights that balance out).
   - Ensuring that variables on the right-hand side are "covered" by the left-hand side.

While KBO could work here, it’s **more tedious** to set up than RPO/LPO for this case, where the precedence is obvious.

---

### **5. Why Not Just "Intuition"?**
While intuition is useful, it’s not rigorous. For example:
   - One might think `fac` terminates because `p` reduces its argument, but this ignores that `times` could grow the term.
   - A formal order (like RPO) **proves** that the recursive call is on a smaller term in a well-founded sense.

Thus, I relied on **RPO/LPO as a lightweight but rigorous method** that matches the system’s structure.
