I chose the **Dependency Pair (DP) framework combined with a polynomial interpretation** because it is mathematically necessary for this specific set of rules, while also perfectly satisfying your strict constraint of not importing structural axioms or object-level assumptions. 

Here is the step-by-step breakdown of exactly why this method was chosen over others:

### 1. Basic methods (Simplification Orderings) mathematically fail here
The most common tools to prove termination in term rewriting are simplification orderings, like the Lexicographic Path Ordering (LPO) or Knuth-Bendix Ordering (KBO). These orderings possess the **subterm property**, meaning a term is always strictly greater than any of its subterms. 

Look at your `fac` rule:
`fac(s(x)) -> times(fac(p(s(x))), s(x))`

Because `s(x)` is a subterm of `p(s(x))`, any simplification ordering will conclude that `p(s(x)) ≥ s(x)`. If we apply the `fac` function to both sides, the ordering dictates that `fac(p(s(x))) ≥ fac(s(x))`. 
Because the right-hand side of a rule must be strictly *smaller* than the left-hand side to prove termination, **no simplification ordering can ever orient this rule.**

### 2. Dependency Pairs focus only on what causes non-termination
Since the `fac` rule gets "larger" on the right side by wrapping the argument in `p(...)`, standard orderings get confused. The Dependency Pair framework (introduced by Arts & Giesl in 1997 specifically to solve this exact problem) isolates the *recursive calls* from the rest of the term.

Instead of trying to prove that the entire right-hand side is smaller, DP says we only need to look at the recursive function calls. For `fac`, the only DP is:
`FAC(s(x)) -> FAC(p(s(x)))`

### 3. Polynomial Interpretations provide the final proof without structural induction
Once we have the Dependency Pair, we just need to prove that the argument strictly decreases: `s(x) > p(s(x))`. 

We cannot do this using syntax alone (for the subterm reasons above), but we can do it by mapping the function symbols to simple math functions (a polynomial interpretation) in the **metatheory**:
*   Let $[s](x) = x + 1$
*   Let $[p](x) = \max(0, x - 1)$

Now evaluate the DP:
*   Left side argument: $[s](x) = x + 1$
*   Right side argument: $[p]([s](x)) = \max(0, (x + 1) - 1) = x$

Since $x + 1 > x$ for all numbers, the strict decrease is proven. We also check that the other rules for `p` don't increase the weight (e.g., `p(s(s(x))) -> s(p(s(x)))` maps to $x+1 \ge x+1$, which is valid since basic rules only require $\ge$). 

### 4. It respects your strict constraints
Your prompt explicitly forbade "importing structural axioms or object-level assumptions." 
If I had said, "We know $x$ is a natural number, so $p(s(x))$ evaluates to $x$," I would have violated your constraints by assuming structural induction (that variables are built only from `0` and `s`) or domain-specific logic. 

The DP framework + polynomial interpretations is a pure metatheoretical tool. It assigns algebraic weights to the *syntax tree* of the terms. It proves that no matter what crazy, ill-formed, or open terms you feed into the system (e.g., `fac(s(times(x, y)))`), the system will eventually halt, using *only* the rewrite rules provided.
