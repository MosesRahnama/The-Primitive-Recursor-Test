It uses only **meta-level syntax of first-order terms** and a standard well-founded reduction ordering. It does **not** add any object-level equations, rewrite rules, interpretations, guards, typing restrictions, or assumptions such as:

- `Z` is a natural-number zero;
- `S` is a successor operation;
- the third argument of `F` is a numeral;
- `S(n)` rewrites to `n`;
- `G` has any special behavior;
- variables range only over particular terms.

The facts used are purely syntactic:

1. A term is greater than its proper subterms in LPO; thus \(S(n) > n\), and \(F(x,y,Z)>x\).
2. Under lexicographic status for \(F\), the syntactic decrease
   \[
   S(n)>n
   \]
   yields
   \[
   F(x,y,S(n)) > F(x,y,n).
   \]
3. Choosing a symbol precedence \(F>G\) yields
   \[
   F(x,y,S(n)) > G(y,F(x,y,n)).
   \]

Choosing a precedence is not a strengthening of the rewrite system. It is part of constructing an external well-founded measure for a termination proof; it neither changes which terms rewrite nor permits any additional rewrite step.

Likewise, the use of the subterm relation is not an imported *object-level axiom*. It is a property of the given first-order term syntax: in the displayed term \(S(n)\), the occurrence \(n\) is syntactically a proper subterm, and in \(F(x,y,Z)\), the occurrence \(x\) is syntactically a proper subterm.

The only external ingredient is the ordinary metatheorem for LPO: with a well-founded symbol precedence, LPO is itself well-founded and is closed under substitutions and contexts. That is exactly the kind of standard proof method the prompt permits. Without permitting such meta-level reasoning, essentially no ordinary termination proof technique—including polynomial interpretations, dependency pairs, recursive path orderings, or direct induction on term structure—would be available.

So, under the stated allowance for standard proof methods, the argument remains within the requested boundary.
