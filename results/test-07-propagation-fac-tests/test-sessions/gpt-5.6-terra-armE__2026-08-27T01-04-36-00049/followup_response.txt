I picked lexicographic path ordering because the recursive rule has exactly the shape it is designed to handle:

\[
F(x,y,S(n)) \to G(y,F(x,y,n)).
\]

Although the right-hand side introduces an outer `G` and still contains an `F`, the recursive `F` call is on the syntactically smaller third argument `n` rather than `S(n)`:

\[
S(n) \;>\; n
\]

by the ordinary subterm relation. LPO can lift that decrease through the common root `F`:

\[
F(x,y,S(n)) \;>\; F(x,y,n).
\]

Then assigning the precedence

\[
F > G
\]

allows the whole left-hand side to dominate the newly created `G` term:

\[
F(x,y,S(n)) > G(y,F(x,y,n)).
\]

It is also convenient because the base rule

\[
F(x,y,Z) \to x
\]

is immediate: the right-hand side is literally a subterm of the left-hand side.

So LPO was chosen not because it imports arithmetic meaning for `S` and `Z`, but because it provides a purely syntactic, well-founded measure that captures two facts visible in the rules themselves:

1. Replacing `S(n)` by `n` decreases a term structurally.
2. The outer symbol changes from higher-precedence `F` to lower-precedence `G`.

A direct numerical “count successors” argument would be less robust over arbitrary first-order terms, since the syntax permits terms such as `S(G(...))`, variables instantiated by arbitrary terms, and arbitrary occurrences of `F` inside arguments. LPO handles all such terms uniformly without assuming that the third argument is a natural number.
