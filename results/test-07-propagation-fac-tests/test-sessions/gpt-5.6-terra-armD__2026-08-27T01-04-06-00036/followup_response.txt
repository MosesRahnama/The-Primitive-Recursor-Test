I picked LPO because the rules have a strong **syntactic dependency hierarchy** that LPO captures directly:

\[
\mathit{times} > \mathit{plus} > p > s > 0.
\]

The key features are:

- `times` reduces to an expression rooted by `plus`;
- `plus` reduces to an expression rooted by `s`;
- `p` reduces to an expression rooted by `s`;
- recursive calls such as `plus(x,y)`, `times(x,y)`, and `p(s(x))` have syntactically smaller arguments than the corresponding left-hand sides.

That is precisely the sort of situation for which a recursive path ordering is designed.

In particular, the potentially concerning rule is

```text
times(s(x), y) -> plus(times(x, y), y)
```

because its right-hand side contains both a recursive `times` call and another occurrence of `y`; so a simple “number of symbols” measure does not obviously decrease. LPO handles this without needing to invent a numeric interpretation: the left-hand side is greater than `times(x,y)` because `s(x) > x`, and it is greater than `y` because `y` is a subterm of the left-hand side. Since `times > plus`, it follows that the whole left-hand side exceeds the `plus(...)` term.

Likewise, for

```text
p(s(s(x))) -> s(p(s(x)))
```

the apparent growth in the right side—the new outer `s`—is harmless under LPO because the recursive call changes from `p(s(s(x)))` to `p(s(x))`, whose argument is syntactically smaller, and `p > s`.

So LPO was selected because it gives a uniform, purely syntactic proof for all seven rules, including the ones that duplicate variables or wrap recursive calls in constructors. It does not rely on interpreting the symbols as natural-number addition, multiplication, successor, or predecessor, and it adds no assumptions to the rewrite system.
