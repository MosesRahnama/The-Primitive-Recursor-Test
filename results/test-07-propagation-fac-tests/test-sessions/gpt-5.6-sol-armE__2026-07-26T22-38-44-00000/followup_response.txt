I picked **lexicographic path ordering (LPO)** because the second rule has two competing features that LPO handles directly:

\[
F(x,y,S(n))\to G(y,F(x,y,n)).
\]

1. **The recursive call decreases structurally.**  
   The inner call changes from \(F(x,y,S(n))\) to \(F(x,y,n)\). LPO captures this through the subterm fact \(S(n)>n\), then compares the arguments of the two \(F\)-terms lexicographically.

2. **The right-hand side is larger in raw syntax.**  
   It introduces a new outer \(G\), so simple measures such as term size or symbol count do not decrease. Assigning the precedence \(F\succ G\) lets LPO show that the whole left-hand \(F\)-term dominates the newly introduced \(G\)-term.

3. **The base rule is immediate.**  
   In
   \[
   F(x,y,Z)\to x,
   \]
   the right-hand side is a proper subterm of the left, which every simplification ordering handles.

4. **It requires no semantic assumptions about constructors.**  
   The proof does not assume that every term is a natural number, that \(S\) is injective, that \(Z\) and \(S\) are disjoint, or that the third argument has any special meaning. It uses only finite term syntax and the displayed rules.

5. **It automatically lifts to arbitrary rewrite positions and substitutions.**  
   LPO is well-founded, substitution-stable, and context-monotone, exactly the properties needed to turn rule orientation into termination of the entire rewrite relation.

A custom measure could also work—for example, an interpretation or a carefully designed recursive ranking—but LPO is the most direct standard tool here because it simultaneously represents the structural decrease in \(S(n)\to n\) and the root-symbol change \(F\to G\).
