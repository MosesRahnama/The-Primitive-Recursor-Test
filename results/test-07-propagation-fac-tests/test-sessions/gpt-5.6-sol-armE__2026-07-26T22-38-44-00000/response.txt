**Verdict: Yes.** Termination follows syntactically from the two rules alone.

Use the **lexicographic path ordering (LPO)** with a precedence containing

\[
F \succ G
\]

(the relative precedence of \(S\) and \(Z\) is immaterial), and give \(F\) lexicographic status.

Let \(>\) denote this LPO.

### First rule

\[
F(x,y,Z)\to x
\]

The left-hand side is greater than \(x\) by the strict subterm property:

\[
F(x,y,Z)>x.
\]

### Second rule

Let

\[
L=F(x,y,S(n)),\qquad B=F(x,y,n).
\]

First,

\[
L>B.
\]

Both terms have root \(F\), and their argument lists agree in the first two positions. At the third position,

\[
S(n)>n
\]

by the strict subterm property. Hence the lexicographic comparison gives

\[
F(x,y,S(n))>F(x,y,n).
\]

Also,

\[
L>y
\]

because \(y\) is a proper subterm of \(L\). Since \(F\succ G\) and \(L\) is greater than both arguments of the right-hand-side \(G\), LPO gives

\[
F(x,y,S(n))>G\bigl(y,F(x,y,n)\bigr).
\]

Thus both rewrite rules strictly decrease a well-founded simplification ordering. LPO is stable under substitutions and closed under contexts, so rewriting inside arbitrary surrounding terms also strictly decreases the ordering.

Therefore, there can be no infinite rewrite sequence: **the system is terminating on all finite first-order terms**, without any additional axioms, guards, side conditions, or assumptions about \(Z\), \(S\), \(F\), or \(G\).
