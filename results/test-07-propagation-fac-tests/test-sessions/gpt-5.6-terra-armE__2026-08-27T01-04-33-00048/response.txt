**Verdict: yes.** Termination is provable from the two presented rewrite rules alone, for ordinary finite first-order terms. No algebraic properties, equations, guards, or assumptions about `G`, `S`, or `Z` are needed.

Use a recursive path ordering (RPO), with:

- precedence \(F \succ G\);
- lexicographic status for \(F\);
- the ordinary subterm property of RPO.

(The relative precedence of `S` and `Z` is irrelevant.)

The rules are:

1. \(F(x,y,Z) \to x\)
2. \(F(x,y,S(n)) \to G(y,F(x,y,n))\)

For rule 1:

\[
F(x,y,Z) \succ x
\]

because \(x\) is a proper subterm of \(F(x,y,Z)\). Thus this rule strictly decreases in RPO.

For rule 2, first compare the left-hand side with the inner `F` term:

\[
F(x,y,S(n)) \succ F(x,y,n).
\]

Both terms have root symbol \(F\). Under lexicographic status, their first two arguments are identical, and the third arguments satisfy

\[
S(n) \succ n
\]

by the subterm property. Hence the whole left side is greater than the inner `F` term.

Also,

\[
F(x,y,S(n)) \succ y
\]

again by the subterm property.

Since \(F \succ G\), and the left side is greater than each argument of the right-hand-side `G` term, RPO gives

\[
F(x,y,S(n))
\succ
G(y,F(x,y,n)).
\]

Thus rule 2 also strictly decreases.

RPO is well-founded and stable under substitutions and contexts, so every rewrite step decreases this ordering. Therefore no infinite rewrite sequence exists.

This covers every rule. In particular, although the second rule retains an `F` occurrence on its right-hand side, its third argument has changed from \(S(n)\) to \(n\), and the new surrounding `G` does not create any rewrite opportunity because there are no rules with root symbol `G`.

Hence the system is terminating as presented.
