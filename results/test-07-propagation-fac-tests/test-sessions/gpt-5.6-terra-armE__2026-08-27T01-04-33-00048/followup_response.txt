I picked recursive path ordering because the rules have the standard shape it handles well:

- A rule may replace an outer symbol by a lower-precedence symbol:
  \[
  F(\ldots) \to G(\ldots)
  \]
  so setting \(F \succ G\) accounts for that outer-symbol change.

- The recursive `F` call in the second rule has a visibly smaller argument:
  \[
  F(x,y,S(n)) \to G(y,F(x,y,n)).
  \]
  The recursive occurrence changes \(S(n)\) to its proper subterm \(n\).

- The first rule simply returns one of its arguments:
  \[
  F(x,y,Z) \to x,
  \]
  which is directly decreasing by the subterm property.

RPO combines exactly these two facts: a term is larger than its proper subterms, and a term rooted by a higher-precedence symbol can dominate one rooted by a lower-precedence symbol when it also dominates that lower symbol’s arguments.

It was also appropriate to the boundary in the question because it is a standard syntactic termination ordering. It does not need any interpretation of `Z` as zero, `S` as successor, `F` as recursion over naturals, or `G` as any particular operation. The proof only uses the displayed term structure and the two rules.
