I chose **dependency pairs with a weak semantic interpretation** because the system mixes two features that make simpler termination arguments awkward:

1. **Several rules increase term size.** For example,
   \[
   \mathrm{times}(s(x),y)\to
   \mathrm{plus}(\mathrm{times}(x,y),y)
   \]
   and the factorial rule both produce larger terms. So raw term size, symbol count, or a simple subterm argument will not work.

2. **The factorial call is not syntactically on a subterm.**
   \[
   \mathrm{fac}(s(x))\to
   \mathrm{times}(\mathrm{fac}(p(s(x))),s(x)).
   \]
   The new argument \(p(s(x))\) is not a proper subterm of \(s(x)\). One therefore cannot simply say “factorial recurses on a smaller natural number,” especially because the requested proof must cover arbitrary first-order terms rather than silently assume that \(x\) denotes a numeral.

Dependency pairs are designed for exactly this situation: they separate the potentially recursive calls from the surrounding computation. After that separation, the recursive decreases become simple:

- `plus` removes one outer \(s\) from its second argument;
- `times` removes one outer \(s\) from its first argument;
- `p` changes \(s(s(x))\) to a call on \(s(x)\);
- `fac` changes a call on \(s(x)\) to one on \(p(s(x))\).

The interpretation
\[
[s(t)]=[t]+1,\qquad [p(t)]=\max([t]-1,0)
\]
then makes the last decrease precise for **all substitutions**, not merely for numeral substitutions:

\[
[s(x)]=[x]+1>[x]=[p(s(x))].
\]

At the same time, the ordinary rewrite rules are value-preserving under the interpretation. That matters because reductions may occur between dependency-pair steps; those intervening reductions cannot restore the amount that the recursive call decreased.

I also chose this method because the dependency graph has a particularly transparent shape. The only cycles are the four direct self-recursions. Calls from `fac` to `times` or `p`, and from `times` to `plus`, never lead back. Thus there is no complicated mutual recursion to rank globally.

In short, this method was selected because it:

- handles arbitrary, including non-ground and non-constructor, terms;
- does not rely on term size;
- does not assume an external natural-number theory;
- isolates every possible recursive cycle;
- allows the computational rules to be weakly oriented while requiring strict decrease only at recursive calls.

A direct polynomial interpretation or recursive path ordering might also be attempted, but it would be much less natural here: the arithmetic expansion rules need not decrease globally, and the `fac` call through `p` obscures the necessary syntactic ordering. Dependency pairs expose exactly the decreases that matter.
