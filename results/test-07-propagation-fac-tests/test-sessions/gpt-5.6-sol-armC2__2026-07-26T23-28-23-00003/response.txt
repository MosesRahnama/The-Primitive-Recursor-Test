**Yes.** Under the usual semantics of rewriting over finite first-order terms, termination can be established from these rules alone.

A suitable dependency/size argument assigns a natural “successor value”:

- \(|0|=0\)
- \(|s(t)|=|t|+1\)
- \(|p(t)|=\max(|t|-1,0)\)
- \(|plus(a,b)|=|a|+|b|\)
- \(|times(a,b)|=|a|\cdot|b|\)

The rules preserve this value, while each recursive call decreases its controlling argument:

- `plus` decreases the second argument,
- `times` decreases the first,
- `p` decreases the successor depth beneath `p`,
- `fac(s(x))` calls `fac(p(s(x)))`, whose argument value is one less.

Calls from `fac` to `times`, and from `times` to `plus`, can be handled by a precedence between defined symbols. A standard dependency-pair or lexicographic/multiset termination proof can formalize this.

Crucially, no unlisted equation such as `p(s(x)) = x` is needed. When the displayed `p` rules cannot reduce a term, the corresponding `fac` recursion simply becomes stuck rather than looping. The missing `fac(0)` rule likewise creates normal forms, not nontermination.
