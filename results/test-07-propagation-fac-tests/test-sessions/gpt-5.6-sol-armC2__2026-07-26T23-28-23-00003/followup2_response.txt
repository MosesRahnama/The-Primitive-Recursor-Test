Yes—provided the natural-number assignment is understood as a **meta-level interpretation used to prove termination**, not as an added object-level axiom.

It does **not** assume equations such as

```text
p(s(x)) = x
```

nor does it add rules, guards, or restrictions on terms. Every finite term, including noncanonical terms such as `p(plus(...))`, is simply assigned a natural number structurally.

A more formal completion of the sketch is:

\[
\begin{aligned}
[0]&=0, & [s(t)]&=[t]+1,\\
[p(t)]&=\max([t]-1,0),&
[plus(a,b)]&=[a]+[b],\\
[times(a,b)]&=[a][b],&
[fac(t)]&=0.
\end{aligned}
\]

All original rewrite rules are weakly oriented—in fact, both sides have equal interpretation. Dependency-pair SCCs then isolate the recursive cycles:

- `PLUS(x,s(y)) → PLUS(x,y)`: second argument decreases.
- `TIMES(s(x),y) → TIMES(x,y)`: first argument decreases.
- `P(s(s(x))) → P(s(x))`: argument decreases.
- `FAC(s(x)) → FAC(p(s(x)))`: interpreted argument decreases because  
  \([s(x)]=[x]+1>[p(s(x))]=[x]\).

The other dependency edges go one way, such as `fac → times → plus`, and do not form additional cycles.

Thus the method relies only on syntactic dependency pairs and a well-founded meta-level order on natural numbers, both standard termination-proof devices. It does not strengthen the rewrite system. My original answer was compliant in substance, although it was a proof sketch and should have made this meta-level/object-level distinction—and the interpretation of `fac`—explicit.
