Good question — I listed three methods (LPO, polynomial interpretation, lexicographic combination) rather than committing to one, so let me explain why **LPO with the precedence `fac > times > plus > p > s > 0`** is what I'd actually reach for first, and what trade-offs the other methods carry.

**Why LPO with that precedence:**

1. **The precedence is essentially forced by the call structure.** Each rule's RHS calls "lower" functions in the precedence:
   - `fac(s(x)) → times(fac(p(s(x))), s(x))` ⇒ `fac > times, fac > p`
   - `times(s(x), y) → plus(times(x,y), y)` ⇒ `times > plus`
   - `plus(x, s(y)) → s(plus(x,y))` ⇒ `plus > s`
   - `p(s(s(x))) → s(p(s(x)))` ⇒ `p > s`
   - `s(...)` builds over `0` ⇒ `s > 0`
   
   The transitive closure is exactly `fac > times > plus > p > s > 0`. It's not a clever choice — any precedence that orients these rules strictly has to be a refinement of this partial order.

2. **The hard case — the `fac` rule — works out cleanly.** Under this precedence, LPO reduces the claim `fac(s(x)) > times(fac(p(s(x))), s(x))` to two sub-claims: the LHS exceeds each RHS argument. The second argument `s(x)` is a subterm of the LHS, and the first argument `fac(p(s(x)))` is handled because `p(s(x))` is smaller than `s(x)` (via `s > p` in the precedence). No measure, no interpretation, no induction needed.

3. **It's a self-contained, decidable check.** No model, no arithmetic, no matrix — just a syntactic orientation. For a system this small, the case analysis is short enough to do by hand and is easy to audit.

**Why I didn't lean on the other methods:**

- **Polynomial interpretation** is awkward here because of `p`. A natural linear interpretation like `s(x) = x+1, p(x) = x` makes the `p(s(s(x))) → s(p(s(x)))` rule collapse to equality rather than strict decrease, so it doesn't certify termination. You have to introduce a non-trivial (often quadratic) interpretation for `s` to make `p` strictly decrease, or switch to a matrix interpretation. That works, but it's more engineering for no real gain.

- **Lexicographic combination measure** (e.g., `(#s(t), |t|)`) is the classical "pre-LPO" trick. It works, but it requires inventing a measure and proving it's monotone under each rule, which is roughly the same effort as LPO but with less reusable machinery.

- **The dependency pair method** is the modern heavyweight and would isolate the `fac` recursion as a single DP `FAC(s(x)) → FAC(p(s(x)))` whose termination is a property of the `p`-subsystem alone. That's more modular and scales to much larger systems, but for a toy like this it's overkill.

So: LPO wins on **directness and auditability** for a system of this size. The precedence isn't something I engineered; it's read off the call graph.
