I picked LPO because the rules have a clear recursive-symbol hierarchy and their recursive calls are structurally smaller in the relevant argument:

\[
\mathsf{times} \succ \mathsf{plus} \succ \mathsf{p} \succ \mathsf{s} \succ 0.
\]

In particular:

- `plus(x,s(y))` calls `plus(x,y)`, decreasing the second argument.
- `times(s(x),y)` calls `times(x,y)`, decreasing the first argument; the surrounding call changes from `times` to lower-precedence `plus`.
- `p(s(s(x)))` calls `p(s(x))`, decreasing its sole argument; the surrounding root changes from `p` to lower-precedence `s`.

That is exactly the pattern LPO is designed to orient: recursive calls decrease lexicographically, and calls nested beneath a lower-precedence symbol remain smaller than the original left-hand side.

I also chose it because it stays strictly within the stated boundary:

- It is a syntactic, first-order termination ordering.
- It works on arbitrary terms, not merely intended numeral terms.
- It does not interpret `plus`, `times`, or `p` as arithmetic operations.
- It does not assume facts such as associativity, commutativity, freeness of constructors, induction over naturals, or a hidden “numeric value” measure.
- It requires no modification of the rewrite system.

Other approaches could work, but some natural-looking ones are less direct here. For example, a simple symbol-count or term-size measure fails for rules such as

\[
\mathsf{times}(\mathsf{s}(x),y)
\to
\mathsf{plus}(\mathsf{times}(x,y),y),
\]

because the right-hand side duplicates \(y\), so its ordinary syntactic size may be larger. A semantic natural-number interpretation would need carefully chosen interpretations and would be less plainly independent of object-level arithmetic assumptions. LPO gives a direct uniform proof for every rule.
