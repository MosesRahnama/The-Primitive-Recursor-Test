I chose the **lexicographic path order (LPO)** because the termination problem fits directly into what LPO can handle without needing any external interpretation or extra assumptions. The constraints in the prompt were:

- No variable binding, no external memory, no imported axioms.  
- Purely first‑order term rewriting with free variables.  
- The proof must use only the given rewrite rules, without strengthening them with guards, side conditions, or hidden assumptions.

LPO is a *simplification order* – it works entirely on the syntactic structure of terms. You only need to provide a precedence on the function symbols. The orientation of each rule is then decided by comparing subterms and applying the precedence. No interpretation into ℕ, no orderings on integers, and no external arithmetic facts are required.

The system itself consists of recursive definitions for `plus`, `times`, and a predecessor‑like `p`, all very common in natural‑number term rewriting. Rules like  

```
plus(x, 0) -> x
plus(x, s(y)) -> s(plus(x, y))
```

are archetypical examples that LPO disposes of easily. The same holds for the `times` rules and the two `p` rules. Indeed, simply ordering the symbols in a natural way (`times > plus > p > s > 0`) makes every left‑hand side strictly larger than its right‑hand side: arguments decrease through subterm or lexicographic comparisons.  

Other termination methods (e.g. polynomial interpretations, matrix interpretations, dependency pairs) would also work here, but many of them rely on interpreting symbols into some carrier set (like ℕ with addition/multiplication) or would require a separate treatment of dependency pairs. LPO remains entirely syntactic, uses no arithmetic outside the term structure, and its application is immediate.  

Thus, LPO was the simplest, most self‑contained method that fully respects the prompt’s boundary: no external memory, no arithmetic reasoning, no extra rewrite rules – just a precedence and the subterm property.
