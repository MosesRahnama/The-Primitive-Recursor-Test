I picked a dependency-pair argument with interpretations because the system is not uniformly decreasing under a simple term-size measure.

In particular:

- `plus` recurses on its second argument:
  ```text
  plus(x, s(y)) -> s(plus(x, y))
  ```
- `times` recurses on its first argument:
  ```text
  times(s(x), y) -> plus(times(x, y), y)
  ```
- `p` recurses by removing one outer `s`:
  ```text
  p(s(s(x))) -> s(p(s(x)))
  ```
- `fac` calls itself through `p`:
  ```text
  fac(s(x)) -> times(fac(p(s(x))), s(x))
  ```

A naïve measure such as total symbol count does not work. For example, the `times` rule can make the right-hand side syntactically larger, and the factorial rule introduces both `times` and another `s(x)` occurrence. So a direct “every rewrite reduces term size” proof is unavailable.

Dependency pairs isolate the only calls capable of sustaining infinite recursion. Once isolated, each recursive cycle has an evident decreasing argument:

- `plus`: second argument changes from `s(y)` to `y`;
- `times`: first argument changes from `s(x)` to `x`;
- `p`: argument changes from `s(s(x))` to `s(x)`;
- `fac`: recursive argument changes from `s(x)` to `p(s(x))`.

The interpretation makes the last point formal without assuming any object-level property of `p`: the rewrite rules themselves are compatible with interpreting

\[
p(n)=\max(n-1,0).
\]

Then, in particular,

\[
p(s(x))
\]

has interpretation \(x\), whereas \(s(x)\) has interpretation \(x+1\). Thus the recursive factorial call is strictly decreasing in the meta-level proof ordering.

The ordinary rules are only required to be weakly decreasing so that reductions between recursive calls cannot invalidate the measure. The dependency pairs for recursive calls are required to decrease strictly. This division is exactly what makes dependency pairs a natural fit: it handles rules that expand or rearrange terms while proving that no recursive cycle can continue forever.
