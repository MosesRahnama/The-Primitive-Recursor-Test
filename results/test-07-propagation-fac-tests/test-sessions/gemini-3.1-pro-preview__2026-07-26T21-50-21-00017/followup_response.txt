I chose the **Dependency Pair (DP) Framework paired with a semantic interpretation (monotonic algebra over $\mathbb{N}$)** because the standard, fully automated syntactic methods (like LPO, KBO, or simple Polynomial Interpretations) inherently fail on this specific set of rules. 

Here is the breakdown of the specific hurdles in your system and why this method was necessary to overcome them:

### 1. Why Syntactic Orderings (LPO / MPO) Fail
Syntactic path orderings like the Lexicographic Path Ordering (LPO) or Multiset Path Ordering (MPO) prove termination by ensuring that recursive calls are made on strict *syntactic subterms* of the original arguments.

Look at your factorial rule:
`fac(s(x)) -> times(fac(p(s(x))), s(x))`

For LPO to succeed, the argument in the recursive call `p(s(x))` must be smaller than the left-hand side argument `s(x)`. Syntactically, `p(s(x))` is *larger* than (or at least not a subterm of) `s(x)` because it has an extra symbol `p` wrapped around it. Syntactic orderings do not understand semantics; they don't "know" that `p` stands for predecessor and will eventually evaluate down to `x`. Because of this nested defined symbol, LPO and MPO instantly fail.

### 2. Why Polynomial Interpretations Fail
A standard alternative to LPO is mapping terms to polynomials over the natural numbers. To do this directly, every left-hand side must map to a polynomial strictly greater than the right-hand side ($[L] > [R]$).

Again, the factorial rule breaks this:
`fac(s(x)) -> times(fac(p(s(x))), s(x))`

Mathematically, this rule dictates factorial growth. If we try to map `fac(x)` to some polynomial $P(x)$, we would need $P(x+1) > P(x) \cdot (x+1)$ (roughly speaking). But no polynomial grows fast enough to satisfy this—factorial growth fundamentally exceeds polynomial bounds. Therefore, any automated prover restricted to standard polynomial interpretations will fail.

### 3. Why Dependency Pairs + Semantic Algebra Works Perfectly
To bypass both problems, we need a method that does two things: separates the recursive loop from the output structure, and allows us to use actual mathematical functions.

*   **The Dependency Pair (DP) Framework:** This technique splits the problem. Instead of forcing the entire right-hand side of a rule to be strictly smaller than the left-hand side, DP only requires that the *rules themselves* are weakly decreasing ($[L] \ge [R]$). The strict decrease ($>$) is only required for the isolated recursive calls (the Dependency Pairs). This neutralizes the syntactic problem of `times(..., s(x))` wrapping the recursive call.
*   **Semantic Interpretation (Math over $\mathbb{N}$):** Because DP only requires the rules to be *weakly* decreasing ($[L] \ge [R]$), we can just assign every symbol its literal mathematical meaning (`times` becomes multiplication, `fac` becomes factorial). Because your rules represent true mathematical identities, the left and right sides evaluate to the exact same values, satisfying $\ge$ trivially as equality ($=$). 

By isolating the recursive call `FAC(s(x)) -> FAC(p(s(x)))` into a Dependency Pair, the semantic interpretation simply looks at the arguments and evaluates $(x+1)! + (x+1) > x! + x$, proving that the loop strictly decreases toward a base case. 

In short, I picked this method because it is the most elegant, standard way to handle a system containing both **nested defined symbols in recursive calls** and **super-polynomial growth rates**.
