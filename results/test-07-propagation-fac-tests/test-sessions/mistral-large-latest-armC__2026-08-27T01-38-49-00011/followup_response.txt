I picked **lexicographic path order (LPO** (or a similar path ordering) for the following reasons, tailored to this specific system:

---

### 1. **Constructor Discipline and Recursive Definitions**
The system is **constructor-based** (assuming `0`, `s`, `plus`, `times` are constructors or defined symbols), and the rules are oriented to reduce terms to "simpler" forms. For example:
- `plus(x, s(y)) -> s(plus(x, y))` reduces the second argument by removing one `s`.
- `times(s(x), y) -> plus(times(x, y), y)` reduces the first argument by removing one `s`.
- `fac(s(x)) -> times(fac(p(s(x))), s(x))` reduces the argument to `fac` by calling `p(s(x))`, which is smaller than `s(x)`.

LPO is well-suited for such systems because it can handle **lexicographic comparisons** (e.g., "if the first argument is smaller, the whole term is smaller").

---

### 2. **No Variable Binding or Higher-Order Features**
The system is **first-order** with no variable binding (e.g., no λ-abstraction or quantifiers). LPO is designed for first-order TRS and does not require higher-order reasoning.

---

### 3. **Precedence Can Be Assigned Naturally**
We can assign a **precedence** to the symbols such that:
- `s > p` (since `p` reduces `s`),
- `times > plus > fac > p` (since `times` reduces to `plus`, which reduces to `s` or `0`, and `fac` reduces to `times`).

Under this precedence:
- `plus(x, s(y)) > s(plus(x, y))` because `s` has higher precedence than `plus`, and the second argument `s(y)` is greater than `y`.
- `times(s(x), y) > plus(times(x, y), y)` because `times` has higher precedence than `plus`, and the first argument `s(x)` is greater than `x`.
- `fac(s(x)) > times(fac(p(s(x))), s(x))` because `fac` has higher precedence than `times`, and the argument `s(x)` is greater than `p(s(x))` (since `p(s(x))` reduces to `x` or `s(p(x))`, which is smaller).

---

### 4. **No Non-Terminating Combinations**
The rules do not interact in a way that could cause non-termination:
- `plus` and `times` reduce to `s` or `0`.
- `fac` reduces to `times`, which terminates.
- `p` reduces the number of `s` constructors, ensuring progress toward a base case.

LPO can handle such **hierarchical definitions** (e.g., `times` reduces to `plus`, which reduces to `s` or `0`).

---

### 5. **Dependency Pairs Confirm Termination**
The **dependency pairs** for this system would be:
- `PLUS(x, s(y)) -> PLUS(x, y)`,
- `TIMES(s(x), y) -> TIMES(x, y)`,
- `TIMES(s(x), y) -> PLUS(times(x, y), y)`,
- `FAC(s(x)) -> FAC(p(s(x)))`,
- `FAC(s(x)) -> TIMES(fac(p(s(x))), s(x))`.

These pairs are **strictly decreasing** under LPO (e.g., `FAC(s(x)) > FAC(p(s(x)))` because `s(x) > p(s(x))`), ensuring no cycles.

---

### 6. **Alternatives Considered (and Rejected)**
Other methods were less suitable:
- **Polynomial Interpretations**: Hard to define for `p` and `fac` (e.g., `p(s(x))` would require a non-linear interpretation).
- **Recursive Path Order (RPO)**: Similar to LPO but less flexible for lexicographic comparisons.
- **Matrix Interpretations**: Overkill for this system and harder to automate.

LPO is **simple, automatable, and widely supported** in termination tools (e.g., AProVE, TTT2).

---

### Summary
LPO was chosen because:
1. The system is constructor-based and first-order.
2. A natural precedence can be assigned.
3. The rules reduce terms in a well-founded ordering.
4. Dependency pairs confirm termination.
5. No higher-order or non-terminating interactions exist.

No additional axioms or assumptions are needed.
