I chose the polynomial interpretation method for a few practical reasons:

1. **Simplicity and directness**  
   All functions in your system are defined by structural recursion on natural numbers (with constructors `0` and `s`). A polynomial interpretation over ℕ mirrors this structure directly — I can assign a simple numeric “weight” to each symbol and then check each rule algebraically. There’s no need to build a complex well-founded order; a decrease in natural numbers immediately gives termination.

2. **Works well with recursive calls**  
   Many rules like `plus(x, s(y)) → s(plus(x, y))` or `p(s(s(x))) → s(p(s(x)))` have recursive calls on a smaller argument, which naturally become a smaller numeric value under a carefully chosen polynomial. The method scales nicely to multiplication, where the interpretation can be made “big enough” to overcome the apparent increase in complexity.

3. **Avoids extra machinery**  
   Other standard techniques — like lexicographic path orders (LPO), the dependency pair framework, or semantic path orders — would also work here, but they often require additional lemmas or more complex argument-filtering. The polynomial approach gives a self-contained, arithmetic proof that first-year students can follow.

4. **Easy to verify**  
   The decrease for each rule boils down to a simple inequality (e.g., `x+1 > x`). No hidden conditions or side checks are needed, and the interpretation is strictly monotonic on all arguments, which satisfies the requirements for termination proofs without further complication.

In short, it was the most straightforward off-the-shelf method that fits the system perfectly and keeps the argument rigorous but short.
