I chose that method through a process of elimination driven by the specific obstacle I found in the rule set, not because it's a default "go-to" tactic. Here's the reasoning chain:

**1. I first tried the simplest class of methods (simplification orderings).**
LPO/RPO/KBO are the standard first attempt for term rewriting termination because they're easy to check and often sufficient. But direct inspection of the `fac`/`p` interaction showed a *structural* incompatibility: the subterm property that all simplification orders share forces `p(s(x)) ≻ s(x)`, which propagates through monotonicity to make the RHS of the `fac` rule bigger than the LHS. This isn't a matter of trying harder with precedences — it's a proof that **no** member of this entire class can work. So I had to move to a strictly more expressive method.

**2. The failure mode told me *why* it failed, which pointed to the fix.**
The problem was the subterm property being too rigid — it can't let `p(s(x))` be treated as "smaller" than `s(x))` even though semantically (as predecessor) it should be. So I needed a method that:
   - drops the subterm property requirement,
   - but still only relies on the given rules (no imported axioms about what `p`, `fac` "mean"),
   - can assign an interpretation where `p` behaves like decrement even though it isn't a subterm-reducing operation syntactically.

**3. Polynomial/matrix interpretations satisfy exactly that.**
They only require monotonicity of the interpretation functions and that each rule's LHS interpretation exceeds the RHS's, under an interpretation you're free to choose independently of subterm structure. This directly circumvents the obstruction from step 1.

**4. I combined this with the dependency pair framework rather than a plain interpretation on the whole TRS.**
This is because `fac`'s recursion is layered through `times` and `plus`, and DP lets you isolate exactly the recursive calls that need to decrease (the usable rules), rather than requiring one global interpretation to orient every rule uniformly. This is standard practice for exactly this kind of "helper function defined via auxiliary recursive functions" pattern (classic `fac`-via-`pred`-style examples in the termination literature), so it's a natural fit once the obstruction ruled out naive orderings.

In short: I picked the method dictated by the failure of the simpler method — the specific way the naive approach failed (subterm-property violation) is a well-known signature that calls for interpretation-based methods within the DP framework, and that combination stays entirely within what's derivable from the given rules, satisfying the problem's constraint.
