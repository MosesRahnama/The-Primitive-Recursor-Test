Great question! The choice of method was guided by the **structure of the rewrite system** and the **types of recursion** present. Here’s why I picked the **Dependency Pair (DP) method combined with Recursive Path Order (RPO)**:

---

### **1. Why Dependency Pairs (DP)?**
The DP method is the **gold standard** for proving termination of rewrite systems because:
- It **decomposes** the problem into smaller, more manageable subproblems (the "DP problems").
- It **handles nested recursion** (like in `fac`) and **non-simply terminating rules** (like `p`) elegantly.
- It **avoids false negatives**: Some systems terminate, but simple methods like polynomial interpretations or plain RPO fail to prove it. DP is more robust.

#### **Why DP was necessary here:**
- The `p` rule (`p(s(s(x))) → s(p(s(x)))`) **does not orient under RPO/LPO** with a natural precedence (since `p` is "weaker" than `s`). DP allows us to analyze it separately.
- The `fac` rule (`fac(s(x)) → times(fac(p(s(x))), s(x))`) has a **nested recursive call** (`fac(p(s(x)))`), which is tricky for plain RPO. DP lets us focus on the recursive structure.

---

### **2. Why Recursive Path Order (RPO)?**
RPO is a **syntactic simplification order** that works well for:
- **Constructor-based systems** (like this one, where `s`, `0` are constructors).
- **Rules that "peel off" constructors** (like `plus(x, s(y)) → s(plus(x, y))` or `times(s(x), y) → plus(times(x, y), y)`).
- **Rules with no deep recursion** (like `plus(x, 0) → x` or `times(0, y) → 0`).

#### **Why RPO was a good fit for most rules:**
- The `plus` and `times` rules are **structurally recursive** (they reduce the second argument or peel off `s`), so RPO handles them easily.
- The `fac` and `p` rules are **not** purely structural (they have nested recursion), so RPO alone isn’t enough—but it still helps orient the non-recursive parts.

---

### **3. Why Not Other Methods?**
#### **Polynomial Interpretations?**
- Works well for **linear or quadratic growth**, but:
  - `fac` grows **factorially**, which is hard to capture with polynomials.
  - `p` is **non-monotonic** (it sometimes increases the term size, e.g., `p(s(0)) → 0` but `p(s(s(x))) → s(p(s(x)))`), making polynomial interpretations tricky.

#### **Lexicographic Path Order (LPO)?**
- Similar to RPO but **less restrictive** (allows more precedences).
- Still fails on `p(s(s(x))) → s(p(s(x)))` because `p` is "weaker" than `s`.

#### **Simplification Orders (e.g., Knuth-Bendix Order)?**
- Requires **weight functions**, which are hard to guess for `fac` and `p`.

#### **Termination via Innermost Termination?**
- The system is **not obviously innermost-terminating** (e.g., `fac(s(x))` calls `p(s(x))`, which might not be fully evaluated yet).
- DP handles this naturally by focusing on the recursive calls.

---

### **4. Why the Combination of DP + RPO?**
- **RPO** handles the "easy" rules (`plus`, `times`, and parts of `fac`).
- **DP** handles the "hard" rules (`p` and the recursive part of `fac`).
- Together, they cover all cases without needing extra assumptions.

---

### **5. Why Not Just Use DP for Everything?**
- DP is **more complex** than RPO (requires computing dependency pairs, solving DP problems, etc.).
- For the rules where RPO works, it’s **simpler and more efficient** to use it.
- The hybrid approach (RPO for most rules, DP for the tricky ones) is **standard practice** in termination tools like AProVE or TTT2.

---

### **6. Summary of the Choice**
| Method               | Why Used?                                                                 | Why Not Used for Everything?                     |
|----------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------|
| **RPO**              | Handles `plus`, `times`, and parts of `fac` easily.                       | Fails on `p` and nested recursion in `fac`.      |
| **Dependency Pairs** | Handles `p` and `fac` by focusing on recursive calls.                     | Overkill for simple rules like `plus(x, 0) → x`. |
| **Combination**      | Best of both worlds: RPO for simple rules, DP for hard ones.              | N/A                                              |

---

### **7. Could Another Method Work?**
Yes! For example:
- **Matrix Interpretations**: Could work for `fac` (using a 2x2 matrix to capture factorial growth), but it’s more complex than DP.
- **Path Orders with Status**: Could handle `p` by giving `p` a **right-to-left status**, but this is less intuitive than DP.
- **Semantic Labeling**: Overkill for this system.

DP + RPO is the **most straightforward and widely applicable** method for this case.

---

### **Final Answer**
I picked **Dependency Pairs + RPO** because:
1. The system has **mixed recursion patterns** (some rules are simple, some are nested).
2. **RPO** handles the simple rules efficiently.
3. **DP** handles the tricky recursive cases (`p` and `fac`) without needing extra assumptions.
4. The combination is **standard, robust, and minimal**—it doesn’t overcomplicate the proof for the easy rules while still covering the hard ones.

No other method would be as **natural or concise** for this system.
