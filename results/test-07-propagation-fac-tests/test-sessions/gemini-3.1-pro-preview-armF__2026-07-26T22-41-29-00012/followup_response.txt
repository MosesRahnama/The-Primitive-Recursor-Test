While standard, mapping abstract symbols to external mathematical domains borders on introducing "object-level assumptions" about what the symbols mean. 

LPO, by contrast, is purely **syntactic**. It treats `times`, `plus`, `s`, and `0` as entirely meaningless grammatical tokens. It relies solely on the shape of the abstract syntax tree and a simple precedence hierarchy. It proves termination based on the mechanics of the rules themselves, not on an external model.

### 2. Tailor-made for Structural Recursion
If you look at the rewrite rules, they operate via structural recursion—specifically, peeling off the `s` (successor) constructor. 
* In `plus(x, s(y)) -> s(plus(x, y))`, the `plus` operation is recursively called, but the second argument strictly shrinks from `s(y)` to `y`. 
* In `plus(s(x), y) -> s(plus(x, y))`, the first argument strictly shrinks from `s(x)` to `x`.

LPO is historically designed to capture exactly this behavior. Its core mechanics—specifically the combination of the **subterm property** (knowing `y` is strictly smaller than `s(y)`) and **lexicographic comparison** (checking arguments left-to-right to see if one has decreased)—allow it to effortlessly prove that recursive calls are operating on structurally smaller inputs. 

### 3. Avoidance of Algebraic Overhead
Other syntactic orderings exist, such as the Knuth-Bendix Ordering (KBO). However, to use KBO, one must assign numerical "weights" to every function symbol and variable, and then solve a system of linear inequalities to prove the left-hand side is "heavier" than the right-hand side. 

LPO requires no arithmetic, no weight assignments, and no equation solving. It requires only a simple priority list (precedence: `times > plus > s > 0`) and a scan of the term structures.
