I chose a syntactic recursive-descent analysis because the system is a first-order TRS and the apparent recursion is exposed directly in the right-hand sides:

- `plus` recurs on a smaller second successor argument.
- `times` recurs on a smaller first successor argument.
- `p` recurs beneath one fewer leading `s`.
- `fac` is the only nontrivial case, since its recursive call is mediated by `p`.

That is the natural first pass when no external equations or semantics may be assumed: inspect recursive calls and determine whether rewriting can create an infinite dependency chain.

However, my previous response stated the conclusion too strongly for what I actually supplied. The informal “arguments get smaller” discussion is not by itself a formal global termination proof, particularly because:

- rewriting is unrestricted, including below function symbols;
- `times` duplicates `y`; and
- `fac` and `p` interact, so the relevant decrease is not simply a decrease in the immediately visible argument of `fac`.

A rigorous establishment should use a genuine TRS termination technique—e.g. dependency pairs with an appropriate well-founded measure/order, or another explicitly defined interpretation/order—and verify every rule and dependency cycle. Such a proof would still be based solely on the displayed rules; the issue is that my original explanation was a heuristic justification rather than that proof.
