I picked **lexicographic path ordering (LPO)** because the rules have a clear syntactic recursive structure, while simpler measures such as term size do not work.

In particular:

- The multiplication recursion replaces  
  \(\mathsf{times}(x,\mathsf{s}(y))\)  
  by a larger-looking term  
  \(\mathsf{plus}(\mathsf{times}(x,y),x)\).
  It also duplicates \(x\), so raw symbol count, depth, and many simple linear measures need not decrease.

- Nevertheless, the recursive call is structurally smaller:
  \[
  \mathsf{times}(x,y)
  \quad\text{instead of}\quad
  \mathsf{times}(x,\mathsf{s}(y)).
  \]

- Similarly, both recursive addition rules replace a call to `plus` by a smaller `plus` call and then wrap the result in `s`.

LPO is designed to capture exactly this pattern. The precedence

\[
\mathsf{times}\succ\mathsf{plus}\succ\mathsf{s}\succ 0
\]

expresses that:

1. recursive `times` calls decrease before producing `plus`;
2. recursive `plus` calls decrease before producing `s`;
3. constructor or constant results are below the defined operations.

Lexicographic status then recognizes the relevant argument decrease:

- the second argument decreases in
  \(\mathsf{times}(x,\mathsf{s}(y))\) and
  \(\mathsf{plus}(x,\mathsf{s}(y))\);
- the first argument decreases in
  \(\mathsf{plus}(\mathsf{s}(x),y)\).

I chose it over dependency pairs or semantic interpretations because it gives a short, direct orientation of **all six rules**, including the duplicating multiplication rule. Most importantly for your boundary condition, LPO is a metatheoretic ordering on the given finite syntax. It does not assume that `0` and `s` are natural numbers, nor does it import induction, associativity, commutativity, constructor freeness, or any additional rewrite behavior.
