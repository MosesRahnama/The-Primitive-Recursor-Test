Yes. The proposed LPO proof complies with those requirements.

The only background used is the standard metatheory already inherent in calling the system a **first-order term rewriting system**:

- terms are finite syntax trees;
- rewriting is closed under substitutions and term contexts;
- LPO is a well-founded ordering on such finite terms and is stable under substitutions and contexts.

The selected precedence

\[
\mathsf{times}\succ\mathsf{plus}\succ\mathsf{s}\succ 0
\]

and lexicographic statuses are **proof parameters**, not new object-level axioms or rewrite rules. They do not assert that `0` and `s` denote natural numbers, or that `plus` and `times` have their usual arithmetic meanings.

In particular, the proof does **not** assume:

- induction over natural numbers;
- that every term is either `0` or of the form `s(t)`;
- constructor freeness or injectivity of `s`;
- associativity or commutativity;
- equations beyond the displayed rewrite rules;
- restrictions on substitutions;
- guards, typing conditions, or additional rules.

LPO handles arbitrary substitutions, including substitutions where variables are replaced by terms headed by `plus` or `times`. The needed “structural decreases,” such as

\[
\mathsf{s}(y)>y,
\]

come from the syntactic subterm property of LPO, not from an imported axiom about natural numbers.

Thus, assuming the usual finite-term definition of a first-order TRS—which the prompt itself specifies—the proof stays entirely within the permitted boundary. If “no structural axioms” were instead intended to prohibit even standard metatheoretic facts about finite first-order syntax or the use of a well-founded reduction ordering, then no standard termination proof could be given; but that interpretation is ruled out by the prompt’s explicit permission to use any standard proof method.
