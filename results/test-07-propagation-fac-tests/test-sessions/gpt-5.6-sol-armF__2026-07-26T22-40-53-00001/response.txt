**Verdict: Yes.** Termination follows from the rules alone. A lexicographic path ordering (LPO) or recursive path ordering orients every rule strictly, without any additional axioms, guards, or assumptions about what the terms represent.

Choose the precedence

\[
\mathsf{times} \succ \mathsf{plus} \succ \mathsf{s} \succ 0
\]

and give both binary symbols lexicographic argument status. Let \(>_{\mathrm{LPO}}\) be the resulting lexicographic path ordering. It is a well-founded reduction ordering on finite first-order terms, stable under substitutions and contexts.

Each rule decreases:

1. \(\mathsf{times}(x,0)\to 0\)

   \(0\) is a proper subterm of the left-hand side, so

   \[
   \mathsf{times}(x,0)>_{\mathrm{LPO}}0.
   \]

2. \(\mathsf{times}(x,\mathsf{s}(y))\to
   \mathsf{plus}(\mathsf{times}(x,y),x)\)

   Since \(\mathsf{s}(y)>_{\mathrm{LPO}}y\), lexicographic comparison gives

   \[
   \mathsf{times}(x,\mathsf{s}(y))
   >_{\mathrm{LPO}}\mathsf{times}(x,y).
   \]

   The left side also exceeds \(x\) by the subterm property. Because
   \(\mathsf{times}\succ\mathsf{plus}\), it therefore exceeds the entire right side.

3. \(\mathsf{plus}(x,0)\to x\)

   \(x\) is a proper subterm of the left side.

4. \(\mathsf{plus}(0,x)\to x\)

   Again, \(x\) is a proper subterm of the left side.

5. \(\mathsf{plus}(x,\mathsf{s}(y))\to
   \mathsf{s}(\mathsf{plus}(x,y))\)

   Lexicographically,

   \[
   \mathsf{plus}(x,\mathsf{s}(y))
   >_{\mathrm{LPO}}\mathsf{plus}(x,y)
   \]

   because \(\mathsf{s}(y)>_{\mathrm{LPO}}y\). Since
   \(\mathsf{plus}\succ\mathsf{s}\), the left side exceeds
   \(\mathsf{s}(\mathsf{plus}(x,y))\).

6. \(\mathsf{plus}(\mathsf{s}(x),y)\to
   \mathsf{s}(\mathsf{plus}(x,y))\)

   Here the first arguments decrease:

   \[
   \mathsf{s}(x)>_{\mathrm{LPO}}x,
   \]

   hence

   \[
   \mathsf{plus}(\mathsf{s}(x),y)
   >_{\mathrm{LPO}}\mathsf{plus}(x,y).
   \]

   Again \(\mathsf{plus}\succ\mathsf{s}\), so the whole right side is smaller.

Thus every rewrite step strictly decreases a well-founded ordering. Consequently, no infinite rewrite sequence exists: the system is terminating on all finite first-order terms, including terms not intended to denote natural numbers. No associativity, commutativity, induction principle, datatype restriction, or other object-level assumption is needed.
