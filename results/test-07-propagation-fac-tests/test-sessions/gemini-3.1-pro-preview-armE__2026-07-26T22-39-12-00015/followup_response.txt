### 1. Why Lexicographic Path Ordering (LPO)?
I chose LPO because it is the most natural fit for the **syntactic structure** of your rules. 

When you look at the system, it is essentially defining a function `F` that recurses on its third argument. In the rule `F(x, y, S(n)) -> G(y, F(x, y, n))`, the third argument shrinks structurally from `S(n)` to `n`, while the first two arguments (`x` and `y`) remain identical. 

LPO was practically invented for this exact scenario. It mathematically formalizes the intuition of "structural descent." Because LPO compares arguments left-to-right (or right-to-left) and respects the subterm property (the fact that `n` is smaller than `S(n)`), it proves termination purely by looking at the shape of the symbols. It requires no arithmetic, no counting, and strictly adheres to your constraint of using nothing but the syntax of the rules.

### 2. Why Polynomial Interpretation?
I included the polynomial interpretation as a complementary method because it provides a **concrete mathematical bound**. 

Sometimes path orderings can feel abstract. A polynomial interpretation translates the abstract syntax trees directly into positive integers. By proving that the left-hand side of every rule evaluates to a strictly larger number than the right-hand side, we map the rewrite system to a strictly decreasing sequence of positive integers. Since you cannot count down infinitely through positive integers, the system *must* terminate.

The rule generates a new `G(y, ...)` for every `S` it unwraps, which is a multiplication-like behavior. The polynomial translates that structural unrolling into standard arithmetic.
