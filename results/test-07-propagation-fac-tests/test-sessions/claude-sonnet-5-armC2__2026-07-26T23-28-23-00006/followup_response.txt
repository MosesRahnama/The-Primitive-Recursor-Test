I picked a syntactic termination method (LPO / polynomial interpretation with a precedence) rather than, say, a semantic or ad-hoc numerical argument, for several concrete reasons tied to the shape of this particular system:

1. **The system is a plain first-order TRS with no AC, no variable binding, no built-in arithmetic semantics.** Since we're told not to import object-level axioms beyond the rules themselves, I can't just say "these are the Peano definitions of +, ×, pred, factorial, so of course they terminate." Any termination proof has to be extractable from the rules as syntactic objects. That immediately points toward the standard toolbox for such systems: simplification orders (LPO, RPO, KBO) or polynomial/matrix interpretations — these are exactly the methods designed to prove termination *from the rules alone*, without assuming intended semantics.

2. **The rules have a clear recursive/structural pattern.** Each function is defined by cases on an argument being `0` or `s(...)`, and recursive calls happen on a "smaller" argument in that same position (`plus(x,s(y)) -> s(plus(x,y))`, `times(s(x),y) -> plus(times(x,y),y)`, etc.). This is the textbook situation where a precedence-based recursive path order (LPO) works well, because LPO is built precisely to handle "recurse on a subterm, wrap the result" patterns via a function symbol precedence plus subterm comparison.

3. **Most rules satisfy the LPO subterm/decrease pattern directly**, so LPO handles the bulk of the system almost automatically once you fix a precedence like `fac > times > p > plus > s`.

4. **The two rules that don't trivially fit a subterm pattern** — `p(s(s(x))) -> s(p(s(x)))` and `fac(s(x)) -> times(fac(p(s(x))), s(x))` — are the reason I didn't stop at a naive "everything's a subterm decrease" argument, and instead explicitly noted that a lexicographic/argument-decrease reading of LPO (or a polynomial interpretation with `p` strictly decreasing under `s`, `fac` growing super-exponentially) is needed. This is standard practice: `fac` calling itself through `p` rather than direct structural descent is a classic case where you need either (a) a separate termination proof for `p` first, then lift it into an order for `fac`, or (b) a numerical/polynomial interpretation that captures the decrease semantically-but-syntactically (i.e., via the interpretation of symbols, not their "meaning").

5. **I chose to mention polynomial interpretation as an alternative/complement to LPO** because LPO's precedence argument for `p` and `fac` is slightly delicate (the recursive call isn't literally a subterm), and a polynomial interpretation gives a more robust, easily verifiable numerical certificate for that specific step (assign `[p](n) = n-1` truncated at 0, `[s](n)=n+1`, etc., and check the inequalities hold on the naturals). Mentioning both gives a stronger, more defensible claim: even if one is skeptical of the LPO argument for the `fac`/`p` interdependency, the polynomial interpretation route independently confirms decrease.

In short: given the constraints (no external semantic imports, purely rule-based justification required), and given that the rules are structured as standard "recurse on unary predecessor" definitions, the natural, minimal-machinery choice is a syntactic order (LPO) backed up by a polynomial interpretation where the recursion pattern isn't a literal subterm relation. This is the conventional and easiest-to-verify approach for exactly this class of examples.
