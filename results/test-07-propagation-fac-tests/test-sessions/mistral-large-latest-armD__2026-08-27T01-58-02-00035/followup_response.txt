Great question! The choice of **recursive path order (RPO)** with a carefully constructed precedence was deliberate, and here’s why it was the right tool for this problem:

---

### **1. Why RPO?**
RPO is a **syntactic termination method** that works directly on the structure of the rewrite rules without requiring additional semantic assumptions (like well-foundedness of integers, structural induction, or external axioms). This aligns perfectly with the constraints of the problem:
- **No variable binding**: RPO treats variables as opaque symbols, so it doesn’t care about their "meaning."
- **No external memory or axioms**: RPO only needs the rewrite rules themselves; it doesn’t rely on imported theories (e.g., Peano arithmetic).
- **No hidden assumptions**: RPO’s proof is purely combinatorial, based on the precedence of symbols and subterm relations.

Other methods (like polynomial interpretations or dependency pairs) could also work, but RPO is often the simplest for systems like this where the rules are "syntactically decreasing" in a clear way.

---

### **2. Why Not Other Methods?**
#### **a) Polynomial Interpretations**
- Requires assigning polynomials to symbols (e.g., `s(x) = x + 1`, `plus(x, y) = x + y`), which implicitly assumes arithmetic properties (e.g., associativity of `+`). This violates the "no external axioms" constraint.
- The `p` (predecessor) rules would require interpreting `p` as `max(x - 1, 0)`, which is not purely syntactic.

#### **b) Dependency Pairs (DP)**
- DP is powerful but more complex. It’s overkill for this system, where the rules are already "obviously" decreasing under a precedence.
- DP would require computing the dependency graph and proving its acyclicity, which is more work than RPO for this case.

#### **c) Lexicographic Path Order (LPO)**
- LPO is a simpler version of RPO but is less flexible (e.g., it doesn’t allow lexicographic status for symbols like `plus` and `times`). RPO’s ability to assign status (lexicographic or multiset) to symbols makes it easier to handle rules like `times(s(x), y) → plus(times(x, y), y)`.

#### **d) Semantic Methods (e.g., Well-Founded Orders)**
- These require interpreting terms in a well-founded domain (e.g., natural numbers), which introduces external assumptions (e.g., "0 is the smallest number"). The problem forbids this.

---

### **3. Why the Specific Precedence?**
The precedence `times > plus > p > s > 0` was chosen to satisfy all rules simultaneously:
- **`times > plus`**: Needed for `times(s(x), y) → plus(times(x, y), y)` to orient correctly (the recursive call `times(x, y)` must be smaller than `times(s(x), y)`).
- **`plus > p`**: Needed for `p(s(s(x))) → s(p(s(x)))` to orient (the `p` on the RHS must be smaller than the `s` on the LHS).
- **`p > s`**: Needed for `p(s(s(x))) → s(p(s(x)))` to ensure the argument `s(s(x))` is larger than `p(s(x))`.
- **`s > 0`**: Needed for `p(s(0)) → 0` (the `s(0)` must be larger than `0`).

The lexicographic status for `plus` and `times` ensures that rules like `plus(x, s(y)) → s(plus(x, y))` orient correctly (the second argument `s(y)` is larger than `y`).

---

### **4. Why Not a Simpler Precedence?**
Initially, I tried `times > plus > s > p > 0`, but this failed for `p(s(s(x))) → s(p(s(x)))` because `s > p` made it hard to compare `s(s(x))` and `p(s(x))`. Swapping `p` and `s` fixed this, but then `times(s(x), y) → plus(times(x, y), y)` failed because `s` was no longer below `plus`. The final precedence `times > plus > p > s > 0` was the minimal adjustment that worked for all rules.

---

### **5. Why Not Multiset Status?**
Multiset status is useful for commutative symbols (e.g., `plus(x, y) = plus(y, x)`), but here `plus` and `times` are not commutative. Lexicographic status (left-to-right) is more natural for these rules. For example:
- `plus(x, s(y)) → s(plus(x, y))` requires comparing the second arguments (`s(y)` vs `y`), which lexicographic status handles cleanly.

---

### **6. Summary of the Choice**
RPO was ideal because:
1. It’s **syntactic** (no external axioms).
2. It’s **flexible** (precedence + status can be tuned to fit the rules).
3. It’s **simple** (no need for complex machinery like dependency pairs).
4. It **scales** to all rules in the system without hidden assumptions.

The precedence was chosen to satisfy all rules simultaneously, and the proof is purely mechanical once the precedence is set. This aligns perfectly with the problem’s constraints.
