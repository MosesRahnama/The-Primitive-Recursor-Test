I chose the **Lexicographic Path Ordering (LPO)** because it is the most natural and direct fit for the specific structural patterns exhibited by this rewrite system, while other standard methods fail or require unnecessary complexity. 

Here is the detailed breakdown of why LPO was the optimal choice:

### 1. The System Exhibits Primitive Recursion on a Specific Argument
Rule 2 (`F(x, y, S(n)) -> G(y, F(x, y, n))`) is a classic primitive recursion scheme. It recurses strictly on the third argument (peeling off an `S`), while passing the first two arguments (`x` and `y`) unchanged. Path orderings (like LPO and RPO) were explicitly invented to prove termination for exactly this kind of structural descent. 

### 2. Knuth-Bendix Ordering (KBO) Fails Due to Variable Duplication
Before trying path orderings, one typically checks the Knuth-Bendix Ordering (KBO), which relies on assigning numeric weights to symbols. However, KBO strictly fails here. 
* Look at the variable `y` in Rule 2: it appears **once** on the left-hand side, but **twice** on the right-hand side (once as an argument to `G`, and once inside the recursive `F` call). 
* KBO requires that the LHS is at least as "heavy" as the RHS for all possible substitutions. Because `y` is duplicated on the RHS, if we substitute a massive term for `y`, the RHS will always outweigh the LHS. Therefore, no valid weight function can orient Rule 2 under KBO.

### 3. Precedence Solves the Root Symbol Change and Embedding
In Rule 2, the root symbol changes from `F` to `G`, and the recursive call `F(x, y, n)` is embedded *inside* the `G` symbol. 
* Polynomial interpretations struggle here because the "size" of the term isn't just growing additively; the recursive call is nested. 
* LPO handles this elegantly via **precedence**. By declaring `F > G`, LPO allows the "heavier" root symbol on the LHS (`F`) to dominate the "lighter" root symbol on the RHS (`G`), provided we can prove the LHS is strictly greater than all the arguments of `G`. This perfectly bypasses the embedding problem.

### 4. Lexicographic Status Matches the Argument Structure
When comparing the recursive call `F(x, y, S(n))` on the LHS to the embedded call `F(x, y, n)` on the RHS, we are comparing two terms with the same root symbol. 
* The first two arguments (`x` and `y`) are identical.
* The third argument strictly decreases (`S(n)` to `n`).
* While a multiset comparison (as used in standard RPO) would also work, the **lexicographic** status of LPO is the most precise conceptual match. It reads the arguments left-to-right, recognizes the tie on the prefix `(x, y)`, and immediately resolves the strict decrease on the suffix `S(n) > n`. 

### Summary
I picked LPO because it is the standard "scalpel" for this exact shape of problem. KBO is broken by the duplicated variable `y`, polynomial interpretations would require guessing complex non-linear weights to handle the nested `G(F(...))` structure, whereas LPO natively supports structural descent, handles the root-symbol change via a simple precedence (`F > G`), and cleanly processes the unchanged argument prefix via lexicographic comparison.
