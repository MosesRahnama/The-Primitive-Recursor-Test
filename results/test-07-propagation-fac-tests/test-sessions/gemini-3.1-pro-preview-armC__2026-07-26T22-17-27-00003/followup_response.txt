When I assessed the system, I relied on two complementary methods: **intuitive structural descent** (for the human explanation) and the **Dependency Pair framework with semantic interpretations** (as the formal, automated counterpart). 

Here is why I specifically highlighted those methods, particularly over simpler formal methods:

### 1. The Limitation of Standard Syntactic Orders
The most basic formal methods for proving termination are syntactic simplification orders, like the Lexicographic Path Order (LPO) or Knuth-Bendix Order (KBO). These methods try to prove that the left-hand side of a rule is syntactically "greater" than the right-hand side. 

However, they **fail** on your factorial rule:
`fac(s(x)) -> times(fac(p(s(x))), s(x))`

For a basic syntactic path order to prove this terminates, it would need to prove that the argument on the left, `s(x)`, is strictly greater than the argument in the recursive call, `p(s(x))`. Syntactically, this is impossible because `p(s(x))` actually *contains* `s(x)` as a subterm. A simple syntactic checker will see the term getting larger and give up.

### 2. Why the Dependency Pair (DP) Framework is Necessary
Because basic syntactic orders fail, we need a method that separates the *evaluation* of arguments from the recursive calls themselves. I picked the **Dependency Pair framework** because it was explicitly designed to solve this exact problem.

The DP framework isolates the recursive call into a "pair":
`FAC(s(x)) -> FAC(p(s(x)))`

Instead of demanding that `p(s(x))` is syntactically smaller than `s(x)`, the DP framework allows us to prove that *once `p` is evaluated*, the resulting term is smaller. 

### 3. Why Polynomial / Matrix Interpretations?
To prove that `p(s(x))` evaluates to something smaller than `s(x)`, we have to map the syntax to mathematics (semantics). I chose to mention methods like **Matrix or Polynomial Interpretations** because they map constructors and functions to numbers or matrices. 

For example, a solver can easily assign a polynomial interpretation like:
*   `s(x) = x + 1`
*   `p(x) = x - 1` (with a floor at 0)

Under this interpretation, the dependency pair `FAC(s(x)) -> FAC(p(s(x)))` becomes:
*   Left side: `x + 1`
*   Right side: `(x + 1) - 1 = x`

Since `x + 1 > x`, the solver mathematically proves that the recursive call decreases in size. 

**Summary:** I chose to explain the structural decrease in the number of `s` constructors because it is the most intuitive way for a human to understand the system. I specifically cited the Dependency Pair framework and interpretations because simpler syntactic methods fail on rules where a function is applied to the recursive argument (like `fac(p(...))`), making DP the standard, necessary tool for this specific system.
