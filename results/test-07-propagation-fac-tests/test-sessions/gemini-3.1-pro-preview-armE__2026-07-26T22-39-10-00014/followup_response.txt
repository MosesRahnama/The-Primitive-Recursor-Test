**1. Lexicographic Path Ordering (The Syntactic Approach)**
I chose LPO because it is the most natural tool for systems that rely on *structural recursion*. 
If you look closely at your second rule:
`F(x, y, S(n)) -> G(y, F(x, y, n))`
You can see it is essentially peeling off an `S` from the third argument. The recursive call `F(x, y, n)` is structurally smaller than `F(x, y, S(n))`. LPO was explicitly invented (by Nachum Dershowitz in the late 1970s) to mechanically capture this exact kind of structural decrease. By simply telling the ordering that `F` is a "heavier" operation than `G` (precedence `F > G`), LPO automatically recognizes that the rule reduces a complex term into a simpler structural configuration. It requires no math, just an analysis of the shape of the trees.

**2. Polynomial Interpretation (The Semantic Approach)**
I chose Polynomial Interpretation because it provides an unassailable *arithmetic* proof. 
Some strict formalists prefer not to rely on theorems about trees and path orderings, wanting a proof rooted in basic grade-school algebra. By mapping the abstract symbols to concrete numbers (e.g., $Z=1$, $S(n)=n+1$), we turn the rewrite system into a simple inequality problem. 
I chose the specific polynomial $[F] = x + yn + 2n$ because it mathematically perfectly models what the rewrite system is doing: it is accumulating `y` into `x` exactly `n` times (with an extra `+ 2n` to ensure strict mathematical decrease at each step). If every rewrite strictly decreases an integer, and the integer can never go below 1, infinite loops are physically impossible.
