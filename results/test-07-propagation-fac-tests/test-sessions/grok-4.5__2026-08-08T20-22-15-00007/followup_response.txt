### Why dependency pairs + a reduction pair (via interpretation)

The system is a pure first-order TRS with nested/mutually recursive defined symbols (`fac` calls `p` and `times`, `times` calls `plus`, and `p` has its own slow “unwinding” rules).  A direct attempt with a single reduction order on the original rules (e.g., a plain polynomial or matrix interpretation, or a recursive path order with a simple precedence) fails for elementary arithmetic reasons:

- The rule `fac(s(x)) → times(fac(p(s(x))), s(x))` forces the measure of `fac` to drop while the right-hand side still contains a recursive call under a multiplication; any interpretation that makes multiplication strictly monotone will typically make the right-hand side larger unless the measure of `fac` is allowed to ignore its argument (or to collapse it).  
- At the same time the two `p`-rules only “peel” one `s` every two steps, so a simple subterm or lexicographic order does not see an immediate decrease on every recursive call.

The dependency-pair (DP) transformation isolates precisely the recursive calls (the six DPs listed in the previous answer) and turns the original termination question into a simpler DP problem.  Once the DPs are extracted, a very weak reduction pair already orients them:

- unmarked symbols receive ordinary arithmetic operations (so all original rules are weakly decreasing),  
- marked symbols receive projections or simple linear functions that make every DP strictly decreasing on \(\mathbb{N}\).

No extra rewrite rules, guards or object-level axioms are added; the only meta-level ingredient is the well-foundedness of \(\mathbb{N}\), which is part of the definition of a reduction pair.  The method is therefore self-contained for the given syntax and rules, complete for first-order TRSs, and needs only a few lines of arithmetic to verify—exactly the lightest argument that still covers every rule.
