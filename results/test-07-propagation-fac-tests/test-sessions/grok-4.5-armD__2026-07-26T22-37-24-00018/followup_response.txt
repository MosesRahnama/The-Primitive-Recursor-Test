### Why LPO?

I chose the lexicographic path order (LPO) for three tightly related reasons that stay inside the boundary you set (purely syntactic, no extra rules/guards/side-conditions, no semantic interpretations or domain axioms).

1. **The shape of the rules immediately suggests a simplification order**  
   Every right-hand side is built from proper subterms of the left-hand side, possibly under a symbol that can be made smaller in a precedence.  
   - The two `plus` rules and the three `times` rules are the classic primitive-recursive schema.  
   - The two `p` rules simply peel a constructor.  
   Simplification orders (of which LPO is the simplest commonly used instance) are designed exactly for this situation: they guarantee  
   \[
   f(s_1,\dots,s_n)\;>\;t
   \]
   whenever \(t\) is built from the \(s_i\) by symbols smaller than \(f\), or by a lexicographic descent in the arguments.  
   No arithmetic interpretation, no polynomial, no matrix, no domain axiom is required—only the inductive definition of the order on the given signature.

2. **A total precedence is read off the call graph in one pass**  
   ```
   times  uses  plus
   plus   uses  s
   p      uses  s
   ```
   Hence the total order
   \[
   \times\;>\;+\;>\;\mathsf{p}\;>\;\mathsf{s}\;>\;0
   \]
   orients every rule by a single root-symbol comparison plus (at most) a trivial lexicographic argument comparison.  
   Finding that precedence needs nothing beyond the seven rules themselves.

3. **Lighter-weight alternatives are unnecessary, heavier ones overkill**  
   - A plain recursive path order (RPO) would also work, but LPO is the special case that already succeeds, so there is no reason to invoke the more general multiset status.  
   - Knuth-Bendix order needs a weight function; that is an extra numeric parameter we do not need.  
   - Dependency-pair frameworks, semantic labelling, etc. are strictly more powerful but also strictly more elaborate; once a direct simplification order orients the whole system they are superfluous.  
   - Any method that would interpret `plus`/`times` as actual arithmetic operations would import object-level assumptions about \(\mathbb{N}\), which you explicitly forbade.

Thus LPO is the most economical standard technique that (a) is justified solely by the syntax and the given rewrite rules, (b) requires no extension of the system, and (c) yields a complete, checkable termination argument for every rule.
