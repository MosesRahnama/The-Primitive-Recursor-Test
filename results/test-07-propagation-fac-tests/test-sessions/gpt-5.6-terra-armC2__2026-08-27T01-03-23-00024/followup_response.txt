I picked a syntactic well-founded-measure approach because the question forbids relying on intended arithmetic meaning. The rules therefore have to be analyzed purely as term transformations: which recursive calls are created, and whether those calls can recur indefinitely.

In particular, the relevant recursive structure is:

- `plus` recurs on the second argument beneath one fewer `s`;
- `times` recurs on the first argument beneath one fewer `s`;
- `p` moves its recursive occurrence inward through a finite `s`-spine;
- `fac` is the only less-obviously structural case, because it calls itself through `p(s(x))`.

That makes a dependency-pair / multicomponent ranking argument the natural proof style, rather than an appeal to facts about natural numbers or factorial.

That said, my earlier wording was too compressed: merely counting the currently visible leading `s` symbols is not, by itself, a complete global termination proof for arbitrary terms. Arbitrary subterms can themselves reduce and expose new `s` symbols. A proper proof would need a more careful lexicographic or dependency-pair argument that accounts for the `p`-mediated `fac` call and for work created by `times` and `plus`.

So I chose the right general kind of method for the stated restriction, but my prior explanation was a proof sketch rather than a fully stated termination proof.
