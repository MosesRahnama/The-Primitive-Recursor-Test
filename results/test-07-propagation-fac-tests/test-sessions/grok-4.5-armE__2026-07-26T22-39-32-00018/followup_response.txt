### Why LPO?

I chose the **lexicographic path order** (LPO) because it is the most direct, purely syntactic, and assumption-free method that works cleanly for this exact system.

- The rules have a classic “primitive-recursive” shape: the third argument of \(F\) is inspected by a constructor case distinction (\(Z\) vs. \(S(n)\)), the recursive call is on a proper subterm \(n\), and the only other symbol that appears (\(G\)) is strictly simpler. LPO is purpose-built for exactly this pattern; the single precedence \(F > G > S > Z\) orients both rules in one step via the subterm property and lexicographic comparison of arguments—no coefficients, no semantic domains, no extra lemmas.
- It stays strictly inside the free term algebra generated by the given signature. No object-level axioms, no well-founded relations imported from outside, no sorts, no guards, and no strengthening of the rewrite system are required. The well-foundedness of LPO itself is a standard meta-theoretic fact about any simplification order.
- It is complete for the purpose at hand: once both rules decrease, every rewrite step (context + substitution) decreases, so termination follows immediately. The same orientation can be obtained by other means (MPO, a linear polynomial interpretation, a Knuth–Bendix order with suitable weights, \ldots), but each of those either needs a little more arithmetic bookkeeping or is essentially equivalent in strength for this tiny example. LPO is the shortest route that still addresses every rule explicitly.

In short, LPO matches the structure of the system perfectly and respects the constraint “use only what is derivable from the given syntax and rewrite rules.”
