I chose the **Dependency Pair (DP) framework** combined with **Strongly Connected Component (SCC) decomposition** and **weakly monotonic interpretations** for three primary reasons:

---

### 1. Modularity (Divide and Conquer)
Direct termination methods (like standard Lexicographic Path Ordering (LPO) or Multiset Path Ordering (MPO)) require finding a single, global ordering relation that satisfies *all* rewrite rules simultaneously. This is highly restrictive.

By using the DP framework, we can decompose the system into independent cycles (SCCs). This allowed us to:
* Prove that `plus` terminates using only its own arguments.
* Prove that `times` terminates using only its own arguments.
* Prove that `p` terminates.
* Focus the most complex part of the proof solely on the interaction between `fac` and `p`, without having to worry about how `plus` or `times` behaved.

### 2. Resolving Conflicting Precedences (The $p$ and $s$ conflict)
Direct path orderings fail or become extremely complicated on this system because of a conflict between the rules for $p$ and the rule for $fac$:
* In the rule $p(s(s(x))) \to s(p(s(x)))$, the constructor $s$ appears *outside* the recursive call to $p$ on the right-hand side. For LPO to orient this rule, it typically requires $p$ to have a higher precedence than $s$ ($p \succ s$).
* In the rule $fac(s(x)) \to times(fac(p(s(x))), s(x))$, the recursive call to $fac$ is nested inside $p$. For LPO to show that the arguments decrease, we would need $s(x) \succ p(s(x))$, which typically requires $s \succ p$. 

This creates a contradiction ($p \succ s$ vs. $s \succ p$). 

By using Dependency Pairs, we don't have to orient the actual rule $p(s(s(x))) \to s(p(s(x)))$ strictly. We only need to orient its dependency pair (which is trivial) or, when proving the $fac$ SCC, we only need to find a *weak* monotonic interpretation where $p(s(s(x))) \ge s(p(s(x)))$. The interpretation $[p(x)] = \max(0, x-1)$ resolves this perfectly because it evaluates both sides of the $p$-rule to exactly $x+1$, satisfying the weak inequality ($\ge$) while allowing the $fac$ dependency pair to strictly decrease ($x+1 > x$).

### 3. Eliminating Non-Recursive Contexts
In the rule:
$$\text{fac}(s(x)) \to \text{times}(\text{fac}(p(s(x))), s(x))$$
the recursive call to $\text{fac}$ is wrapped inside the $\text{times}$ operator. In a direct proof, you must prove that the entire right-hand side is "smaller" than the left-hand side.

The DP framework extracts the "active" transition:
$$\text{FAC}(s(x)) \to \text{FAC}(p(s(x)))$$
This completely eliminates the $\text{times}$ operator and the extra $s(x)$ term from the comparison, simplifying the target of the proof to just showing that the argument $s(x)$ transitions to a strictly smaller argument $p(s(x))$.
