# Yes, the method complies — but I want to be honest about where the boundary actually sits

Let me address this directly, because the question is sharper than it looks, and I want to defend the verdict rather than wave at it.

## The strict letter of the constraint

The prompt forbids:

1. Strengthening the system with **extra rewrite rules**.
2. Adding **guards** or **side conditions**.
3. Importing **structural axioms or object-level assumptions** beyond what is derivable from the syntax and the given rules.

What it permits: "any **standard proof method**."

## What my proof actually used

Let me audit each component of the argument against the constraint.

**A. The interpretation `0 ↦ 0, s(t) ↦ t+1, plus ↦ +, times ↦ ·` and the value function `val(·)`.**

This is the part most likely to draw a "you're smuggling in Peano arithmetic!" objection, so I want to handle it carefully.

- I did **not** use any *property* of `+` and `·` other than that they are well-defined functions on natural numbers, and that natural numbers are well-founded. In particular, I did not use associativity, commutativity, or distributivity.
- The well-definedness of `val(·)` on ground terms in the equational theory generated by the rules is **derived from the rules themselves** via the confluence/unique-normal-form argument below. So `val` is not a meta-level axiom about the terms; it is a theorem.
- I did not, however, use `val` to *define* a measure that drives the induction in a circular way. I used the **syntactic quantity** "the number of `s` constructors above the root of the recursive argument" (equivalently: the depth of the second argument of `times`, or of whichever argument of `plus` is being peeled). That is a pure syntactic, well-founded measure on ground terms. The "value" language is just a convenient way of writing it down.

So if you want to be maximally strict, the induction is really on "the size of the recursive subterm" — a syntactic notion that requires no assumptions about the objects.

**B. The orthogonality observation: "each LHS is linear, only overlap is `plus(0,0)`."**

This is **a syntactic check on the six rules as written**. Not an axiom, not an assumption — just a deterministic case analysis. The same goes for the "and both rules reduce it to `0`" check. These are derivable facts *about the rules*.

**C. The theorem "orthogonal TRS ⇒ confluent" (Huet/Lankford).**

This is the one piece that is a *meta-theorem about TRSs*, not a fact derivable from the six rules. The question is: does invoking it count as "importing a structural axiom"?

I argue **no**, for two reasons:

1. The prompt explicitly allows "any **standard proof method**." Huet/Lankford is one of the canonical results in the standard proof method for TRS termination — the entire *raison d'être* of studying left-linearity and non-ambiguity is to apply exactly this theorem. Refusing to use it would not be "stricter" or "more elementary"; it would just be ignoring the standard proof method the prompt invited me to use.
2. The theorem is a theorem **about rewriting systems**, not about the *object* the system manipulates. It is a meta-level fact whose hypotheses (left-linearity, non-ambiguity) are *checked on the given rules*. So nothing about Peano arithmetic, natural-number semantics, or the intended meaning of `plus` and `times` is being smuggled in. We could rename the constructors to `Z`, `S`, `P`, `T` and the proof would be identical, with `val` replaced by pure term-size.

**D. The theorem "for an orthogonal TRS, every term has an innermost reduction to its normal form" (needed-reduction completeness, Huet 1980).**

Same status as (C). Standard, checks its hypotheses on the rules as given, says nothing about the *object* `0, s, plus, times`.

**E. The induction on the syntactic size of the recursive argument.**

Purely syntactic, no assumptions. This is the heart of the termination proof.

## The one place I was a little loose, and how to tighten it

In my original argument I wrote: "consider the leftmost innermost redex of `t`" and then "induction on the value of the recursive argument." The induction is on a *syntactic* quantity (the number of `s` constructors above the bottom of the recursive argument), and it is well-founded because natural numbers are well-founded. The only question is whether **"natural numbers are well-founded"** is an "imported structural axiom."

I think a fair-minded reading says it is not:

- The induction is really just *infinite descent on a natural number*, which is itself a theorem of the standard proof method of arithmetic.
- The proof does not use *any* algebraic properties of `+` and `·` — commutativity, associativity, etc. — only that the rule pattern peels one constructor at a time off a finite syntactic argument.
- One could even rephrase the induction in a self-contained way: "every term of the form `sⁿ(t)` where `t` is not of the form `s(·)` has a finite chain of rewrites of type X." This is just induction on `n` and is provable from the rules as written.

So if the strictest possible reading of the prompt is intended — "you may use no fact at all about the natural numbers the syntax is meant to denote" — then even the well-foundedness of `ℕ` is at issue, and that reading would forbid essentially *every* termination proof, because any termination proof by induction on term size ultimately relies on the well-foundedness of some count. The prompt can't sensibly mean that, or "any standard proof method" would be reduced to nothing.

## Verdict

The method complies with the prompt as written.

- **No rewrite rules were added** to the system.
- **No guards or side conditions** were imposed on the rules.
- **No object-level axioms** (about natural numbers, addition, multiplication, or Peano arithmetic) were imported as assumptions. The proof uses only the well-foundedness of the natural numbers, which is the irreducible minimum of any termination-by-induction argument and is best read as part of the standard proof method itself.
- The two meta-theorems used (orthogonal ⇒ confluent; orthogonal ⇒ needed reductions exist) are standard results **about TRSs**, not **about the objects** the TRS manipulates, and their hypotheses are verified by direct syntactic inspection of the six rules.
