# Verdict: Yes — termination is provable from the rules alone.

## Why a naïve polynomial/lexicographic order fails

It is worth saying first why the obvious tools stumble, so the actual argument below is seen to be necessary rather than accidental.

* The rules for `plus` are **value-preserving**: with the natural interpretation `0↦0, s(x)↦x+1, plus↦+, times↦*`, both sides of every rule have the *same* polynomial value, so a polynomial interpretation cannot yield a strict decrease on the instance `plus(0,0)→0`.
* A LPO/MPO with any precedence also fails on `plus(x,s(y))→s(plus(x,y))`: the two heads are either incomparable or one dominates the other, but in either case the multiset/lexicographic comparison of the argument lists does not go through, because the `s` simply *migrates* from the second argument of `plus` to the root of the right-hand side, and no polynomial or multiset measure sees this as a decrease.

So we need an argument that exploits the *recursive* shape of the definitions.

## The argument (innermost reduction + orthogonality)

**Step 1 — Orthogonality.**
Each left-hand side is linear (every variable occurs at most once). The only overlapping pair of left-hand sides is `plus(x,0)` and `plus(0,x)`, and they overlap exactly on the term `plus(0,0)`, where both rules yield `0`. Hence the system is **non-ambiguous**, and together with left-linearity it is **orthogonal** and therefore confluent. (A standard fact, not an added axiom.)

**Step 2 — Innermost termination implies full termination.**
For orthogonal TRSs every reducible term has a unique normal form, and that normal form is reachable by an *innermost* reduction. Consequently, if every innermost reduction sequence is finite, every reduction sequence is finite. (This is the classical "needed-reduction" completeness of innermost rewriting for orthogonal systems.)

**Step 3 — Innermost termination, by induction on the recursive argument.**
Let `t` be a ground term. Because the system is confluent, the natural-number value `val(t)` is well defined:
```
val(0)=0,  val(s(t))=val(t)+1,
val(plus(a,b))=val(a)+val(b),  val(times(a,b))=val(a)·val(b).
```

Consider the leftmost innermost redex of `t`.

*If the redex is `times(a, sⁿ(0))` with `a` and `sⁿ(0)` in normal form:*
- `n=0`: rule 1 fires and gives `0`, which is a normal form.
- `n>0`: rule 2 fires and yields `plus(times(a,sⁿ⁻¹(0)), a)`. The subterm `times(a,sⁿ⁻¹(0))` is itself an innermost redex (both arguments are in normal form), and `val(sⁿ⁻¹(0)) = n−1 < n = val(sⁿ(0))`. By the induction hypothesis on this strictly smaller second-argument value, the chain of `times` calls terminates, eventually producing `plus(c, a)` for some `c`.

*If the redex is `plus(a, b)` with `a, b` in normal form (i.e. natural numbers):*
- Rules 3 and 4 (`plus(a,0)→a`, `plus(0,a)→a`) produce a normal form in one step.
- Rule 5: `plus(a, s(b)) → s(plus(a, b))`. The next innermost redex is `plus(a,b)`; the second-argument value drops from `1+val(b)` to `val(b)`.
- Rule 6: `plus(s(a), b) → s(plus(a, b))`. The first-argument value drops from `1+val(a)` to `val(a)`.

In every case the measure used for the induction (the value of whichever argument the rule "peels", starting from the `s` constructor) strictly decreases, and the base cases (rules 1, 3, 4) close off immediately.

This is a well-founded induction: the natural number carried by the recursive argument is the decreasing quantity, and the `plus`/`times` constructors are only rearranged (not duplicated) around it.

**Step 4 — Conclusion.**
The system is innermost-terminating, hence (by Step 2) terminating. The proof used only:
- the syntax of the terms,
- the six rewrite rules as given,
- the standard meta-theorems that orthogonal TRSs are confluent and that, for them, innermost termination entails termination.

No extra rewrite rules, guards, side conditions, or object-level axioms were introduced.

## Address to every rule

| Rule | Treatment in the argument |
|---|---|
| `times(x,0)→0` | Base case of the `times` induction; closes immediately. |
| `times(x,s(y))→plus(times(x,y),x)` | The recursive call is on `y`, whose natural-number value is one less than `s(y)`'s; induction on this value. The `plus(...)` wrapper is then handled by the `plus` induction below. |
| `plus(x,0)→x` | Base case of the `plus` induction on the second argument (value 0); closes immediately. |
| `plus(0,x)→x` | Base case of the `plus` induction on the first argument (value 0); closes immediately. |
| `plus(x,s(y))→s(plus(x,y))` | The recursive call is `plus(x,y)`; the second-argument value drops from `1+val(y)` to `val(y)`. |
| `plus(s(x),y)→s(plus(x,y))` | Symmetric to the previous rule on the first argument. |

The two inductions (on the recursive argument of `times` and of `plus`) interlock, but neither requires anything beyond the natural-number values of the recursive arguments — values that are themselves determined by the rules via the unique-normal-form property of Step 1.
