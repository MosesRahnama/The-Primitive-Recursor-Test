I picked orthogonality + innermost rewriting + induction on the value of the recursive argument because it is, in a precise sense, the proof that *the rules themselves want you to give*. Let me walk through the reasoning rather than just restate the result.

## 1. The rules are "value-preserving"

If you read `0` as `0`, `s(t)` as `t+1`, `plus` as `+`, and `times` as `·`, then every rule has the *same* value on both sides:

- `times(x,0) ↔ 0 = x·0`
- `times(x,s(y)) ↔ x·(y+1) = x·y + x = plus(times(x,y), x)`
- `plus(x,0) ↔ x+0 = x`
- `plus(0,x) ↔ 0+x = x`
- `plus(x,s(y)) ↔ x+(y+1) = 1+(x+y) = s(plus(x,y))`
- `plus(s(x),y) ↔ (x+1)+y = 1+(x+y) = s(plus(x,y))`

This is a strong structural signal. The rules aren't *computing* a new function — they implement the standard Peano definitions of `+` and `·`, and each rewrite step preserves the meaning of the term while making the **shape** of the term more "constructor-like".

That immediately rules out a whole family of methods:

- **Polynomial / matrix interpretations** rely on the right-hand side having a *strictly smaller* value under some well-chosen interpretation. But here the LHS and RHS are literally equal as natural numbers, so no polynomial map can separate them. The same goes for any "semantic" interpretation that treats `plus` as addition and `s` as successor.
- **Simplification orders in the LPO / MPO / RPO family** (lexicographic path, multiset path, recursive path orders) compare *terms* lexicographically or by multisets. But look at `plus(x, s(y)) → s(plus(x,y))`: the head constructor migrates from the second argument to the root, the sizes of the two terms are equal, and no precedence makes one strictly greater than the other. So RPO/LPO also fails.

So I needed a method that exploits *syntactic progress on the recursive argument*, not semantic or lexicographic progress on the whole term.

## 2. What the rules *do* do: peel one `s` at a time

Look again, this time structurally rather than semantically:

- `times(x, s(y))` strips one `s` from the second argument.
- `plus(x, s(y))` strips one `s` from the second argument.
- `plus(s(x), y)` strips one `s` from the first argument.
- The `… 0` and `… 0` rules are the base cases that close off the recursion.

In every recursive rule, exactly one constructor instance of `s` is "moved" or "removed" from one of the two function arguments, and the recursive call uses the *stripped* version. The natural measure to track is therefore the number of `s`'s in (or, equivalently, the natural-number value of) the argument being peeled — a standard Peano induction.

## 3. The catch that pushes the method toward "innermost first"

A naive "induct on the value of the recursive argument" almost works, but has a subtle obstacle: the recursive call inside, say, `plus(x, s(y)) → s(plus(x, y))` is `plus(x, y)`, and `x` or `y` might itself contain reducible subterms. If we induct on the value of `s(y)` *as it appears in the original term*, we don't immediately know that the *reduced* version of `x` is still a natural number of the same value, or that the inner reductions terminate.

This is exactly the situation **innermost rewriting** is designed to handle: by restricting to redexes whose proper subterms are all in normal form, we are guaranteed that when we descend into `plus(x, s(y))` with `x, s(y)` both in normal form, they really are natural numbers, and their values are exactly what the induction assumes.

The cost is that innermost termination only gives you termination *via innermost sequences*. To bridge that gap to arbitrary sequences, you need confluence — and that's where orthogonality comes in.

## 4. Why orthogonality is free here

The rules are all left-linear, and the only critical pair is `plus(0,0)`, which both `plus(x,0)` and `plus(0,x)` reduce to `0`. So the system is **orthogonal** (non-ambiguous + left-linear), and an orthogonal TRS is confluent by the classical Huet/Lankford result. That is a standard meta-theorem about TRSs, not an "added axiom" or a hidden assumption about the objects; it's a derived fact about *the rules you wrote down*.

Combined with the standard fact that, for an orthogonal system, every term has a *needed* (in particular, an innermost) reduction to its normal form, innermost termination lifts to full termination. Now the proof is complete.

## 5. Alternatives I considered and why I didn't pick them

- **Size-Change Termination (SCT)**: This would work — and indeed the argument I gave is morally an SCT argument. But for a 6-rule ground system, appealing to SCT machinery is overkill; you can just write the well-founded induction directly, and the result is more transparent.
- **Dependency pairs (Arts & Giesl)**: Would also work, and is essentially the automated analogue of what I did by hand. Same reason not to bother here: small system, transparent induction.
- **A "structural" recursive path order with a hand-picked precedence**: I tried to construct one mentally and the `s`-migration on `plus` keeps blocking it. It would only work with auxiliary notions (status, argument filters) that essentially re-introduce the induction I'm already doing.
- **A quasi-model or non-standard well-founded order on terms**: Possible, but it would be an ad-hoc construction when the rules already give you a perfectly clean induction.

## 6. Bottom line

I picked the method because **the rules are doing Peano recursion on the value of one argument, and the standard proof of termination of Peano recursion is, literally, an induction on that value**. The only non-trivial bookkeeping is (a) using innermost rewriting so the induction's value-preservation assumption is honest, and (b) invoking orthogonality so that "innermost-termination" upgrades to "termination". Both (a) and (b) are standard results about orthogonal TRSs that follow from the rules themselves, not extra axioms or extensions.
