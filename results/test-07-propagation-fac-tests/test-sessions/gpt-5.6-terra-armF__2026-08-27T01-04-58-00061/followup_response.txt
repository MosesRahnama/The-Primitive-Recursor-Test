I picked LPO because the rules have a shape that it handles directly:

- Recursive calls occur on structurally smaller arguments:
  - `times(x, s(y))` calls `times(x, y)`;
  - `plus(x, s(y))` calls `plus(x, y)`;
  - `plus(s(x), y)` calls `plus(x, y)`.
- The only apparent complication is that recursive calls may be placed under another symbol, such as
  `times(x, s(y)) -> plus(times(x, y), x)`.
- A precedence placing `times` above `plus`, and `plus` above `s`, lets LPO compare those enclosing symbols without needing to interpret them arithmetically.

Thus the choice

\[
\mathsf{times} \succ \mathsf{plus} \succ \mathsf{s} \succ \mathsf{0}
\]

matches the dependency direction visible in the rules:

- `times` rewrites to an expression rooted by `plus`;
- `plus` rewrites to an expression rooted by `s`;
- `s` itself has no rewrite rules.

Lexicographic status is appropriate because each recursive call decreases one argument while retaining earlier arguments:

\[
\mathsf{times}(x,\mathsf{s}(y)) > \mathsf{times}(x,y),
\]

\[
\mathsf{plus}(x,\mathsf{s}(y)) > \mathsf{plus}(x,y),
\]

\[
\mathsf{plus}(\mathsf{s}(x),y) > \mathsf{plus}(x,y).
\]

LPO was therefore a compact purely syntactic proof technique: it uses only term structure, the listed function symbols, and a chosen well-founded symbol precedence. It does not rely on interpreting `0`, `s`, `plus`, or `times` as natural-number operations.

A polynomial interpretation or recursive-path ordering could also establish termination, but LPO is especially natural here because the rules are standard primitive-recursion-shaped rules and their root-symbol dependencies are acyclic:

\[
\mathsf{times} \to \mathsf{plus} \to \mathsf{s}.
\]
