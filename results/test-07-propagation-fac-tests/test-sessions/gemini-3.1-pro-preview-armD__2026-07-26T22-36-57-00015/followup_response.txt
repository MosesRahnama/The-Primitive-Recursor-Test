I chose the **Lexicographic Path Ordering (LPO)** method for four specific reasons, which perfectly align with both the mathematical structure of your rules and the strict constraints of your prompt.

Here is the breakdown of why LPO was the ideal tool for this specific system:

**1. Strict Adherence to Your Constraints (No External Assumptions)**
Your prompt explicitly forbade "imported object-level axioms," "hidden assumptions," or relying on anything outside the presented syntax. 
* Many termination proofs use *semantic* methods, like **Polynomial Interpretations**, which map function symbols to natural numbers (e.g., mapping `plus(x,y)` to $x + y$). However, doing so requires importing the axioms of standard arithmetic over $\mathbb{N}$ to prove the inequalities hold. While standard, this brushes against your constraint against importing external mathematical structures.
* **LPO is purely syntactic.** It relies on absolutely nothing outside the symbols written in the prompt. It operates solely on the shape of the terms and a simple hierarchical precedence of the symbols (`times > plus > p > s > 0`). It proves termination intrinsically, honoring the strictest reading of your constraints.

**2. Perfect Fit for Primitive Recursion**
The rules you provided are classic examples of structural recursion:
* `plus` is defined by peeling an `s` off its second argument.
* `times` is defined by peeling an `s` off its first argument.
* `p` is defined by peeling an `s` off its argument.
LPO was historically developed (by Nachum Dershowitz and independently by Kamin & Lévy) specifically to handle primitive recursive schemas. The core engine of LPO—the subterm property—naturally captures the idea that a function evaluating a smaller piece of its original input is guaranteed to terminate.

**3. Why *Lexicographic* rather than *Multiset* Path Ordering?**
There are two main flavors of path orderings: Lexicographic (LPO) and Multiset (MPO). I chose Lexicographic because of how the variables are handled in your rules.
* In `plus(x, s(y)) -> s(plus(x, y))`, the first argument remains `x`, and the second argument strictly decreases from `s(y)` to `y`. 
* In `times(s(x), y) -> plus(times(x, y), y)`, the first argument strictly decreases from `s(x)` to `x`, and the second argument remains `y`.
A left-to-right lexicographic comparison elegantly handles both of these cases. When it compares the arguments of `plus` or `times`, it checks the first position, finds equality or a decrease, and if equal, moves to the second position to find the strict decrease. It solves the equations with zero friction.

**4. Simplicity and Exhaustiveness**
LPO provides a mechanical, step-by-step checklist (the three conditions I outlined in the previous response). Because it doesn't require "clever" guessing (other than figuring out the precedence hierarchy, which was trivially `times > plus > p > s > 0` based on which functions call which), it allowed me to provide a completely transparent proof where every single rule could be addressed uniformly without hand-waving.
