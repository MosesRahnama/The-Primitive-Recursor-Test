I chose **Lexicographic Path Ordering (LPO)** for several specific reasons, rooted both in the structural properties of your term rewriting system (TRS) and the strict constraints of your prompt. 

Here is why LPO is the most appropriate and robust tool for this system:

### 1. Strictly Syntactic (No External "Imports")
The prompt strictly forbade importing external structural axioms or object-level assumptions. 
* **Alternative methods** like *Polynomial Interpretations* require mapping terms to mathematical domains (usually the natural numbers $\mathbb{N}$) and proving inequalities using the properties of addition and multiplication over those domains. This introduces external mathematical theories.
* **LPO is purely syntactic.** It works entirely within the syntax of the terms themselves. It only requires defining a strict partial order (precedence) on the finite set of your system's function symbols ($\text{times}, \text{plus}, p, s, 0$). Once this finite precedence is set, the entire proof is verified by syntactic, structural decomposition of the terms.

### 2. Natural Fit for Hierarchical Definitions
Your rewrite system is organized in a clear hierarchy, where "complex" operations are defined using "simpler" operations:
* $\text{times}$ is defined in terms of $\text{plus}$ and $s$.
* $\text{plus}$ is defined in terms of $s$.
* $p$ is defined in terms of $s$.
* $s$ is the constructor.

LPO is designed specifically for such hierarchical systems. By setting the precedence of the function symbols to mirror this hierarchy ($\text{times} > \text{plus} > s > 0$ and $p > s > 0$), LPO allows a symbol of higher precedence (like $\text{times}$) to introduce symbols of lower precedence (like $\text{plus}$ and $s$) on the right-hand side, provided the arguments to the recursive calls are structurally smaller.

### 3. Elegant Handling of Left-to-Right Recursion
The rule:
$$\text{plus}(x, s(y)) \to s(\text{plus}(x, y))$$
shifts the constructor $s$ from the inside to the outside. LPO handles this effortlessly because $\text{plus} > s$. 

For the rule:
$$\text{times}(s(x), y) \to \text{plus}(\text{times}(x, y), y)$$
the root symbol on the left ($\text{times}$) has higher precedence than the root on the right ($\text{plus}$). LPO splits this into proving that $\text{times}(s(x), y)$ is strictly greater than both arguments on the right: $\text{times}(x, y)$ and $y$. 
* $y$ is a subterm (trivial).
* $\text{times}(x, y)$ has the same root symbol as the left-hand side, so LPO compares their arguments lexicographically from left-to-right. Since $s(x) > x$ (by subterm), the left side is strictly greater.

### 4. Simple Decidability
Because LPO is a well-founded simplification ordering, showing that $l >_{lpo} r$ for all rules in a finite TRS is a completely decidable and automated process. It removes any need for "human ingenuity" or auxiliary lemmas, making the proof self-contained and undeniably correct within the boundaries of the rules provided.
