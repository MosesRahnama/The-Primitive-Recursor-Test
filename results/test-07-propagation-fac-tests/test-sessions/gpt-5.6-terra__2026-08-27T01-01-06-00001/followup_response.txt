I picked a **decomposition/stratification argument** because the rules naturally divide into two parts with different termination mechanisms:

1. `plus`, `times`, and `p` are ordinary first-order recursive definitions whose recursive calls visibly descend on constructor structure.
2. `fac` is the only rule that introduces a recursive occurrence of its own symbol, and that recursive occurrence is mediated by `p`.

For the first part, a standard reduction ordering such as recursive path ordering is a natural tool: it checks all the `plus`, `times`, and `p` rules uniformly, including the duplicating-looking `times` rule, without needing arithmetic interpretations of the symbols.

For the second part, trying to orient every rule in one simple global ordering is less transparent. In particular, the `fac` rule contains

```text
fac(s(x)) -> times(fac(p(s(x))), s(x)).
```

The recursive argument is not syntactically a direct subterm of `s(x)`; it is transformed by `p`. So the relevant observation is operational: another `fac` step can occur only if `p(s(x))` reduces to an `s`-headed term, and the displayed `p` rules permit that only by consuming successor structure. If no such structure remains—or if the argument is some arbitrary nonmatching term—then the recursive `fac` call is stuck rather than continuing.

So the method was chosen to reflect the actual rule structure:

- use a conventional termination ordering where it is clean and routine;
- isolate the one self-recursive symbol;
- analyze its recursive-call chain directly from the two `p` rules.

That approach was intended to stay within the requested boundary: it treats `p` only according to its rewrite rules, not as an assumed mathematical predecessor function, and it does not assume all terms are natural-number encodings.
