I chose the **Dependency Pair (DP) framework combined with a polynomial interpretation** because standard, simpler termination methods fail on this specific rewrite system due to the structure of the `fac` (factorial) rule. 

Here is the detailed breakdown of why this method was necessary:

### 1. The Core Obstacle: Non-Subterm Recursion
Look closely at the recursive call in the factorial rule:
`fac(s(x)) -> times(fac(p(s(x))), s(x))`

For a function to be proven terminating using standard **simplification orderings** (like Lexicographic Path Ordering (LPO), Recursive Path Ordering (RPO), or Knuth-Bendix Ordering (KBO)), the argument of the recursive call must be a *strict subterm* of the original argument. 
* The original argument is `s(x)`.
* The recursive argument is `p(s(x))`.

Syntactically, `p(s(x))` is **not a subterm** of `s(x)`; in fact, it contains more symbols. Because the subterm property is violated, all standard simplification orderings will blindly fail to prove that `fac` terminates. 

### 2. Why Dependency Pairs?
The Dependency Pair framework (developed by Arts and Giesl) is the gold standard for automated termination analysis precisely because it bypasses the limitations of simplification orderings. 

Instead of trying to order the entire left-hand side against the entire right-hand side, DPs isolate *only the recursive calls*. It extracts the "dependency pair":
`FAC(s(x)) -> FAC(p(s(x)))`

This shifts the problem from "prove the whole rule decreases" to "prove the arguments of the recursive calls decrease along cycles in the call graph."

### 3. Why Polynomial Interpretations?
Once we have the dependency pair `FAC(s(x)) -> FAC(p(s(x)))`, we still need a mathematical ordering to prove that `p(s(x))` is "smaller" than `s(x)`. 

Because `p` (predecessor) and `s` (successor) are semantic inverses of one another, we need an ordering that can perform arithmetic cancellation. **Polynomial interpretations** are perfectly suited for this:
* By interpreting `s` as adding 1: `[s(x)] = x + 1`
* By interpreting `p` as subtracting 1: `[p(x)] = x - 1`

The interpretation naturally evaluates the arguments:
* Left side argument: `[s(x)] = x + 1`
* Right side argument: `[p(s(x))] = (x + 1) - 1 = x`

Since `x + 1 > x` for all natural numbers, the polynomial interpretation easily orients the dependency pair strictly downward, proving termination.

### Summary
I picked this method because it is the exact theoretical tool designed to solve the "predecessor inside a recursive call" problem. Simpler structural methods choke on `p(s(x))` not being a subterm of `s(x)`, whereas Dependency Pairs isolate the recursion and Polynomial Interpretations provide the arithmetic semantics needed to prove the argument is shrinking.
