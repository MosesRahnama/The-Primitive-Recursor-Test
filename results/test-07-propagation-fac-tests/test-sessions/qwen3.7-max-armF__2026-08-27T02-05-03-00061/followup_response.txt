I chose **Recursive Path Ordering (RPO) with multiset argument status** because it is the most conceptually natural, robust, and standard method for handling the specific structural symmetries present in this term rewriting system (TRS). 

Here is the detailed breakdown of why this specific method and configuration were chosen over alternatives:

### 1. The Symmetry of the `plus` Rules (Why Multiset Status?)
The most critical feature of this TRS is how addition is defined:
* `plus(x, s(y)) -> s(plus(x, y))`
* `plus(s(x), y) -> s(plus(x, y))`

These rules treat the arguments of `plus` symmetrically. The function simply looks for an `s(...)` constructor on *either* the left or the right, peels it off, and moves it to the outside. 

If I had used **lexicographic status** (often called Lexicographic Path Ordering, or LPO), I would have to assign a fixed left-to-right or right-to-left priority to the arguments of `plus`. 
* *Technically*, left-to-right lexicographic status *does* work for this exact TRS. In the first rule, the first arguments are syntactically identical variables (`x` and `x`), which triggers a tie-breaker that successfully compares the second arguments (`s(y) > y`). 
* *However*, relying on this is fragile. It only works because the "untouched" argument is a bare variable. If the system were slightly extended (e.g., `plus(f(x), s(y)) -> s(plus(f(x), y))` vs `plus(s(x), f(y)) -> s(plus(x, f(y)))`), lexicographic ordering would fail because the untouched arguments wouldn't be syntactically identical, breaking the tie-breaker. 

**Multiset status** ignores argument positions entirely and just compares the "pool" of arguments. Since $\{x, s(y)\} \succ_{mul} \{x, y\}$ and $\{s(x), y\} \succ_{mul} \{x, y\}$, multiset status perfectly captures the semantic intent of the rules without relying on syntactic quirks.

### 2. The Definition Hierarchy (Why the Precedence `times > plus > s`?)
The precedence relation is directly dictated by the primitive recursive structure of the rules:
* **`times > plus`**: The `times` function is defined by making recursive calls to itself and calling `plus` (`times(x, s(y)) -> plus(times(x, y), x)`). To orient this, the head symbol of the LHS (`times`) must be strictly greater than the head symbol of the RHS (`plus`).
* **`plus > s`**: The `plus` function is defined by making recursive calls to itself and wrapping them in the successor function `s` (`plus(...) -> s(plus(...))`). Therefore, `plus` must be strictly greater than `s`.
* **`s > 0`**: Constants are typically placed at the bottom of the precedence hierarchy.

This yields the strict, linear precedence `times > plus > s > 0`, which is the textbook standard for Peano arithmetic TRSs.

### 3. Simplicity and Standard Practice (Why not other methods?)
When assessing termination, one could theoretically use heavier machinery:
* **Polynomial Interpretations:** I could map `s(x)` to $x+1$, `plus(x,y)` to $x+y$, and `times(x,y)$ to $x \cdot y + x + y$ (or similar). While this would prove termination, finding the exact polynomial coefficients by hand is tedious, and it is massive overkill for a standard primitive recursive system.
* **Matrix Interpretations / Dependency Pairs:** These are highly powerful techniques used by automated provers for complex, non-simply-terminating systems. However, applying them here would obscure the simple structural recursion that is plainly visible in the rules.

### Summary
I picked **RPO with multiset status** because it is the lightest, most standard tool that perfectly aligns with the semantics of the system. The multiset status elegantly handles the commutative-like argument permutation in `plus`, and the precedence hierarchy naturally mirrors the primitive recursive definitions of multiplication and addition.
