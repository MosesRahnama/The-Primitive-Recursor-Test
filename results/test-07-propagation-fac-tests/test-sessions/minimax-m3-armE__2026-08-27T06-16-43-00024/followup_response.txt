# Why Polynomial Interpretation?

I had several reasonable options here. Here's why I landed on polynomial interpretation, and what I considered.

## Alternatives I Could Have Used

**1. A direct structural/inductive argument.** Rule (2) calls `F(x, y, n)`, where the third argument of the outer call was `S(n)`. Since `n` is structurally smaller than `S(n)`, the third argument of `F` strictly decreases on each recursive call, and rule (1) provides the base case. This is arguably the most natural argument.

**2. Recursive/lexicographic path ordering.** Set a precedence like `F > G` and use a status that compares `F`'s third argument in a multiset/lexicographic fashion. This would work but requires more bookkeeping (precedence choice, status, subterm relationships).

**3. Dependency pairs + reduction pairs.** A more heavy-weight framework, overkill for two rules.

**4. Polynomial interpretation.** What I used.

## Why I Picked It

A few reasons specific to this problem:

- **It's a "just compute" proof.** No structural reasoning about subterms, no precedence guessing, no status. You write down numbers, evaluate, compare, done. The reader can verify each step in seconds.
- **The coefficients are tiny and the arithmetic is obviously correct.** `2·0 + 1 = 1` and `2·(φ(n)+1)+1 = 2·φ(n)+3` vs. `0 + 2·φ(n)+1` — there is essentially nothing to misread.
- **It packages the "third argument decreases" intuition in a single scalar.** The interpretation `φ(F(t₁,t₂,t₃)) = 2·φ(t₃) + 1` literally says "the weight of an `F`-term is a function of its third argument only." The `+1` in front and the `+1` after `S` are exactly what is needed to make rule (2) decrease, mirroring the structural intuition.
- **No auxiliary lemmas or external facts are invoked.** I never claimed "`S(n)` is bigger than `n`" or "subterms are smaller than superterms" — I just assigned numbers and compared. The well-foundedness of `>` on ℕ is the only thing the argument leans on, and that is a standard fact of the meta-theory, not an object-level axiom.
- **It scales uniformly.** If the system grew, the same technique would still apply (perhaps with different polynomials), whereas an ad hoc structural argument would need to be redone.

## Trade-off

The honest downside: polynomial interpretation is somewhat over-powered here, and it doesn't explain *why* the system terminates as transparently as a structural argument does. A reader who wanted intuition would be better served by the "third argument of `F` decreases" story. The polynomial interpretation gives a *certificate* more than an *explanation*. For a system this small, both have merit, and the structural argument would have been equally valid. I chose the polynomial interpretation because it produces a self-contained, mechanically checkable proof with no meta-arguments beyond the well-foundedness of the natural numbers.
