I chose **Lexicographic Path Ordering (LPO)** for three key reasons: its **purely syntactic nature** (which perfectly respects your constraints), the **clear hierarchical structure** of your rewrite rules, and the **structural recursion** present in the arguments. 

Here is the detailed breakdown of why LPO was the most appropriate and elegant tool for this specific system:

### 1. It is Purely Syntactic (Zero External Assumptions)
Your prompt strictly prohibited importing external axioms, semantic models, or object-level assumptions. 
* Many other termination proof methods (such as **Polynomial Interpretations**) require mapping the terms to an external mathematical domain like the natural numbers ($\mathbb{N}$) or real numbers ($\mathbb{R}$). To prove termination that way, you have to assume the standard algebraic properties of addition and multiplication over those domains.
* **LPO**, by contrast, is a **purely syntactic simplification order**. It operates entirely on the symbols and tree structures of the terms themselves. Proving $l >_{lpo} r$ requires nothing more than the definition of LPO and a chosen precedence (a finite ordering on the symbols). It is entirely self-contained within the syntax of the TRS.

### 2. The Rules Have a Clear Hierarchy (Precedence)
If you look at how the operations are defined, there is a clear "definition dependency" which screams for a precedence-based ordering:
* `times` is defined using `plus` (Rule 2: `times` $\to$ `plus(...)`). This means `times` is a "higher-level" operation than `plus`.
* `plus` is defined using `s` (Rules 5 and 6: `plus` $\to$ `s(...)`). This means `plus` is higher-level than `s`.
* `s` is the successor constructor, and `0` is the base constant.

LPO captures this hierarchy perfectly via the precedence relation: $\text{times} \succ \text{plus} \succ s \succ 0$. Because LPO allows a higher-precedence symbol to "pay for" introducing lower-precedence symbols on the right-hand side, it effortlessly handles rules like:
$$\text{times}(x, s(y)) \to \text{plus}(\text{times}(x, y), x)$$
Here, the root symbol drops from `times` to `plus`, allowing LPO to easily prove the reduction.

### 3. Structural Decrease in Arguments (Lexicographic Status)
The recursive rules in your system decrease the "size" of the arguments structurally:
* In `times(x, s(y)) \to ... times(x, y) ...`, the second argument decreases from $s(y)$ to $y$.
* In `plus(x, s(y)) \to s(plus(x, y))`, the second argument decreases from $s(y)$ to $y$.
* In `plus(s(x), y) \to s(plus(x, y))`, the first argument decreases from $s(x)$ to $x$.

LPO with lexicographic status is designed precisely to track these kinds of coordinate-wise structural decreases. Since the arguments are strictly stripped of their outer constructor ($s$), the lexicographic comparison of the argument list is guaranteed to decrease, which LPO translates directly into a termination proof.

LPO is the "first-line defense" in termination analysis because it is decidable, requires no arithmetic semantics, and perfectly mirrors the structural recursion of primitive recursive arithmetic functions. For this system, it provides an absolute, watertight proof of termination using only the syntax of the rules.
